1 · The Solution ModelStart with particles and roles

1The Solution Model

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
SolutionA homogeneous mixture: composition is uniform at the scale observed, but it can vary from one prepared sample to another.Solutions can be gas, liquid, or solid: air, saltwater, brass.
SolventComponent present in the largest amount or doing the dissolving. It usually determines the phase of the solution.In aqueous solutions, water is the solvent.
SoluteComponent dispersed at the particle level in the solvent. There may be more than one solute.Salt and sugar can both be solutes in the same water sample.
Aqueous notation(aq) means dissolved in water—not liquid and not automatically molecular.NaCl(aq) represents separated hydrated Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.
Key distinctionDissolving distributes particles; it does not require the solute to disappear chemically.Sugar molecules remain sugar; soluble ionic solids dissociate into ions.
2 · Why Anything DissolvesDissolution is an attraction trade

2Why Anything Dissolves

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Three interaction changesSeparate some solute particles; open spaces among solvent particles; create solute–solvent attractions.Breaking attractions costs energy; forming new attractions releases energy.
“Hole” modelTo insert a solute particle, solvent–solvent attractions must be disrupted. Dissolution works well when new solute–solvent interactions compensate.Water’s hydrogen-bond network resists admitting nonpolar oil.
Like dissolves likePolar/ionic substances tend to dissolve in polar solvents; nonpolar substances in nonpolar solvents.This is an attraction guideline, not a claim that identical structures are required.
Prediction sequenceClassify solvent polarity → classify solute polarity/ionic character → compare possible attractions.Water + methanol: favorable hydrogen bonding; water + CCl₄: poor match.
3 · Ionic and Molecular SolutesWhat the solution actually contains

3Ionic and Molecular Solutes

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Ionic dissociationWhen a soluble ionic solid dissolves, its cations and anions separate and become surrounded by solvent molecules.CaCl₂(aq) contains Ca²⁺ and twice as many Cl⁻ particles.
Hydration orientationWater’s oxygen end is partially negative and points toward cations; hydrogen ends are partially positive and point toward anions.Ion–dipole attractions stabilize separated ions.
Polar molecular solutesPolar molecules may remain intact while mixing through dipole interactions or hydrogen bonding.Sugar dissolves as molecules; ethanol’s O–H group interacts strongly with water.
Nonpolar solutesNonpolar molecules cannot replace water–water attractions effectively, so separate phases often remain.Oil and water; CCl₄ and water.
Electrolyte connectionIonic solutions conduct because mobile ions carry charge; molecular sugar solution has particles but essentially no ions.Same molarity does not imply same electrical conductivity or particle count.
4 · Solubility, Saturation, and ConcentrationThree ideas students often mix up

4Solubility, Saturation, and Concentration

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
SolubilityMaximum amount of a solute that dissolves in a given solvent under stated conditions, especially temperature.A property/limit, commonly expressed as g solute per 100 g solvent.
UnsaturatedContains less dissolved solute than the solubility limit; more can dissolve.Can be concentrated or dilute depending on the limit.
SaturatedAt the limit: dissolved solute is in dynamic equilibrium with undissolved solute if excess solid is present.Particles still dissolve and crystallize at equal rates.
Concentrated vs diluteRelative descriptions of how much solute is present per amount of solution; they do not say whether the limit was reached.A saturated solution of a poorly soluble substance may still be dilute.
TemperatureSolubility must be tied to conditions. Many solids become more soluble as temperature rises, but the pattern is substance-specific.Do not infer concentration change without knowing whether solvent evaporated or solute amount/volume changed.
Connection: Now that “how dissolving works” is clear, concentration gives precise ways to say how much solute is present.
5 · Mass PercentPart by mass out of the whole solution

5Mass Percent

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Formulamass % = mass solute / mass solution × 100.Mass solution = mass solute + mass solvent.
Classic trapThe denominator is not mass of solvent.4.9 g glucose + 95.7 g water → solution mass 100.6 g.
Worked result4.9/100.6×100 = 4.9% by mass (appropriate sig figs).Mass units must match, but any mass unit works.
Reverse usemass solute=(mass %/100)×mass solution.Percent is divided by 100 before calculation.
6 · MolarityThe main bridge between measurable volume and chemical amount

6Molarity

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
DefinitionM = mol solute / L solution.M means mol/L, not moles, and volume is final solution volume.
Rearrangementsmol=MV; V=mol/M.Use liters with molarity unless units cancel consistently in a ratio.
Volume trap125.0 mL = 0.1250 L.1.00 mol/0.1250 L = 8.00 M.
Mass → M routegrams → divide by molar mass → moles → divide by liters of solution.Never divide grams directly by liters and call it molarity.
Interpretation2.00 M means 2.00 mol solute in every 1.00 L of that solution—not necessarily 1 L of solvent.Solution volume may not equal sum of component volumes.
7 · Comparing SolutionsUse M=n/V rather than intuition

7Comparing Solutions

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Equal volumesHigher molarity contains more solute moles because n=MV.At equal V, n is proportional to M.
Equal molesLower-molarity solution requires a larger volume because V=n/M.This explains the chapter’s HCl comparison.
Same mass, different solutesEqual grams do not mean equal moles; molar mass decides.100 g NaOH is 2.50 mol; 100 g KCl is about 1.34 mol.
Largest volume trapA larger-volume sample may contain fewer moles if its concentration is sufficiently smaller.Compute MV before comparing.
8 · Concentration of IonsFormula subscripts become particle multipliers

8Concentration of Ions

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Dissociation coefficientsFor complete dissociation, multiply formula-unit molarity by each ion’s coefficient.0.25 M CaCl₂ → [Ca²⁺]=0.25 M, [Cl⁻]=0.50 M.
Particle molarityTotal ion concentration is the sum of individual ion molarities.0.25 M CaCl₂ ideally gives 0.75 M total ions.
Total moles of ionsFirst find formula-unit moles MV, then multiply by ions per formula unit.Volume, molarity, and dissociation count all matter.
NonelectrolyteSucrose remains molecular; 0.10 M sucrose gives 0.10 M dissolved molecules, not ions.Do not count atoms inside a molecule as separate solution particles.
Connection: Concentration becomes useful experimentally only when a solution can be prepared accurately.
9 · Preparing a Standard SolutionTechnique is part of correctness

9Preparing a Standard Solution

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Standard solutionA solution whose concentration is accurately known.Requires quantitative transfer and calibrated final volume.
From solidCalculate mass → weigh → dissolve in some solvent → quantitatively transfer to volumetric flask → rinse transfers → fill to mark → mix.Do not fill flask completely before solute dissolves.
MeniscusRead bottom of a clear aqueous meniscus at eye level.Parallax causes systematic volume error.
Final-volume ruleAdd solvent until total solution reaches the mark.Do not add the flask’s labeled volume of water.
MixingStopper and invert repeatedly after bringing to volume.The neck region otherwise may have a different concentration.
10 · DilutionSame solute moles, more space

10Dilution

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Particle pictureDilution adds solvent; solute particles become farther apart, but their count stays constant.Concentration falls because V rises while n is unchanged.
EquationM₁V₁=M₂V₂, derived from nbefore=nafter.Applies when no solute is added, removed, or reacted.
Direction checkA true dilution has M₂<M₁ and V₂>V₁; required stock aliquot is smaller than final volume.If calculated V₁ exceeds final V₂, recheck setup.
ProcedurePipet stock aliquot into volumetric flask containing some water; dilute to mark; mix by inversion.For safety with strong acids: add acid to water, not water to concentrated acid.
What does not change MPouring out a well-mixed portion removes solute and solution in the same ratio, so remaining molarity is unchanged.Evaporation raises M; adding solute usually raises M; adding water lowers M.
Connection: Molarity is now a conversion factor from laboratory volume to reacting moles.
11 · Solution StoichiometryMolarity replaces the balance as the route to moles

11Solution Stoichiometry

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Core roadmapbalanced equation → mol=MV for each reactant → limiting reactant → mole ratio → requested unit.For ionic reactions, a net ionic equation often makes the actual ratio clearer.
Mixed volumeAfter mixing, concentrations of leftover species use total final solution volume if volumes are assumed additive.Convert all mL to L when calculating molarity.
Spectator ionsSpectators do not react, but their final concentration changes because total volume changes.Count their initial moles, then divide by combined volume.
Precipitate massFind limiting ion → moles solid → multiply by solid molar mass.Solubility rules identify which product is solid.
12 · Worked Precipitation SystemPhosphate + lead(II), from start to leftovers

12Worked Precipitation System

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Given10.0 mL 0.30 M Na₃PO₄ + 20.0 mL 0.20 M Pb(NO₃)₂.Initial PO₄³⁻=0.0100×0.30=0.0030 mol; Pb²⁺=0.0200×0.20=0.0040 mol.
Net ionic3Pb²⁺ + 2PO₄³⁻ → Pb₃(PO₄)₂(s).Charge and atoms balance.
Limiting test0.0030 mol PO₄³⁻ needs 0.0045 mol Pb²⁺, but only 0.0040 exists → Pb²⁺ limiting.Product=0.0040/3=0.00133 mol.
Mass solidMolar mass Pb₃(PO₄)₂ ≈ 811.5 g/mol → about 1.1 g.Keep guard digits until final rounding.
Leftover phosphateConsumed=(2/3)(0.0040)=0.00267 mol; left≈0.00033 mol; total V=0.0300 L → 0.011 M.Subtract reacted moles before dividing by total volume.
Nitrate spectatorInitial nitrate=2(0.0040)=0.0080 mol; [NO₃⁻]=0.0080/0.0300=0.27 M.Spectator remains chemically unchanged but is diluted in combined volume.
Connection: Neutralization is solution stoichiometry in which H⁺ and OH⁻ capacity matters.
13 · Neutralization and TitrationCount acidic H⁺ and basic OH⁻ equivalents

13Neutralization and Titration

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Strong acid/base net ionicH⁺(aq)+OH⁻(aq)→H₂O(l).At equivalence, reactive equivalents—not necessarily formula moles—are equal.
Stoichiometric factorH₂SO₄ can furnish 2 mol H⁺ per mol acid; Ba(OH)₂ furnishes 2 mol OH⁻ per mol base.1.00 L×0.500 M H₂SO₄=0.500 mol acid=1.00 mol H⁺, needing 1.00 mol NaOH.
TitrationA known solution reacts with an unknown until equivalence; measured volume plus stoichiometry gives unknown concentration.Do not assume 1:1 unless balanced chemistry supports it.
Endpoint vs equivalenceEquivalence is exact stoichiometric completion; endpoint is observed indicator/instrument signal chosen to approximate it.A good indicator changes near the equivalence point.
14 · Equivalents and NormalityReaction capacity per liter

14Equivalents and Normality

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
EquivalentAmount that furnishes or consumes 1 mol H⁺ (acid–base context).1 mol HCl=1 eq; 1 mol H₂SO₄=2 eq; 1 mol Ba(OH)₂=2 eq.
Equivalent weightMolar mass divided by number of reactive H⁺ or OH⁻ per formula unit.H₂SO₄: 98.08/2=49.04 g/eq for full neutralization.
NormalityN = equivalents/L. For a specified acid–base reaction, N=M×equivalents per mole.0.500 M H₂SO₄ is 1.00 N for complete neutralization.
Context warningNormality depends on the reaction role and number of equivalents transferred. Molarity is composition-only; normality is reaction-capacity based.Always state the reaction context.
