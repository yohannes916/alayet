UNIT 1 · Atomic Structure & Electron Configurations §1–§7

1 Where Electrons Live — Shells, Sublevels, Orbitals Unit 1

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Shell (energy level, n)Electrons live in numbered layers around the nucleus: n = 1, 2, 3… Bigger n = farther from the nucleus and higher energy.Shell n holds at most 2n² electrons (n=1 → 2, n=2 → 8, n=3 → 18).
Sublevel (s, p, d, f)Each shell is split into sublevels. Shell n contains exactly n sublevels: shell 1 → s; shell 2 → s, p; shell 3 → s, p, d; shell 4 → s, p, d, f.Energy order within a shell: s < p < d < f.
OrbitalA region of space inside a sublevel where an electron actually sits. Each orbital holds at most 2 electrons.s = 1 orbital (sphere) · p = 3 orbitals (dumbbells) · d = 5 · f = 7.
Sublevel capacities ⭐Capacity = (number of orbitals) × 2 electrons each.s = 2 · p = 6 · d = 10 · f = 14. Memorize this line cold — half the config questions use it.

2 The Periodic Table's Blocks — s, p, d, f Unit 1

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
The 4 blocksThe periodic table is literally a map of which sublevel is being filled. An element's block = the sublevel its highest-energy electron occupies.s-block: Groups 1–2 (+ He) · p-block: Groups 13–18 · d-block: Groups 3–12 (transition metals) · f-block: the two detached rows (lanthanides/actinides).
Group 1 & 2 ⭐Every Group 1 and Group 2 element's highest-energy electron is in an s sublevel — that's what makes them the s-block.Na ends in 3s¹; Ca ends in 4s². (He belongs to the s-block too: 1s².)
Block widthsBlock width = sublevel capacity: s-block is 2 columns wide, p-block 6, d-block 10, f-block 14.The table's shape is the 2, 6, 10, 14 rule.
Reading n from the periodFor the s and p blocks, the shell number = period number. The d block runs one shell behind (n − 1); the f block runs two behind (n − 2).Period 4 d-block elements are filling 3d (not 4d) — that's why Fe = [Ar]4s²3d⁶.

3 The Three Filling Rules — Aufbau, Pauli, Hund Unit 1

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Aufbau PrincipleElectrons fill sublevels from lowest energy upward ("aufbau" = building up). Because shells overlap, the order is NOT simply 1, 2, 3…Order: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s 4d 5p 6s 4f 5d 6p. The famous surprise: 4s fills before 3d.
Pauli Exclusion PrincipleOne orbital holds at most 2 electrons, and they must have opposite spins (drawn ↑↓). No two electrons in an atom are identical in every way.This is WHY capacities are 2, 6, 10, 14: orbitals × 2.
Hund's RuleWithin one sublevel, put one electron in each orbital first (all spinning the same way), and only then start pairing them up.The "bus-seat rule": strangers take empty seats before doubling up. N's 2p³ = ↑ ↑ ↑, not ↑↓ ↑ _.
Which rule is which ⭐Test trap: they love to describe a rule and ask its name.Order of filling → Aufbau · 2 per orbital, opposite spins → Pauli · spread out before pairing → Hund.

4 Writing Full Electron Configurations Unit 1

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
The notation2p⁴ means: shell 2, sublevel p, holding 4 electrons. A full configuration lists every occupied sublevel in Aufbau order.O (Z=8): 1s²2s²2p⁴.
The method① Find the element's electron count (= atomic number for a neutral atom). ② Fill sublevels in Aufbau order to capacity (2, 6, 10, 14) until the electrons run out.S (Z=16): 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁴ · Fe (Z=26): 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶4s²3d⁶.
The self-check ⭐The superscripts must add up to the total electron count. Always check.Fe: 2+2+6+2+6+2+6 = 26 ✓.
4s before 3dWrite (and fill) 4s before 3d: after 3p⁶ comes 4s², then 3d.Br (Z=35): 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p⁶4s²3d¹⁰4p⁵.

5 Condensed (Noble-Gas) Configurations Unit 1

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
The shortcutReplace the beginning of the configuration with the noble gas that comes BEFORE the element in brackets, then write only what's left.P (Z=15): full = 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p³ → condensed = [Ne]3s²3p³.
The noble-gas mile markers ⭐You must know their electron counts to subtract from.He = 2 · Ne = 10 · Ar = 18 · Kr = 36 · Xe = 54.
Picking the right oneUse the noble gas at the end of the previous period — never the one after the element.Ca (Z=20): [Ar]4s² · Fe: [Ar]4s²3d⁶ · I (Z=53): [Kr]5s²4d¹⁰5p⁵.
Why it worksThe bracket hides the boring, full inner shells (the core electrons); what you still write are the outer, chemistry-doing electrons.This "outer electrons" idea becomes valence electrons in §8.

6 The Two Famous Exceptions — Chromium & Copper Unit 1

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Chromium (Cr, Z=24) ⭐Aufbau predicts [Ar]4s²3d⁴ — but a half-filled d sublevel (d⁵) is extra stable, so one 4s electron moves over.Actual: [Ar]4s¹3d⁵.
Copper (Cu, Z=29) ⭐Aufbau predicts [Ar]4s²3d⁹ — but a completely filled d sublevel (d¹⁰) is extra stable, so again one 4s electron moves over.Actual: [Ar]4s¹3d¹⁰.
The whyHalf-filled (d⁵) and filled (d¹⁰) sublevels have a symmetric, low-energy electron arrangement — worth "paying" one 4s electron for.Same pattern one row down: Mo = [Kr]5s¹4d⁵, Ag = [Kr]5s¹4d¹⁰.
How it's tested"Write the configuration of Cr/Cu" → they want the actual (exception) answer, and often the reason.Answer template: "…because a half-filled/filled d sublevel is unusually stable."

7 Ion Configurations — Cations Lose s First Unit 1

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Anions (−)Gained electrons: add them, continuing the normal filling order.O²⁻: 8 + 2 = 10 e⁻ → 1s²2s²2p⁶ = [Ne].
Main-group cations (+)Lost electrons: remove from the outermost shell.Na⁺ = [Ne] · Ca²⁺ = [Ar].
Transition-metal cations ⭐⭐THE trap of Unit 1: electrons are removed from the 4s sublevel BEFORE 3d — even though 4s filled first. (Once 3d is occupied, the outermost shell — n = 4 — is the first to empty.)Fe [Ar]4s²3d⁶ → Fe²⁺ [Ar]3d⁶ (both 4s gone) → Fe³⁺ [Ar]3d⁵. NOT [Ar]4s²3d⁴!
More examplesApply "s leaves first, then d" every time.Zn²⁺ = [Ar]3d¹⁰ · Cu⁺ = [Ar]3d¹⁰ · Ti²⁺ = [Ar]3d². Bonus: Fe³⁺ is extra stable — half-filled 3d⁵.
Rhyme to remember"First in, first out — for filling. Last shell empties first — for losing."Fill: 4s then 3d. Lose: 4s then 3d. (The s goes first both times!)
UNIT 2 · Lewis Structures, Formal Charge & Resonance §8–§11
🔗 Bridge: the electrons that matter for bonding are the valence electrons — the ones OUTSIDE the noble-gas core you bracketed in §5. For main-group elements, valence e⁻ = the element's A-group number (O is group 6A → 6 valence e⁻). Lewis structures are maps of valence electrons only.

8 Lewis Structures — the N·A·S Recipe Unit 2

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Octet ruleAtoms bond to reach a noble-gas count of 8 valence electrons (H wants just 2).The whole recipe exists to hand every atom its octet.
The recipe ⭐N = electrons needed = 8 × (non-H atoms) + 2 × (H atoms).
A = electrons available = sum of valence e⁻, +1 per negative charge, −1 per positive.
S = shared = N − A → bonds = S/2 · lone pairs = (A − S)/2.		CO₂: N = 24, A = 16, S = 8 → 4 bonds (two C=O doubles), lp = 4 → 2 on each O.
Central atomLowest group number / least electronegative goes in the middle; ties → higher period. H is NEVER central (it makes only one bond).CCl₄ → C central · SO₃ → S central · H₂O → O central by default.
Placing the electronsDraw single bonds → give outer atoms octets first → leftovers go on the center → if the center is short, convert lone pairs into double/triple bonds (the S = N − A math predicts this).CO: S = 6 → triple bond C≡O, one lone pair each.
IonsAdjust A for the charge, then draw brackets with the charge outside — the charge belongs to the whole ion.NH₄⁺: A = 5 + 4 − 1 = 8 → 4 bonds, 0 lone pairs · BF₄⁻: A = 32 → 4 bonds, 3 lp per F.

9 Octet Rule Exceptions Unit 2

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Electron-deficientSome small atoms are stable with fewer than 8.H = 2 · Be = 4 · B = 6 (BF₃ boron has 3 bonds = 6 e⁻).
Odd-electron (radicals)An odd total electron count forces one electron to stay unpaired → a reactive free radical.NO (11 valence e⁻), NO₂ (17). Two radicals can pair up: 2NO₂ → N₂O₄.
Expanded octets ⭐Central atoms from Period 3 or below (P, S, Cl, Br, I, Xe…) can hold MORE than 8 — they're bigger and have empty d orbitals.PCl₅ (10 e⁻ on P) · SF₆ (12 on S) · XeF₄ (12 on Xe) · ClF₃.
Who can never expandPeriod 2 atoms (C, N, O, F) are capped at 8. Always.If a structure puts 10 electrons on carbon or nitrogen, it's wrong.

10 Formal Charge Unit 2

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
The formula ⭐FC = (valence e⁻) − (non-bonding e⁻) − (bonds)
= "electrons the atom brought" minus "electrons it owns in the structure" (all its lone-pair electrons + one per bond).		Count non-bonding as individual electrons (a lone pair = 2), bonds as lines (a double bond = 2 bonds).
Worked exampleCO₃²⁻ (one C=O, two C–O): C: 4 − 0 − 4 = 0 · double-bond O: 6 − 4 − 2 = 0 · each single-bond O: 6 − 6 − 1 = −1.Sum: 0 + 0 − 1 − 1 = −2 = the ion's charge ✓.
The sum check ⭐Formal charges must always add up to the overall charge (0 for a neutral molecule). Free error-detector — use it every time.—
What FC is forWhen several valid structures exist, formal charge picks the best one (see §11).—

11 Resonance — Major vs Minor Structures Unit 2

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
What resonance isTwo or more valid Lewis structures that differ only in where the electrons sit — the atoms never move. The real molecule is a single hybrid (an average), not a molecule flipping back and forth.O₃, NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻ — drawn with double-headed arrows ↔ between structures.
DelocalizationThe "extra" bonding electrons are spread over several atoms → all the bonds become identical, with fractional bond order.O₃: bond order 1.5 · NO₃⁻: 4/3 — experiment confirms equal bond lengths.
Equivalent structuresSame pattern, just rotated (same formal charges everywhere) → they contribute equally to the hybrid.The 3 structures of NO₃⁻ or CO₃²⁻.
Non-equivalent → major/minor ⭐Different FC patterns → rank them: the major (biggest contributor) has ① the smallest formal charges, ② no same-sign charges on adjacent atoms, ③ any negative FC on the most electronegative atom.NCO⁻: N≡C–O⁻ wins (all 0 except −1 on O). The −2-on-N structure is minor/negligible.

🔗 Bridge: VSEPR is applied to a finished Lewis structure. From Unit 2 you need: the drawn structure, the lone pairs on the central atom, and this rule: a single, double, or triple bond each counts as ONE electron domain. (Full detail lives in the Ch10 notes — this is the exam-ready core.)

12 VSEPR — Domains, Geometry, Shape Unit 3

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Electron domainOne "direction" of electron density on the central atom: any bond (single, double, or triple) = 1 domain; each lone pair = 1 domain.CO₂: 2 domains · SO₂: 3 (2 bonds + 1 lp).
Two different names ⭐Electron-domain geometry counts ALL domains. Molecular geometry (shape) names only where the atoms are. They match only when there are no lone pairs.H₂O: tetrahedral domain geometry, bent shape.
Lone-pair effectsLone pairs repel hardest → they squeeze bond angles (109.5° → 107° → 104.5°) and in 5-domain molecules they always take equatorial spots.The cascade: CH₄ 109.5° → NH₃ 107° → H₂O 104.5°.

13 Molecular Polarity Unit 3

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Bond dipoleUnequal sharing between atoms of different electronegativity → the bond has a δ+ end and a δ− end (an arrow toward the more electronegative atom).Every O–H, C–Cl, B–F bond is polar.
The 2-question test ⭐① Are there polar bonds? ② Does the 3-D shape let their dipoles cancel? Cancel → nonpolar. Don't cancel → net dipole → polar.Polar bonds do NOT guarantee a polar molecule.
Symmetric = cancelsShapes with identical outer atoms spaced evenly: linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, octahedral, and square planar.Nonpolar: CO₂, BF₃, CCl₄, XeF₄.
Asymmetric = net dipoleLone-pair shapes (bent, pyramidal, seesaw, T-shaped) or different outer atoms.Polar: H₂O, NH₃, CHCl₃, SF₄, COS (linear but O ≠ S!).
Classic pairsSame ingredients, opposite results — the shape decides.CO₂ vs H₂O · CCl₄ vs CHCl₃ · BF₃ vs NH₃.
UNIT 4 · Chemical Reactions & Stoichiometry §14–§18
🔗 Bridge: Units 1–3 built single molecules. Now molecules react: bonds break and re-form, making new substances. Balanced equations count the molecules; moles let us weigh them on a real scale.

14 The 5 Signs of a Chemical Reaction Unit 4

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
The 5 signs ⭐Evidence that new substances formed: ① color change ② gas produced (bubbles) ③ precipitate (a solid appears from two clear solutions) ④ energy change (heat or light given off — or absorbed, the flask gets cold) ⑤ odor change.Rusting (color), baking soda + vinegar (gas), AgNO₃ + NaCl (precipitate), burning (heat/light), rotten eggs (odor).
Careful!Bubbles from boiling or fizz from opening a soda are physical changes — no new substance is made. A sign only counts when a new substance appears.Melting ice ≠ reaction.

15 The 5 Reaction Types & the Activity Series Unit 4

TypePattern & how to spot itExample
SynthesisA + B → AB — two things become one.2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
DecompositionAB → A + B — one thing breaks into two or more.CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
CombustionFuel (usually CxHy) + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O, always. Spot it by the O₂ reactant.CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
Single replacementA + BC → AC + B — a lone element swaps in for one partner. Element + compound.Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂
Double replacementAB + CD → AD + CB — two compounds trade partners. Driven by forming a precipitate, gas, or water.AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃
ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Activity series ⭐For single replacement: a metal can only kick out metals BELOW it on the list. Above H → can also release H₂ from acids. If the lone metal is lower → NO REACTION.K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Ni > Sn > Pb > (H) > Cu > Ag > Au
Applying itFind both metals on the list; compare positions.Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu ✓ (Zn above Cu) · Cu + ZnSO₄ → NR · Ag + HCl → NR (Ag below H).

16 Balancing Chemical Equations Unit 4

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
The one lawAtoms are never created or destroyed (conservation of mass) — each element's count must match on both sides.—
The one rule ⭐Balance by changing COEFFICIENTS only — NEVER subscripts. Changing a subscript changes the substance itself (H₂O → H₂O₂ is a different chemical!).2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O ✓  ·  H₂ + O₂ → H₂O₂ ✗
Strategy① Balance one element at a time. ② Treat polyatomic ions (SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻…) as whole units when they survive the reaction. ③ Save H and O (or any lone element) for last. ④ A coefficient multiplies everything in its formula.C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O · 3Ca(NO₃)₂ has 6 N and 18 O.

17 Molar Mass & Hydrates Unit 4

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Molar massGrams of one mole (6.022 × 10²³ particles). Compute: each atom's atomic mass × its subscript, then add.H₂O = 2(1.01) + 16.00 = 18.02 g/mol · NaCl = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44.
ParenthesesA subscript after parentheses multiplies everything inside.Ca(NO₃)₂ = 40.08 + 2(14.01 + 48.00) = 164.10 g/mol.
Hydrates ⭐The dot in CuSO₄·5H₂O means "plus 5 water molecules" — you ADD 5 × 18.02. (Trap: the dot is NOT multiplication.)CuSO₄·5H₂O = 63.55 + 32.07 + 4(16.00) + 5(18.02) = 249.7 g/mol.
Percent water% H₂O = (mass of water part ÷ total) × 100.90.10 / 249.7 ≈ 36.1 % of the hydrate's mass is water.

18 Stoichiometry — the Mole Road Map Unit 4

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
The road map ⭐mass A → (÷ molar mass) → mol A → (× mole ratio) → mol B → (× molar mass) → mass B. You can NEVER jump straight from grams of A to grams of B.The mole ratio comes from the balanced equation's coefficients.
Mole ratioCoefficients tell how molecules trade: 2AgNO₃ + CaCl₂ → 2AgCl + Ca(NO₃)₂ means 2 mol AgNO₃ : 2 mol AgCl (i.e. 1:1).—
Worked exampleHow many grams of AgCl precipitate from 8.50 g AgNO₃ with excess NaCl? (AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl + NaNO₃)8.50 ÷ 169.9 = 0.0500 mol AgNO₃ → ×(1/1) → 0.0500 mol AgCl → × 143.3 = 7.17 g AgCl.
Sanity checks① Divide by molar mass going TO moles, multiply coming FROM moles. ② Answer should be same order of magnitude as the given mass (unless the molar masses are very different).—
UNIT 5 · Solubility, Net Ionic Equations, Acids & Bases §19–§21
🔗 Bridge: double-replacement reactions (§15) usually happen dissolved in water, where soluble ionic compounds float around as separate ions. Solubility rules predict which product crashes out as a solid — and the net ionic equation strips away everything that just watched.

19 Solubility Rules — Predicting Precipitates Unit 5

RuleWhat to understandKey facts / exceptions
Always soluble ⭐Compounds of Group 1 metals (Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺…), NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻, acetate C₂H₃O₂⁻, and ClO₄⁻ dissolve — no exceptions.If either ion is Na⁺/K⁺/NH₄⁺/NO₃⁻ → mark it (aq), done.
Halides (Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻)Soluble……EXCEPT with Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺ (AgCl, PbI₂ are precipitates).
Sulfates (SO₄²⁻)Soluble……EXCEPT with Ba²⁺, Pb²⁺, Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, Hg₂²⁺ (BaSO₄ ↓).
Mostly INsolubleHydroxides (OH⁻), carbonates (CO₃²⁻), phosphates (PO₄³⁻), sulfides (S²⁻) are insoluble……EXCEPT with Group 1 / NH₄⁺. (Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂ are slightly soluble — and are the strong bases of §21.)
Using the rulesSwap partners (double replacement), then check each product: any insoluble combination → write it (s) — that's the precipitate. Both soluble → no reaction.Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2KI → PbI₂(s) + 2KNO₃ · NaCl + KNO₃ → NR.

20 Molecular → Complete Ionic → Net Ionic Unit 5

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Molecular equationEverything written as full compounds with states.AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO₃(aq)
Complete ionic ⭐Split ONLY the strong electrolytes — things (aq) that fully break into ions: soluble ionic compounds, the 7 strong acids, strong bases. Keep intact: (s), (l), (g), water, weak acids/bases.Ag⁺ + NO₃⁻ + Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl(s) + Na⁺ + NO₃⁻
Spectator ionsIons that appear identical on both sides — they just floated and watched. Cancel them.Here: Na⁺ and NO₃⁻.
Net ionicWhat's left = the actual chemistry.Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s)
Balance checkA correct net ionic balances atoms AND total charge.+1 + (−1) = 0 on the left; 0 on the right ✓.

21 Strong & Weak Acids and Bases Unit 5

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
The 7 strong acids ⭐⭐Fully ionize in water (≈100%) → strong electrolytes → split them in ionic equations. Memorize the list — everything else is weak.HCl · HBr · HI · HNO₃ · H₂SO₄ · HClO₄ · HClO₃
Strong basesGroup 1 hydroxides (LiOH, NaOH, KOH…) + the heavy Group 2 hydroxides: Ca(OH)₂, Sr(OH)₂, Ba(OH)₂.Also split in ionic equations.
Weak acids & basesIonize only a little → mostly stay as whole molecules → NEVER split them. Trap: HF is weak (despite HCl/HBr/HI being strong); H₂CO₃, CH₃COOH (acetic) weak; NH₃ is the classic weak base."Not on the strong list → weak" is the whole test.
Strong + strong net ionic ⭐Every strong-acid + strong-base neutralization boils down to the same net ionic.H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) (HCl + NaOH, HNO₃ + KOH… all identical).
Weak acid + strong baseThe weak acid stays intact on the left.CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O — the acid appears as a whole molecule.

★ The Four Recipes to Anchor It All

① ELECTRON CONFIGURATION electron count → fill 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p 5s… to capacity (2·6·10·14) condensed: [previous noble gas] + rest exceptions: Cr 4s¹3d⁵ · Cu 4s¹3d¹⁰ cation? remove 4s BEFORE 3d (Fe²⁺ = [Ar]3d⁶) ② LEWIS → SHAPE → POLARITY N − A = S → bonds = S/2, lone pairs = (A−S)/2 → structure count domains (any bond = 1) → domain geometry → subtract lone pairs → shape + angle polar bonds? do dipoles cancel by symmetry? cancel → nonpolar (CO₂, BF₃, CCl₄, XeF₄) ③ REACTION → NET IONIC classify type → single replacement? check activity series (below = NR) double replacement? swap partners → solubility rules → mark the (s) split ONLY strong electrolytes → cancel spectators → net ionic (atoms + charge balance) ④ STOICHIOMETRY ROAD MAP mass A ÷ molar mass → mol A → × coefficient ratio → mol B → × molar mass → mass B hydrate molar mass: ADD n × 18.02 for ·nH₂O (CuSO₄·5H₂O = 249.7)

⭐ Highest-Yield — Drill These First

  1. 2 · 6 · 10 · 14 — sublevel capacities (s, p, d, f) and orbital counts (1, 3, 5, 7 × 2 each). The blocks of the periodic table have exactly these widths; Groups 1–2 end in s.
  2. The three rules by name — Aufbau = lowest first (4s before 3d!) · Pauli = 2 per orbital, opposite spins · Hund = spread out before pairing.
  3. Cr = [Ar]4s¹3d⁵ and Cu = [Ar]4s¹3d¹⁰ — half-filled/filled d stability. And cations lose 4s before 3d: Fe²⁺ = [Ar]3d⁶, Fe³⁺ = [Ar]3d⁵.
  4. N·A·S recipe — N = 8/atom (2 for H), A = valence ± charge, S = N − A, bonds = S/2. Expanded octets only Period 3+ (P, S, Cl, Xe); Period 2 never exceeds 8.
  5. FC = valence − nonbonding − bonds; FCs sum to the charge; major resonance structure = smallest FCs with − on the most electronegative atom.
  6. The shape table (§12) — domains → geometry → shape → angle: 180 / 120 / <120 / 109.5 / 107 / 104.5 / TBP / 90. Any bond = ONE domain; XeF₄ = square planar, not tetrahedral.
  7. Polar bonds ≠ polar molecule — 2-question test. Nonpolar: CO₂, BF₃, CCl₄, XeF₄. Polar: H₂O, NH₃, CHCl₃, COS.
  8. 5 signs · 5 types · activity series — a metal only replaces metals below it (Cu + ZnSO₄ → NR).
  9. Hydrate dot = ADD water — CuSO₄·5H₂O = 249.7 g/mol. Road map: mass → mol → mol → mass; never grams-to-grams directly.
  10. The 7 strong acids (HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄, HClO₃) + strong bases (Group 1 + Ca/Sr/Ba hydroxides). Split ONLY strong electrolytes; weak acids (HF! CH₃COOH) stay whole. Strong+strong net ionic is always H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.
