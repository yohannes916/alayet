1 What Matter Is Made Of §4.1, 4.3

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
MatterAnything with mass and volume. The umbrella for everything; splits into pure substances and mixtures.Top of the hierarchy.
MixtureTwo+ substances physically combined, NOT chemically bonded, in variable proportions. Separable by physical means (filtering, distillation, evaporation).Salt water, air, trail mix. Most natural materials are mixtures.
Pure substanceFixed, uniform composition throughout. It is either an element or a compound.Distilled water, pure copper.
ElementPure substance made of only ONE type of atom; cannot be broken down chemically.118 known; 88 natural, rest lab-made. Just 9 elements = >98% of Earth's crust + oceans + atmosphere. (O, C, N are the basis of all biological molecules — living matter ≠ crust.)
CompoundPure substance of 2+ different elements chemically bonded in a fixed mass ratio. Has new properties, unlike the elements it's made of.H₂O, NaCl, CO₂. H (explosive) + O (feeds fire) → water (douses fire).
AtomThe smallest unit that still has an element's identity. Built from protons, neutrons, electrons.The "brick." (Detailed in §5–6.)
MoleculeTwo+ atoms bonded together. The trap: a molecule can be an element (same atoms) OR a compound (different atoms).O₂ = molecule + element; H₂O = molecule + compound. Not all molecules are compounds.
Diatomic moleculeA molecule of exactly 2 atoms. Seven elements occur naturally only in this form.H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂. They make a "7" shape on the periodic table + H.
AllotropeDifferent structural forms of the same element (same atoms, different arrangement/properties).Carbon → diamond, graphite, buckminsterfullerene (C₆₀).
Uses of "element"The word is context-dependent — read each case.A single atom (Ar), a natural molecular form (H₂), or atoms bound in something (Na in the body).

2 Symbols & Names of Elements §4.2

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Symbol ruleEach element has a unique 1–2 letter symbol. First letter ALWAYS capital, second ALWAYS lowercase. Usually the first letter(s) of the name.O (oxygen), Kr (krypton). Watch: "CO" = carbon + oxygen (2 elements) vs "Co" = cobalt (1 element).
Latin / Greek symbolsSome symbols come from the old (Latin/Greek) name, not English — must be memorized.Au=aurum (gold), Pb=plumbum (lead), Na=natrium (sodium), K=kalium (potassium), Fe=ferrum (iron), Ag=argentum (silver), Sn=stannum (tin), Sb=stibium (antimony), Hg=hydrargyrum (mercury), Cu=cuprum (copper), W=wolfram (tungsten).
Common symbols to knowMemorize the everyday elements so formulas read instantly.H, C, N, O, Na, Mg, Al, Si, P, S, Cl, K, Ca, Fe, Cu, Zn, Ag, Sn, I, Au, Hg, Pb.

3 Dalton's Atomic Theory & the Laws Behind It §4.3

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Law of Constant (Definite) CompositionA given compound always has the same proportions by mass of its elements, no matter its source.Water: always 8 g O per 1 g H. CO₂: always 2.7 g O per 1 g C.
Dalton's Atomic Theory (5 points)(1) Elements are made of tiny atoms. (2) All atoms of a given element are identical. (3) Atoms of different elements differ. (4) Atoms combine in fixed whole-number ratios to form compounds. (5) Atoms are indivisible — not created or destroyed in reactions; a reaction just rearranges them.Explains constant composition + conservation of mass.
What Dalton got WRONG (modern update)Common exam question. Wrong today: atoms ARE divisible (protons/neutrons/electrons); atoms of one element are NOT all identical (isotopes); atoms can change in nuclear (not chemical) reactions. Still true: atoms exist, different elements = different atoms, fixed ratios in compounds.Point (2) fails because of isotopes; point (5) fails only for nuclear reactions.

4 Chemical Formulas §4.4

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Chemical formulaShows the types of atoms and the number of each in one unit/molecule of a compound.H₂O = 2 H + 1 O.
Rules for writing formulasEach atom = its element symbol; the count is a subscript to the right; a subscript of "1" is never written.DDT = C₁₄H₉Cl₅ → 14 C, 9 H, 5 Cl.

5 How We Discovered the Atom's Structure (History) §4.5

Scientist / modelWhat to understandKey facts / result
Thomson — Plum Pudding (also discovered the electron)Atom = a uniform "pudding" of positive charge with negative electrons scattered throughout to balance it.Later shown wrong — but it introduced electrons.
Rutherford (1911) — Gold Foil experimentFired alpha particles at thin metal foil. Most passed straight through, but a few bounced back sharply → the atom has a tiny, dense, positive NUCLEUS; electrons are far outside; the atom is mostly empty space. Disproved plum pudding.Discovered the nucleus and the proton (+ charge = electron's − charge).
Chadwick (1932) — the NeutronMost nuclei also contain a neutron: a neutral particle, slightly more massive than a proton.Completes the 3 subatomic particles.

6 The Modern Atom — Subatomic Particles §4.6

ParticleCharge / mass / locationWhy it matters
ProtonCharge +1; mass ≈ 1 amu (1.0073); in the nucleus.# of protons = the element's identity.
NeutronCharge 0; mass ≈ 1 amu (1.0087, ~same as proton); in the nucleus.Adds mass, not charge; its count varies between isotopes.
ElectronCharge −1; mass ≈ 1/1836 amu (~0.0005, negligible); outside the nucleus.Determines chemical behavior (see below).
NucleusTiny, dense core holding protons + neutrons; contains nearly all the mass.If the atom were a stadium, the nucleus is a pea at center — atom is mostly empty space.
Why electrons drive chemistryAn atom's chemistry comes from its electrons — they're what "intermingle" when atoms bond.This is why isotopes behave chemically the same (same electrons).
Neutral atomIn a neutral atom, # protons = # electrons (charges cancel).Change electrons → ion; change neutrons → isotope; change protons → different element.

7 Counting Inside the Atom — Isotopes §4.7

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
NotationWritten ᴬZX — mass number on top, atomic number on bottom, element symbol X.¹⁴₆C, ¹²₆C.
Atomic number (Z)= number of PROTONS. Defines which element it is. (In a neutral atom, also = # electrons.)Z(C) = 6.
Mass number (A)= number of PROTONS + NEUTRONS.A(¹⁴C) = 14.
Finding neutronsneutrons = A − Z (mass number − atomic number).¹⁴C: 14 − 6 = 8 neutrons. ¹²C: 6 neutrons.
IsotopeAtoms of the same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A). Nearly identical chemistry. Elements occur naturally as mixtures of isotopes.¹²C (6 n) vs ¹⁴C (8 n) — both carbon.
Worked exampleGiven protons & neutrons → find A and identify element.23 p + 28 n → A = 51; Z = 23 → Vanadium (V).

8 Organizing the Elements — Periodic Table §4.8

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
OrderingAll known elements listed by increasing atomic number (Z).—
Group / FamilyVertical column. Elements share similar chemical properties (same # of outer/valence electrons).Predicts ion charge (§10).
PeriodHorizontal row.—
MetalsLeft + middle (most elements). Conduct heat & electricity; malleable (hammer into sheets); ductile (draw into wire); lustrous. Tend to LOSE electrons → cations.—
NonmetalsRight side. Lack metallic properties; vary widely; solid/liquid/gas at room temp. Tend to GAIN electrons → anions.—
MetalloidsAlong the metal/nonmetal "staircase"; a mix of metallic + nonmetallic properties.B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te.
Key named groupsLearn these — they predict ion charges.1A alkali metals · 2A alkaline earth metals · 7A halogens · 8A noble gases · middle block = transition metals.

10 Ions — Charged Atoms §4.10

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
IonAn atom (or group) that has gained or lost electrons → net charge. Protons stay fixed; only electrons change.—
Cation (+)Positive ion; formed when an atom (usually a METAL) LOSES electrons. Named the same as the parent atom.Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ ("sodium ion"). Memory: cation, the "t" is a "+".
Anion (−)Negative ion; formed when an atom (usually a NONMETAL) GAINS electrons. Named as root + "-ide".Cl → Cl⁻ (chloride); O → O²⁻ (oxide).
Predict charge from groupAtoms gain/lose electrons to reach the nearest noble-gas electron count (octet). So charge is predictable by column:1A → 1+ · 2A → 2+ · (3A → 3+) · 5A → 3− · 6A → 2− · 7A → 1− · 8A → 0.
Isoelectronic idea (the "why")Ions form so their electron count matches a noble gas. Different ions can have the same # electrons.Na⁺, Mg²⁺, F⁻, O²⁻ all have 10 e⁻ (like Ne).
ID an ion from particle countsKey equation: protons = electrons + charge (charge signed: +2 adds, −1 subtracts). Then protons → element via Z.Ex 1: 3+ ion, 23 e⁻ → p = 23+3 = 26 → Fe³⁺. Ex 2: X⁺, 54 e⁻, 78 n → p = 55 (Cs), A = 55+78 = 133. CC1: XCl₂ so X is 2+; 20 e⁻ → p = 22 → Ti²⁺ (not Ca!).

11 Compounds That Contain Ions §4.11

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Ionic compoundForms when cations + anions attract and combine (electrostatic attraction). Usually metal + nonmetal.NaCl.
PropertiesHigh melting points. Conduct electricity when melted or dissolved in water (ions free to move) — but NOT as a solid.—
Neutrality ruleAn ionic compound is electrically neutral — total + charge and total − charge must sum to zero.—
Writing ionic formulasWrite cation first, then anion; choose subscripts so charges cancel. Crossover trick: use each ion's charge number as the other's subscript, then reduce.Ca²⁺ + Br⁻ → CaBr₂. Al³⁺ + O²⁻ → Al₂O₃. CC2: alkaline-earth ion with 36 e⁻ = Sr²⁺; with Br⁻ → SrBr₂.

★ The One Diagram to Anchor It All

MATTER ├── Pure Substances (fixed composition) │ ├── Elements → one type of atom (O₂, Fe, He) │ └── Compounds → 2+ elements bonded (H₂O, NaCl, CO₂) └── Mixtures (variable composition, not bonded) (air, salt water)

The two distinctions tested most:  ① Element vs compound → one atom type vs multiple types bonded.  ② Compound vs mixture → chemically bonded / fixed ratio vs physically mixed / variable ratio.

⭐ Highest-Yield — Drill These First

  1. Isotope calculations — from ᴬZX: protons = Z, neutrons = A − Z; identify element from Z.
  2. ID ions from particle counts — protons = electrons + charge; then name/identify.
  3. Predict ion charge from group (1A→1+, 2A→2+, 7A→1−, 8A→0) via the octet rule.
  4. Write neutral ionic formulas — crossover so charges sum to zero (CaBr₂, Al₂O₃, SrBr₂).
  5. The 7 diatomic elements + carbon allotropes.
  6. History: Thomson (electron/plum pudding) → Rutherford (nucleus/gold foil) → Chadwick (neutron).
  7. Dalton's theory — the 5 points + which fail today (isotopes; nuclear reactions).
  8. Element vs compound vs mixture classification + the 3 subatomic particles' charge/mass/location.
