                                  Chemical Bonds  
                         Forces that hold groups of two or more atoms together and make them function as a unit  
              Bond energy      
           Energy required to break a chemical bond        
                                           
                                  Ionic Bonding  
                                   Ionic compo          und                  results when a metal reacts with a nonmetal  
           Electrons are transferred from an atom that can lose electrons relatively easily to an atom that has a high affinity for electrons        
                                           
                                  Covalent Bonding  
                                Covalent bonds result when electrons are shared by nuclei        
                                           
                                  Polar Covalent Bond  
                         Unequal sharing of electrons between two atoms  
    One atom attracts the electrons more than the other atom  
    Results in the development of bond polarity (partial positive and negative charges)

                                  Nature of Bonds and Their Relationship to        Electronegativity   
                             Electronegativity  
     Relative ability of an atom in a molecule to attract shared electrons to itself  
    On the periodic table, electronegativity generally increases across a period and decreases down a group for the representative elements  
    Range of electronegativity values is from 4.0 for fluorine (the most electronegative element) to 0.7 for cesium and francium (the least electronegative element)

                         Group 1 metals always form 1+ cations  
    Group 2 metals always form 2+ cations  
    Aluminum in Group 3 always forms a 3+ cation  
    Group 7 nonmetals form 1− anions  
    Group 6 elements always form 2− anions  
              
                                  Stable Compounds  
                         Atoms in stable compounds of the representative elements have a noble gas electron configuration  
     Metals lose electrons to reach a noble gas configuration  
     Nonmetals gain electrons to reach a noble gas configuration  
  
              
                                  Table 12.2: The Formation of Ions by Metals and Nonmetals  
                                            
                                  Electron Configurations of Ions  
                    Representative (main-group) metals form ions by losing enough electrons to achieve the configuration of the previous noble gas  
    Nonmetals form ions by gaining enough electrons to achieve the configuration of the next noble gas  
              
                                  Electron Configurations and Bonding  
                                 When a      nonmetal and a Group 1, 2, or 3 metal      react to form a binary ionic compound, the ions form in such a way that   
            the valence-electron configuration of the nonmetal is completed to achieve the configuration of the next noble gas and  
            the valence orbitals of the metal are emptied to achieve the configuration of the previous noble gas  
           
            When      two nonmetals      react to form a covalent bond, they share electrons in a way that completes the valence-electron configurations of both atoms

                                  Structures of Ionic Compounds  
                         Large amounts of energy are required to separate ionic compounds that result from the reaction of metals and nonmetals  
     Strong bonding in these ionic compounds results from the attractions among the oppositely charged cations and anions  
              
                                  Structures of Ionic Compounds      (continued 1)  
                         Actual solid in lithium fluoride contains huge and equal numbers of Li     +      and F     −      ions packed together in a way that maximizes the attraction of the oppositely charged ions   
                                            
                                  Structures of Ionic Compounds      (continued 2)   
                           Cations are always smaller than their parent atoms  
    Anions are always larger than their parent atoms  
                                           
                                  Ionic Compounds Containing Polyatomic Ions  
                         Polyatomic ions work in the same way as simple ions  
     Covalent bonds hold the individual polyatomic ions together, with all of the atoms behaving as a unit  
              
                                  Lewis Structure   
                         Shows how valence electrons are arranged among atoms in a molecule  
    Most important requirement      for the formation of a stable compound   
     Atoms achieve noble gas electron configurations (          octet rule     ,           duet rule     , and so on)  
                                           
                                  Writing Lewis Structures  
                                  Bonding pairs        are shared between two atoms  
             Unshared pairs      (         lone pairs     ) are not shared and not involved in bonding        
                                           
                                  Steps for Writing Lewis Structures  
                         Sum the valence electrons from all the atoms  
    Use one pair of electrons to form a bond between each pair of bound atoms  
    Arrange the remaining electrons to satisfy the octet rule for each second-row element and the duet rule for hydrogen

                                  The VSEPR Model  
                                  VSEPR:      Valence shell electron pair repulsion  
    Useful for predicting the molecular structures of molecules formed from nonmetals  
    The structure around a given atom is determined principally by minimizing repulsions between electron pairs  
              
                                  Two Pairs of Electrons  
                                 Whenever two pairs of electrons are present around an atom, they should be placed at an angle of 180° to each other to give a linear arrangement  
              Example:          BeCl         2        has a linear structure with a      180       °bond angle  
                                             
                                  Three Pairs of Electrons  
                                 Whenever three pairs of electrons are present around an atom, they should be placed at the corners of a triangle  
            Example: BF     3        has a trigonal planar structure  
            Boron atom is surrounded by three pairs of electrons  
         Greatest distance between electron pairs is achieved by angles of 120       °  
                                             
                                  Four Pairs of Electrons  
                                 Whenever four pairs of electrons are present around an atom, they should be placed at the corners of a tetrahedron  
              Example:          CH         4        has a           tetrahedral        arrangement with a bond angle of 1     09.5       °  
                                                                     
                                  Steps for Predicting Molecular Structure Using the VSEPR Model  
                         Draw the Lewis structure for the molecule  
    Count the electron pairs and arrange them in the way that minimizes repulsion  
     Put the pairs as far apart as possible  
    Determine the positions of the atoms from the way the electron pairs are shared  
    Determine the name of the molecular structure from the positions of the atoms  
              
                                  Rules for Predicting Molecular Structure Using the VSEPR Model   
                         Two pairs of electrons on a central atom in a molecule are always placed 180° apart  
       This is a linear arrangement of pairs     
    Three pairs of electrons on a central atom in a molecule are always placed 120° apart in the same plane as the central atom  
       This is a trigonal planar (triangular) arrangement of pairs     
    Four pairs of electrons on a central atom in a molecule are always placed 109.5° apart  
       This is a tetrahedral arrangement of electron pairs     
              
                                  Rules for Predicting Molecular Structure Using the VSEPR Model      (continued)  
                         When every pair of electrons on the central atom is shared with another atom, the molecular structure has the same name as the arrangement of electron pairs  
    When one or more of the electron pairs around a central atom are unshared (lone pairs), the name for the molecular structure is different from that for the arrangement of electron pairs
