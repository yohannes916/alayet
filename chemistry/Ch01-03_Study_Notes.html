CHAPTER 1 · Thinking and Measuring Like a ChemistWhy measurement is an argument, not just a number

1Thinking and Measuring Like a Chemist

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Chemistry and matterChemistry studies the structure, composition, properties, and behavior of matter. Matter has mass and occupies space; energy is not matter.A flame includes matter (hot gases) and energy transfer (light/heat).
Observation → questionAn observation is recorded evidence. A good scientific question is specific and testable. Qualitative observations use descriptions; quantitative observations use numbers and units.“Blue” is qualitative; “absorbs at 450 nm” is quantitative.
HypothesisA tentative, testable explanation that produces predictions. It must be capable of being contradicted by evidence.“The liquid boiled faster because pressure was lower” predicts a measurable pressure/boiling relation.
Experiment and controlsChange one independent variable, measure the dependent variable, and hold other relevant variables constant. Replication distinguishes a pattern from chance.A control gives a baseline; it does not mean “nothing happens.”
Theory vs lawA law describes a reproducible relationship; a theory explains a broad body of evidence. Theories do not become laws.Both may be revised if better evidence appears.
Measurement languageEvery reported value carries magnitude, unit, and precision

2Measurement language

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
MeasurementA measurement is a comparison with a standard and must include number + unit. The final reported digit is estimated.Writing 5 without a unit is not a complete experimental measurement.
Accuracy vs precisionAccuracy = closeness to the accepted value. Precision = closeness of repeated results to one another and/or measurement resolution.A tight cluster can be precise but inaccurate if calibration is biased.
UncertaintyAll measured quantities have uncertainty. More displayed digits are justified only by the instrument or method—not by the calculator.A ruler marked every mm cannot honestly report nanometers.
Exact numbersCounts and defined relationships have unlimited significant figures.12 eggs; 1 in = 2.54 cm exactly. They never limit sig figs.
SI system and prefixesThe metric system turns scale changes into powers of ten

3SI system and prefixes

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Base unitsLength m; mass kg; time s; temperature K; amount mol; current A; luminous intensity cd.Chemistry often uses derived or convenient forms such as g, mL, cm³.
Prefix ladderkilo k=10³; centi c=10⁻²; milli m=10⁻³; micro μ=10⁻⁶; nano n=10⁻⁹.1 km=10³ m; 1 mg=10⁻³ g; 1 nm=10⁻⁹ m.
Volume links1 L = 1000 mL; 1 mL = 1 cm³; 1 m³ = 1000 L.Length factors must be cubed for volume: 1 m³=(100 cm)³=10⁶ cm³.
Mass vs weightMass is amount of matter; weight is gravitational force. Mass is location-independent, weight is not.Same mass on Earth and Moon; lower weight on Moon.
Temperature and scientific notationScale and exponent sense-making

4Temperature and scientific notation

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Particle meaningTemperature reflects average particle kinetic energy. Heat is energy in transit because of a temperature difference.Higher temperature means greater average motion, not that every particle has identical speed.
KelvinK=°C+273.15. Kelvin has no degree sign and begins at absolute zero.25.0 °C=298.15 K; a change of 1 K equals a change of 1 °C.
Fahrenheit°F=(9/5)°C+32; °C=(5/9)(°F−32).The +32 must occur in the correct order.
Scientific notationWrite a×10ⁿ with 1≤|a|<10. Moving decimal left makes n positive; right makes n negative.1.05×10¹²; 4.060×10⁻⁹. Coefficient digits preserve sig figs.
Exponent arithmeticMultiply coefficients and add exponents; divide coefficients and subtract exponents; addition requires a common exponent.(2×10³)(3×10⁻⁵)=6×10⁻².
CHAPTER 2 · Reliable Chemical CalculationsPrecision rules, unit logic, density, and percentages

5Reliable Chemical Calculations

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Nonzero/captive zerosAll nonzero digits count; zeros between nonzero digits count.3456 has 4; 16.07 has 4.
Leading zerosZeros before the first nonzero digit are placeholders and do not count.0.00480 has 3 sig figs: 4, 8, final 0.
Trailing zerosTrailing zeros count when a decimal point explicitly shows precision. Scientific notation removes ambiguity.150 usually 2; 150. has 3; 1.50×10² has 3.
Rounding onceCarry guard digits throughout and round only the final result. Early rounding can change the answer.Next digit <5: stay; ≥5: increase retained digit by 1.
Multiply/divideAnswer has the same number of significant figures as the least precise factor.1.342×5.5=7.381→7.4 (2 sig figs).
Add/subtractAnswer has the same number of decimal places as the least precise term.23.445+7.83=31.275→31.28.
Dimensional analysisLet units expose the path and catch errors

6Dimensional analysis

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
EquivalencyAn equivalency states equal quantities; a conversion factor is either ratio, numerically equal to 1.1 tsp=5 mL → 5 mL/1 tsp or 1 tsp/5 mL.
Unit cancellationStart with the given and orient factors so unwanted units cancel. The surviving unit must be requested.3.5 tsp×(5 mL/1 tsp)=18 mL (2 sig figs).
RatesTreat numerator and denominator units independently.mi/hr→km/s requires miles→km and hours→seconds.
Squared/cubed unitsRaise the entire conversion factor to the same power.in³×(2.54 cm/1 in)³; never use the length factor only once.
Sanity checkEstimate direction and magnitude before trusting arithmetic.Converting liters to milliliters should make the number larger by 1000.
Density and displacementA bridge between mass and volume

7Density and displacement

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Definitiond=m/V; rearrange to m=dV or V=m/d. Density is intensive for a pure substance at fixed conditions.Typical units g/mL or g/cm³.
Displacement volumeFor an irregular solid, Vobject=Vfinal−Vinitial.22.3→27.2 mL gives 4.9 mL, limited to one decimal place.
Float/sink modelIn a given liquid, less dense objects tend to float and more dense objects sink, assuming other effects are negligible.Density compares equal volumes, not total masses.
Temperature dependenceMost materials expand on warming, increasing volume and lowering density; record temperature with precise density data.Ethanol: 0.806 g/mL at 0 °C vs 0.789 g/mL at 20 °C.
Percent compositionPart-to-whole reasoning with consistent units

8Percent composition

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Mass percentmass %=mass part/mass whole×100. Numerator and denominator must use compatible mass units.0.014%=0.00014 as a fraction.
Volume percentvolume %=volume part/volume whole×100.78% of 5.2 L=0.78×5.2=4.1 L.
Reverse percentpart=(percent/100)×whole; whole=part/(percent/100).Always convert percent to a decimal before multiplying.
CHAPTER 3 · Matter and ChangeUsing evidence to classify what exists and what happened

9Matter and Change

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
SolidFixed shape and fixed volume; particles vibrate about relatively fixed positions.Rigid does not mean particles stop moving.
LiquidFixed volume but adopts container shape; particles remain close while rearranging.Not easily compressed.
GasNo fixed shape or volume; expands to fill container and is compressible.Particles are far apart relative to their size.
Phase changeMelting, freezing, boiling, condensation, sublimation, deposition change physical state without changing chemical identity.H₂O remains H₂O in ice, liquid water, and vapor.
Properties and changesAsk whether identity changed

10Properties and changes

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Physical propertyObserved/measured without forming a new substance: color, state, density, hardness, melting/boiling point.“Boils at 78 °C” is a property, not a change.
Chemical propertyDescribes ability to form new substances: flammability, tendency to rust, reactivity with acid.A chemical property may be stated without the reaction currently happening.
Physical changeComposition stays the same; form, size, state, or distribution changes.Crushing salt, melting iron, dissolving sugar if recoverable unchanged.
Chemical changeAtoms rearrange into substances with new composition/properties.Burning wood, rusting iron, fermenting sugar, electrolysis of water.
Evidence cautionGas, precipitate, color, odor, light, or temperature change may support a reaction—but context matters.Bubbles from boiling are a phase change, not automatically chemical evidence.
Elements, compounds, and formulasPure-substance categories

11Elements, compounds, and formulas

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
ElementCannot be decomposed by ordinary chemical methods; contains one kind of atom.Fe, Al, H₂, O₂. A molecular element is still an element.
CompoundTwo or more different elements chemically combined in fixed proportions; decomposable chemically.H₂O, CO₂, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁. Properties differ from constituent elements.
Formula readingElement symbols identify kinds of atoms; subscripts give relative counts. A missing subscript means 1.N₂O₄ is a compound: two elements, six atoms per molecule.
Fixed compositionEvery pure sample of a compound has the same atom and mass ratios.Water is not “more watery” in one pure sample than another.
Pure substances and mixturesComposition and separation decide classification

12Pure substances and mixtures

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
Pure substanceFixed composition and characteristic properties; either element or compound.Pure H₂O, Au, O₂, CO₂.
MixturePhysical combination with variable composition; components retain identities and can be separated physically.Wood, wine, coffee.
Homogeneous mixtureUniform at the scale observed; one phase; also called a solution.Air, brass, gasoline, salt water.
Heterogeneous mixtureNonuniform regions or distinguishable phases.Oil/vinegar, sand/water, soil.
Scale mattersA mixture can appear uniform to the eye yet be heterogeneous under magnification; state the observation scale.Milk is colloidal even though it looks uniform.
Separation methodsChoose a physical property that differs

13Separation methods

ConceptWhat to understandKey facts / examples
FiltrationSeparates an insoluble solid from a fluid by particle size.Sand from water; not dissolved salt.
DistillationSeparates substances using different volatilities/boiling points.Water from salt solution or liquids with different boiling behavior.
Evaporation/crystallizationRemoves solvent or changes conditions so dissolved solid forms crystals.Recover salt from saltwater.
ChromatographySeparates because components partition differently between stationary and mobile phases.Ink pigments travel different distances.
Decision ruleIf separation is physical, original was a mixture. If chemical bonds must be broken, it was a compound.Electrolysis decomposes water chemically; boiling only separates/changes phase physically.
