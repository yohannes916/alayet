Lesson 30 — Integration by Parts I

The Product Rule, Run in Reverse · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 30 of 40

📚 All lessons← Lesson 29Next: Lesson 31 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: meet the second great integration technique. U-substitution reversed the chain rule; today's tool — integration by parts — reverses the product rule. It cracks integrals like ∫x·eˣ dx that u-sub can't touch, by trading your integral for an easier one. You'll learn the formula, where it comes from, and the art of choosing the parts.

1 A problem our tools can't solve (yet)

  • Try to compute ∫x·eˣ dx with what we know. U-sub needs a fingerprint: the derivative of the inside function sitting nearby as a factor.
  • Attempt u = x: its derivative is 1 — there's no fingerprint to absorb, and the leftover eˣ still has x in it. Stuck.
  • Attempt u = eˣ: its derivative is eˣ again — substituting changes nothing. Stuck again.
  • The real issue: x and eˣ are a product of two unrelated functions — neither is the derivative of the other's inside. U-sub undoes the chain rule, but nothing here came from a chain rule. A product needs a product-rule tool.

2 Deriving the tool from the product rule — slowly

  • Start from the product rule for two functions u(x) and v(x): (uv)′ = u′v + uv′.
  • Integrate both sides. The left side is easy — integrating a derivative just gives the function back (that's the FTC): uv = ∫u′v dx + ∫uv′ dx.
  • Rearrange — move one integral to the other side: ∫uv′ dx = uv − ∫u′v dx.
  • In differential shorthand (dv = v′dx, du = u′dx), this is the famous formula: ∫u dv = uv − ∫v du
  • Translate to plain English: "trade your integral for a hopefully-easier one." You pay a price of uv up front, and in exchange, ∫u dv becomes ∫v du — a different integral, where u has been differentiated and dv has been integrated. If you choose the parts well, the new integral is easier than the old one.

3 The ritual — and the flagship example

  • Every by-parts problem follows the same four-step ritual:
StepDo this
1Pick u = the part that gets simpler when differentiated
2Let dv = everything else (must be something you can integrate)
3Compute du (differentiate u) and v (integrate dv — no +C needed yet)
4Assemble: uv − ∫v du, then finish the new integral
Flagship: ∫x eˣ dx.
Pick u = x (differentiating turns it into plain 1 — much simpler) and dv = eˣ dx.
Then du = dx and v = ∫eˣ dx = eˣ.
Assemble: ∫x eˣ dx = uv − ∫v du = x eˣ − ∫eˣ dx = x eˣ − eˣ + C.
Check by differentiating (always!): d/dx[x eˣ − eˣ] = (1·eˣ + x·eˣ) − eˣ = x eˣ ✓. The product rule hands back exactly the integrand — the answer is certified.
  • Notice what the trade did: the hard ∫x eˣ dx became the easy ∫eˣ dx. The x got differentiated away.

4 Choosing u — the LIATE ladder

  • The whole game is choosing u well. A friendly priority list called LIATE almost always works: pick u from as high on the ladder as possible, and dv from what's left.
the LIATE ladder — pick u from as HIGH as you can L Logarithms ln x ← grab for u first I Inverse trig arctan x, arcsin x A Algebraic x, x², 3x + 1 T Trig sin x, cos x E Exponentials eˣ, e²ˣ ← last pick for u (but a great dv!)
  • The intuition behind the order: things at the top (logs, polynomials) get simpler when differentiated — ln x becomes 1/x, x² becomes 2x becomes 2. Things at the bottom (exponentials, trig) never simplify — eˣ stays eˣ, sin x just cycles to cos x. So differentiate the top stuff (u), integrate the bottom stuff (dv).
  • In ∫x eˣ dx: x is Algebraic, eˣ is Exponential. A sits above E → u = x. Exactly what worked.

5 Two more worked examples (watch the signs)

∫x cos x dx. LIATE: u = x (Algebraic beats Trig), dv = cos x dx → du = dx, v = sin x.
∫x cos x dx = x sin x − ∫sin x dx = x sin x − (−cos x) + C = x sin x + cos x + C.
Careful: ∫sin x dx = −cos x, and subtracting it gives +cos x. Signs are the #1 by-parts trap.
Check: d/dx[x sin x + cos x] = (sin x + x cos x) + (−sin x) = x cos x ✓.
∫x sin x dx. u = x, dv = sin x dx → du = dx, v = −cos x.
∫x sin x dx = x·(−cos x) − ∫(−cos x) dx = −x cos x + ∫cos x dx = −x cos x + sin x + C.
Check: d/dx[−x cos x + sin x] = (−cos x + x sin x) + cos x = x sin x ✓.

6 What if you pick u wrong?

  • Let's deliberately make the bad choice on the flagship: ∫x eˣ dx with u = eˣ, dv = x dx.
  • Then du = eˣ dx and v = x²/2, so the formula gives: ∫x eˣ dx = (x²/2)eˣ − ∫(x²/2)eˣ dx.
  • Look at the new integral: ∫(x²/2)eˣ dx — the power of x went UP from x to x². We traded our problem for a harder one. Each wrong-way trade raises the power forever.
  • The good news: a wrong choice isn't broken, just worse. The equation above is still perfectly true! You haven't made an error — you've just walked the wrong direction. Back up, swap the roles of u and dv, and walk the other way.
  • Rule of thumb: if the new integral looks harder than the one you started with, swap your choice.

1.flagship redo
Compute ∫x eˣ dx yourself, from scratch: state u, dv, du, v, assemble, and check by differentiating.

Worked solution

Background: redoing the flagship with your own hand builds the ritual into muscle memory: pick u (gets simpler), pick dv (integrable), compute du and v, assemble uv − ∫v du.
u = x, dv = eˣ dx → du = dx, v = eˣ.
Assemble: x eˣ − ∫eˣ dx = x eˣ − eˣ + C.
Check: d/dx[x eˣ − eˣ] = (eˣ + x eˣ) − eˣ = x eˣ ✓.
x eˣ − eˣ + C
2.choose u and dv only
For each integral, state u and dv (do NOT compute the integral): (a) ∫x sin x dx   (b) ∫x² eˣ dx   (c) ∫x ln x dx   (d) ∫x e5x dx.

Worked solution

Background: choosing is a separate skill from computing, so we drill it alone. Climb the LIATE ladder: Logs, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trig, Exponential — u comes from the highest rung present.
(a) x is Algebraic, sin x is Trig; A beats T → u = x, dv = sin x dx.
(b) x² is Algebraic, eˣ is Exponential → u = x², dv = eˣ dx. (Differentiating x² twice will eventually kill it — that's Lesson 31's "repeat" trick.)
(c) ln x is a Log — the very top of the ladder → u = ln x, dv = x dx. (Even though x looks "simpler," ln x is the one that improves when differentiated: it becomes 1/x.)
(d) u = x, dv = e5x dx — same shape as (a) and the flagship.
(a) u = x, dv = sin x dx   (b) u = x², dv = eˣ dx   (c) u = ln x, dv = x dx   (d) u = x, dv = e⁵ˣ dx
3.by parts · trig
Compute ∫x cos x dx with the full ritual, and check your answer by differentiating.

Worked solution

Background: the trap in trig by-parts is the double negative: you'll subtract an integral that itself produces a minus sign. Write every sign out — no mental shortcuts.
u = x, dv = cos x dx → du = dx, v = sin x.
Assemble: x sin x − ∫sin x dx = x sin x − (−cos x) + C = x sin x + cos x + C.
Check: d/dx[x sin x + cos x] = (sin x + x cos x) − sin x = x cos x ✓.
x sin x + cos x + C
4.by parts · trig
Compute ∫x sin x dx, and check by differentiating.

Worked solution

Background: here v = −cos x carries a minus sign from the start, and then you subtract ∫v du. Two separate minus signs, handled one at a time.
u = x, dv = sin x dx → du = dx, v = −cos x.
Assemble: x·(−cos x) − ∫(−cos x) dx = −x cos x + ∫cos x dx = −x cos x + sin x + C.
Check: d/dx[−x cos x + sin x] = (−cos x + x sin x) + cos x = x sin x ✓.
−x cos x + sin x + C
5.check by differentiating
Without redoing the integral, verify by differentiation that x eˣ − eˣ + C really is an antiderivative of x eˣ. Name the differentiation rule you use.

Worked solution

Background: every by-parts answer contains a product (the uv term), so checking always uses the product rule — fitting, since by parts IS the product rule in reverse. This check is free insurance: use it on every problem today.
d/dx[x eˣ] by the product rule: (derivative of x)·eˣ + x·(derivative of eˣ) = eˣ + x eˣ.
d/dx[−eˣ] = −eˣ, and d/dx[C] = 0.
Total: eˣ + x eˣ − eˣ = x eˣ — exactly the integrand ✓.
Derivative is x eˣ ✓ (product rule) — the antiderivative is certified
6.conceptual
In a sentence or two: why does u-substitution fail on ∫x cos x dx?

Worked solution

Background: u-sub only undoes the chain rule — it needs an inside function whose derivative appears as a factor (the fingerprint). Recognizing WHY a tool fails tells you which tool to reach for instead.
Try u = cos x: du = −sin x dx, but there's no sin x factor in the integrand to absorb. Try u = x: du = dx, which changes nothing — the cos x still has x inside it... actually cos x is not a composite at all.
The integrand is a product of two unrelated functions — neither one's derivative appears as the other factor, so no fingerprint exists. Nothing here was produced by a chain rule, so no substitution can unwind it. It WAS produced by a product rule — hence integration by parts.
No fingerprint: neither factor's derivative appears in the product, so u-sub has nothing to absorb
7.by parts + divide-by-a
Compute ∫x e2x dx. (Remember from Lesson 17: ∫e2x dx = (1/2)e2x + C.) Check by differentiating.

Worked solution

Background: by parts combines smoothly with the divide-by-a rule for eax: every time you integrate e2x you pay a factor of 1/2. It happens twice in this problem — once for v, once in the final integral.
u = x, dv = e2x dx → du = dx, v = (1/2)e2x.
Assemble: x·(1/2)e2x − ∫(1/2)e2x dx = (x/2)e2x − (1/2)·(1/2)e2x + C = (x/2)e2x − (1/4)e2x + C.
Check: d/dx[(x/2)e2x] = (1/2)e2x + (x/2)·2e2x = (1/2)e2x + x e2x. And d/dx[−(1/4)e2x] = −(1/2)e2x. Total: x e2x ✓.
(x/2)e2x − (1/4)e2x + C
8.by parts + inner constant
Compute ∫x sin(2x) dx, and check by differentiating.

Worked solution

Background: same pattern as #7 but with trig: integrating sin(2x) costs a factor of 1/2 and a sign (∫sin(2x) dx = −(1/2)cos(2x)). Track the 1/2s and the minus signs separately.
u = x, dv = sin(2x) dx → du = dx, v = −(1/2)cos(2x).
Assemble: x·(−(1/2)cos 2x) − ∫(−(1/2)cos 2x) dx = −(x/2)cos 2x + (1/2)∫cos 2x dx.
Finish: (1/2)·(1/2)sin 2x = (1/4)sin 2x. Answer: −(x/2)cos 2x + (1/4)sin 2x + C.
Check: d/dx[−(x/2)cos 2x] = −(1/2)cos 2x + (x/2)·2 sin 2x = −(1/2)cos 2x + x sin 2x. d/dx[(1/4)sin 2x] = (1/2)cos 2x. Total: x sin 2x ✓.
−(x/2)cos 2x + (1/4)sin 2x + C
9.by parts · tidy surprise
Compute ∫(x + 1)eˣ dx. Simplify your answer as far as possible — something pleasant happens.

Worked solution

Background: u can be any polynomial, not just plain x. And sometimes the uv term and the traded integral partially cancel, collapsing into something tiny — always simplify at the end.
u = x + 1, dv = eˣ dx → du = dx, v = eˣ.
Assemble: (x + 1)eˣ − ∫eˣ dx = (x + 1)eˣ − eˣ + C.
Simplify: (x + 1)eˣ − eˣ = x eˣ + eˣ − eˣ = x eˣ. So the answer is just x eˣ + C.
Check: d/dx[x eˣ] = eˣ + x eˣ = (x + 1)eˣ ✓ — exactly the integrand.
x eˣ + C
10.constant in front
Compute ∫3x cos x dx, and check by differentiating.

Worked solution

Background: Lesson 29's linearity rule teams up with by parts: slide the 3 out front, use the ∫x cos x result, multiply back through at the end.
∫3x cos x dx = 3·∫x cos x dx.
From the ritual (u = x, dv = cos x dx): ∫x cos x dx = x sin x + cos x + C.
Multiply by 3: 3x sin x + 3 cos x + C.
Check: d/dx[3x sin x + 3 cos x] = (3 sin x + 3x cos x) − 3 sin x = 3x cos x ✓.
3x sin x + 3 cos x + C
11.wrong choice demo
On ∫x sin x dx, deliberately make the wrong choice: u = sin x, dv = x dx. Carry it through one step, explain how you can TELL it was the wrong choice, and say what to do next.

Worked solution

Background: feeling a wrong choice go bad — on purpose, once — teaches you to recognize it instantly in real problems. The tell: the new integral is messier than the one you started with.
Wrong choice: u = sin x, dv = x dx → du = cos x dx, v = x²/2.
Assemble: ∫x sin x dx = (x²/2)sin x − ∫(x²/2)cos x dx.
The tell: the new integral ∫(x²/2)cos x dx has x² where we used to have x — the power went UP. We traded for a HARDER problem. (Not a mathematical error — the equation is true — just the wrong direction.)
What to do: back up and swap: u = x, dv = sin x dx, which gives −x cos x + sin x + C (exercise 4). LIATE would have said so: Algebraic sits above Trig.
New integral gains x² → harder → swap the choice (LIATE: u = x)
12.by parts · e⁻ˣ
Compute ∫x e−x dx. (Recall ∫e−x dx = −e−x + C.) Check by differentiating.

Worked solution

Background: e−x is eax with a = −1, so every integration of it costs a factor of 1/(−1) = −1. The minus signs multiply up — write each one down.
u = x, dv = e−x dx → du = dx, v = −e−x.
Assemble: x·(−e−x) − ∫(−e−x) dx = −x e−x + ∫e−x dx = −x e−x − e−x + C.
Check: d/dx[−x e−x] = −e−x + x e−x (product rule with chain rule on e−x). d/dx[−e−x] = e−x. Total: −e−x + x e−x + e−x = x e−x ✓.
−x e−x − e−x + C
13.definite · beautiful
Evaluate ∫01 x eˣ dx exactly.

Worked solution

Background: for a definite integral, do by parts to get the antiderivative, then evaluate at the endpoints as usual with the FTC. Keep exact values (e, not 2.718...) until the very end.
Antiderivative (from #1): F(x) = x eˣ − eˣ.
F(1) = 1·e − e = 0.   F(0) = 0·1 − 1 = −1.
Integral = F(1) − F(0) = 0 − (−1) = 1.
Sense-check (Lesson 29 style): x eˣ ≥ 0 on [0, 1], so a positive answer is right; the function runs from 0 up to e ≈ 2.7, so a total of 1 over a width-1 interval is plausible ✓. All those e's melting into a clean 1 — that's a classic.
1
14.by parts + inner constant
Compute ∫x cos(3x) dx, and check by differentiating.

Worked solution

Background: the inner 3 costs a factor of 1/3 each time you integrate: ∫cos(3x) dx = (1/3)sin(3x), and integrating sin(3x) later costs another 1/3 — which is how the 1/9 appears.
u = x, dv = cos(3x) dx → du = dx, v = (1/3)sin(3x).
Assemble: (x/3)sin 3x − ∫(1/3)sin 3x dx = (x/3)sin 3x − (1/3)·(−(1/3)cos 3x) + C = (x/3)sin 3x + (1/9)cos 3x + C.
Check: d/dx[(x/3)sin 3x] = (1/3)sin 3x + (x/3)·3cos 3x = (1/3)sin 3x + x cos 3x. d/dx[(1/9)cos 3x] = −(1/3)sin 3x. Total: x cos 3x ✓.
(x/3)sin 3x + (1/9)cos 3x + C
15.definite · classic
Evaluate ∫0π x sin x dx exactly.

Worked solution

Background: a famous AP definite integral. The antiderivative comes from exercise 4; the endpoint arithmetic uses cos π = −1, cos 0 = 1, sin π = sin 0 = 0. Watch the minus-times-minus at x = π.
Antiderivative: F(x) = −x cos x + sin x.
F(π) = −π·cos π + sin π = −π·(−1) + 0 = π.
F(0) = −0·cos 0 + sin 0 = 0.
Integral = π − 0 = π. Sense-check: x sin x ≥ 0 on [0, π] (both factors non-negative there), so a positive answer ≈ 3.14 fits ✓.
π
16.polynomial u
Compute ∫(2x + 3)cos x dx, and check by differentiating.

Worked solution

Background: u = 2x + 3 differentiates to plain 2 — the whole polynomial melts to a constant in one step, so one round of by parts finishes the job.
u = 2x + 3, dv = cos x dx → du = 2 dx, v = sin x.
Assemble: (2x + 3)sin x − ∫sin x · 2 dx = (2x + 3)sin x − 2∫sin x dx = (2x + 3)sin x + 2 cos x + C.
Check: d/dx[(2x + 3)sin x] = 2 sin x + (2x + 3)cos x. d/dx[2 cos x] = −2 sin x. Total: (2x + 3)cos x ✓.
(2x + 3)sin x + 2 cos x + C
17.story · work done by a force
A motorized cart sits at position x = 0 on a track. As it moves, the motor's push is F(x) = x e−x newtons at position x meters (the push starts at zero, builds as the cart gets going). In physics, the work done moving from x = 0 to x = 1 is W = ∫01 F(x) dx joules. Find W exactly, then as a decimal (2 places).

Worked solution

Background (physics): when a force changes with position, work = force × distance becomes work = the integral of force over the distance — the same "chop, multiply, add" story as ever: each little step dx contributes F(x)·dx of work. The integrand x e−x needs by parts, done in exercise 12.
Antiderivative (from #12): F(x)'s antiderivative is −x e−x − e−x.
At 1: −1·e−1 − e−1 = −2/e.   At 0: −0 − e⁰ = −1.
W = (−2/e) − (−1) = 1 − 2/e.
Decimal: 2/e ≈ 2 ÷ 2.71828 ≈ 0.7358, so W ≈ 1 − 0.7358 ≈ 0.26. Sense-check: the force is non-negative on [0, 1] and never exceeds e−1 ≈ 0.37, so work between 0 and 0.37 joules is exactly right ✓.
W = 1 − 2/e ≈ 0.26 joules
18.definite · negative answer
Evaluate ∫0π x cos x dx exactly, then explain in one sentence why a negative answer makes sense here.

Worked solution

Background: definite integrals count area below the axis as negative. On [π/2, π], cos x is negative while x is large — so the late, below-axis area can outweigh the early, above-axis area. Lesson 29's sense-checks work on by-parts answers too.
Antiderivative (from #3): F(x) = x sin x + cos x.
F(π) = π·sin π + cos π = 0 + (−1) = −1.
F(0) = 0·sin 0 + cos 0 = 1.
Integral = −1 − 1 = −2.
Why negative makes sense: x cos x is positive only on [0, π/2] where x is small, but negative on [π/2, π] where the factor x is big — the larger below-axis area wins.
−2
19.fix the wrong answer
A student claims ∫x sin x dx = −x cos x + C. Differentiate the claim to show it's wrong, identify exactly what's missing, and fix it.

Worked solution

Background: this is the most common by-parts slip: writing the uv term and forgetting to finish the traded integral −∫v du. Differentiating the claim exposes the leftover instantly — which is why the check is worth 20 seconds every single time.
Differentiate the claim: d/dx[−x cos x] = −cos x + x sin x = x sin x − cos x.
We wanted x sin x, but we got x sin x − cos x: there's an unwanted −cos x left over. The claim is wrong.
What's missing: the student stopped after the uv term and never added ∫cos x dx = sin x from the traded integral.
Fix: add sin x. Check: d/dx[−x cos x + sin x] = (−cos x + x sin x) + cos x = x sin x ✓.
Correct answer: −x cos x + sin x + C (the traded integral was never finished)
20.challenge · definite
Evaluate ∫0π/2 x cos x dx exactly, and sense-check the sign and rough size of your answer.

Worked solution

Background: everything in one problem — by parts for the antiderivative, exact trig values at π/2, and a Lesson 29 sense-check at the end. Exact values: sin(π/2) = 1, cos(π/2) = 0.
Antiderivative (from #3): F(x) = x sin x + cos x.
F(π/2) = (π/2)·sin(π/2) + cos(π/2) = (π/2)·1 + 0 = π/2.
F(0) = 0·sin 0 + cos 0 = 1.
Integral = π/2 − 1.
Sense-check: on [0, π/2] both x and cos x are ≥ 0, so the answer must be positive — and π/2 − 1 ≈ 1.571 − 1 = 0.57 > 0 ✓. Rough size: the region fits inside a rectangle of width π/2 ≈ 1.57 and height at most π/2·1... the integrand peaks below 1, so an answer around 0.5 is very reasonable ✓.
π/2 − 1 ≈ 0.57
