🎯 Goal of this lesson: almost no new theory today — this is a practice lesson. You already know the Fundamental Theorem: ∫ab f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a). Today you drill it until evaluating a polynomial integral feels as automatic as long division: smooth, quick, and hard to mess up.

1 The three-step routine (memorize this box)

  • Every single problem in this lesson is solved with the exact same routine. Here it is:
THE ROUTINE — three steps, every time:
Step 1. Find the antiderivative F(x), term by term, with the power rule: xn → xn+1/(n+1). (A constant c becomes cx.)
Step 2. Plug the TOP limit into F: compute F(b).
Step 3. Plug the BOTTOM limit into F: compute F(a) — and SUBTRACT: answer = F(b) − F(a).
  • Why "term by term"? Because integration, like differentiation, treats each added term separately. ∫ (x2 + 2x) dx is just ∫ x2 dx plus ∫ 2x dx.
  • A coefficient rides along for free: the antiderivative of 6x2 is 6 · x3/3 = 2x3.
  • Handy shorthand: we write [F(x)]ab to mean "evaluate F at b, evaluate at a, subtract." The square brackets are a little to-do list.

2 The four classic mistakes (know your enemy)

  • Almost every wrong answer on FTC problems comes from one of these four slips. Look at each wrong-vs-right pair carefully — spotting them in advance is the cheapest way to get faster.
Mistake ① — forgetting to subtract F(a).
Problem: ∫13 2x dx. Antiderivative: F(x) = x2.
❌ Wrong: "F(3) = 9, done. Answer 9."
✅ Right: F(3) − F(1) = 9 − 1 = 8. The integral measures accumulation between the two limits, so the bottom value must always be subtracted — even when it feels small.
Mistake ② — dividing by the OLD power instead of the new one.
Problem: antiderivative of x3.
❌ Wrong: x4/3 (divided by the old power, 3).
✅ Right: x4/4 — raise the power to 4, then divide by that NEW power, 4. Check it: the derivative of x4/4 is 4x3/4 = x3 ✓. The derivative of the wrong version is 4x3/3 ✗.
Mistake ③ — sign errors when F(a) is negative: minus a negative is PLUS.
Problem: ∫13 (2x − 3) dx. Antiderivative: F(x) = x2 − 3x. So F(3) = 9 − 9 = 0 and F(1) = 1 − 3 = −2.
❌ Wrong: 0 − 2 = −2 (dropped the parentheses around the negative value).
✅ Right: F(3) − F(1) = 0 − (−2) = +2. Safety habit: always write F(a) inside parentheses before subtracting.
Mistake ④ — forgetting a term.
Problem: ∫02 (x2 + 3) dx.
❌ Wrong: antiderivative "x3/3" — the lonely +3 got left behind. That gives 8/3.
✅ Right: F(x) = x3/3 + 3x, so F(2) − F(0) = (8/3 + 6) − 0 = 26/3. Constants integrate too: 3 becomes 3x. Count your terms before and after — same number!

3 Fractions without fear

  • The power rule loves to hand you fractions: the antiderivative of x2 is x3/3, and plugging in x = 2 gives 23/3 = 8/3. Nothing scary happened — cube the number first, THEN divide by 3. Leave it as 8/3; a fraction is an exact, finished answer (a decimal like 2.6667 is not).
  • Adding a whole number to a fraction: rewrite the whole number over the same denominator. 8/3 + 2 = 8/3 + 6/3 = 14/3, because 2 = 6/3.
  • Adding fractions with different denominators: convert both to a common denominator first. Example: 1/4 + 1/2. The denominators 4 and 2 both fit into 4, so 1/2 = 2/4, and 1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4.
  • Subtracting works the same way: 6 − 2/3 = 18/3 − 2/3 = 16/3.
  • Tidy-up tip: if the top and bottom share a factor, simplify (27/3 = 9, 16/4 = 4) — but a fraction like 50/3 that won't simplify is a perfectly good final answer.

4 Two fully worked examples — every move shown

Example A: ∫13 (x2 + 2x) dx

Step 1 (antiderivative, term by term): x2 → x3/3 and 2x → x2 (because 2 · x2/2 = x2). So F(x) = x3/3 + x2.
Check by differentiating: derivative of x3/3 is x2 ✓, derivative of x2 is 2x ✓. We got back x2 + 2x — safe to continue.
Step 2 (top limit): F(3) = 27/3 + 9 = 9 + 9 = 18.
Step 3 (bottom limit, subtract): F(1) = 1/3 + 1 = 1/3 + 3/3 = 4/3.
Answer: 18 − 4/3 = 54/3 − 4/3 = 50/3. Done — and 50/3 stays as a fraction.
Example B: ∫02 (3x2 − 2x + 1) dx

Step 1: 3x2 → x3, −2x → −x2, 1 → x. So F(x) = x3 − x2 + x. Three terms in, three terms out ✓.
Check: derivative of x3 − x2 + x is 3x2 − 2x + 1 ✓.
Step 2: F(2) = 8 − 4 + 2 = 6.
Step 3: F(0) = 0 − 0 + 0 = 0.
Answer: 6 − 0 = 6. (When the bottom limit is 0 and there's no constant term weirdness, F(0) is usually 0 — but WRITE it anyway. The habit protects you when the bottom limit isn't 0.)

5 Pep talk: real speed comes from the CHECK habit

  • It feels slower to differentiate your F before plugging in numbers — but it's actually the fastest way to work. Here's why: a wrong antiderivative poisons everything after it, and you end up redoing the whole problem. The 5-second derivative check catches the error at the cheapest possible moment.
  • The check is easy because differentiating is easier than integrating — you already know the power rule for derivatives cold. Take your F(x), differentiate it in your head, and ask: did I get back exactly what's inside the integral? If yes, plug in with total confidence. If no, fix it now.
  • Fast musicians practice slowly. Do these 20 problems with the full routine — all three steps, plus the check — written out. By problem 15 the routine will start running itself, and THAT is what fluency feels like.

1.single term
Evaluate ∫02 x dx.

Worked solution

Background: the Fundamental Theorem says: find an antiderivative F (a function whose derivative is the thing inside), then compute F(top) − F(bottom). For x (which is x1), the power rule raises the power to 2 and divides by 2.
Step 1: F(x) = x2/2. Check: derivative of x2/2 is 2x/2 = x ✓.
Step 2: F(2) = 4/2 = 2.
Step 3: F(0) = 0. Subtract: 2 − 0 = 2.
2
2.single term
Evaluate ∫03 x2 dx.

Worked solution

Background: power rule for antiderivatives: xn becomes xn+1/(n+1) — raise the power by one, divide by the NEW power. For x2 that's x3/3 (divide by 3, not by 2!).
Step 1: F(x) = x3/3. Check: derivative is 3x2/3 = x2 ✓.
Step 2: F(3) = 27/3 = 9.
Step 3: F(0) = 0. Subtract: 9 − 0 = 9.

3.constant
Evaluate ∫04 5 dx.

Worked solution

Background: a constant rate accumulates steadily, so the antiderivative of a constant c is cx. (Picture check: the region under the flat line y = 5 from 0 to 4 is a rectangle, 4 wide and 5 tall.)
Step 1: F(x) = 5x. Check: derivative of 5x is 5 ✓.
Step 2: F(4) = 20.
Step 3: F(0) = 0. Subtract: 20 − 0 = 20.
Rectangle check: width 4 × height 5 = 20 ✓ — the FTC agrees with plain geometry.

Worked solution

Step 1: F(x) = x2 + x. Check: derivative is 2x + 1 ✓.
Step 2: F(3) = 9 + 3 = 12.
Step 3: F(0) = 0 + 0 = 0. Subtract: 12 − 0 = 12.

7.fraction adding
Evaluate ∫02 (x2 + x) dx. Leave your answer as a fraction.

Worked solution

Background: when the two terms produce different denominators (here thirds and a whole number), rewrite everything over one common denominator before adding. Remember: a whole number n equals 3n/3.
Step 1: F(x) = x3/3 + x2/2. Check: derivative is x2 + x ✓.
Step 2: F(2) = 8/3 + 4/2 = 8/3 + 2 = 8/3 + 6/3 = 14/3.
Step 3: F(0) = 0. Subtract: 14/3 − 0 = 14/3.

8.nonzero bottom limit
Evaluate ∫13 x2 dx.

Worked solution

Background: now the bottom limit is 1, not 0 — so F(bottom) is NOT zero and the subtraction really matters. This is where Mistake ① (forgetting to subtract F(a)) costs points.
Step 1: F(x) = x3/3. Check: derivative is x2 ✓.
Step 2: F(3) = 27/3 = 9.
Step 3: F(1) = 1/3. Subtract: 9 − 1/3 = 27/3 − 1/3 = 26/3.

9.both limits nonzero
Evaluate ∫12 (2x + 3) dx.

Worked solution

Background: the full routine with nothing free: two terms to antidifferentiate AND a nonzero bottom limit. Write F(a) in parentheses before subtracting — that habit prevents sign slips later.
Step 1: F(x) = x2 + 3x. Check: derivative is 2x + 3 ✓.
Step 2: F(2) = 4 + 6 = 10.
Step 3: F(1) = 1 + 3 = 4. Subtract: 10 − (4) = 6.

10.coefficients do the canceling
Evaluate ∫01 (4x3 + 3x2) dx.

Worked solution

Background: sometimes the coefficients are chosen so the division cancels perfectly: 4x3 becomes 4 · x4/4 = x4, and 3x2 becomes 3 · x3/3 = x3. Clean antiderivatives like this are common on tests — enjoy them.
Step 1: F(x) = x4 + x3. Check: derivative is 4x3 + 3x2 ✓.
Step 2: F(1) = 1 + 1 = 2.
Step 3: F(0) = 0. Subtract: 2 − 0 = 2.

11.fraction subtraction
Evaluate ∫14 (x + 2) dx. Leave your answer as a fraction.

Worked solution

Background: here F(a) itself is a mixed pile: 1/2 + 2. Combine it into a single fraction FIRST (1/2 + 2 = 1/2 + 4/2 = 5/2), then do one clean subtraction. Doing arithmetic in tidy stages beats juggling everything at once.
Step 1: F(x) = x2/2 + 2x. Check: derivative is x + 2 ✓.
Step 2: F(4) = 16/2 + 8 = 8 + 8 = 16.
Step 3: F(1) = 1/2 + 2 = 5/2. Subtract: 16 − 5/2 = 32/2 − 5/2 = 27/2.
27/2
12.spot the error
A student wrote this solution: "∫03 x2 dx = [x3/2]03 = 27/2 − 0 = 27/2." Find the mistake, name it, and fix the answer.

Worked solution

Background: error-spotting builds fluency faster than plain practice, because you have to actively CHECK someone's antiderivative instead of trusting it. Tool: differentiate their F and see if you get the integrand back.
Check their F: the derivative of x3/2 is 3x2/2 — NOT x2. Busted.
This is Mistake ②: they divided by the OLD power (2) instead of the NEW power (3). Correct antiderivative: x3/3.
Fix: F(3) − F(0) = 27/3 − 0 = 9.
Mistake ②, dividing by the old power; correct answer: 9
13.negative coefficient
Evaluate ∫02 (3x2 − 4x) dx. (The answer may surprise you — say one sentence about what it might mean.)

Worked solution

Background: a minus sign in the integrand just rides along through the routine: −4x has antiderivative −4 · x2/2 = −2x2. Handle the sign like a coefficient and nothing changes.
Step 1: F(x) = x3 − 2x2. Check: derivative is 3x2 − 4x ✓.
Step 2: F(2) = 8 − 8 = 0.
Step 3: F(0) = 0. Subtract: 0 − 0 = 0.
Zero! The curve 3x2 − 4x dips below the x-axis for a while and comes back up, and the two effects exactly cancel. Lesson 9 is entirely about this idea.

14.both limits nonzero · fractions
Evaluate ∫23 (x2 − 1) dx. Leave your answer as a fraction.

Worked solution

Background: everything at once — two terms, a negative sign, both limits nonzero, and fraction arithmetic in F(a). Work in stages: fully simplify F(b), fully simplify F(a), then subtract once.
Step 1: F(x) = x3/3 − x. Check: derivative is x2 − 1 ✓.
Step 2: F(3) = 27/3 − 3 = 9 − 3 = 6.
Step 3: F(2) = 8/3 − 2 = 8/3 − 6/3 = 2/3. Subtract: 6 − (2/3) = 18/3 − 2/3 = 16/3.

15.three terms
Evaluate ∫12 (3x2 − 2x + 1) dx.

Worked solution

Background: three terms means three chances to drop one (Mistake ④). Count them: 3x2, −2x, +1 → three terms in, so F must have three terms out.
Step 1: F(x) = x3 − x2 + x (three terms ✓). Check: derivative is 3x2 − 2x + 1 ✓.
Step 2: F(2) = 8 − 4 + 2 = 6.
Step 3: F(1) = 1 − 1 + 1 = 1. Subtract: 6 − (1) = 5.

16.spot the error
A student wrote: "∫12 2x dx = [x2]12 = 4." Find the mistake, name it, and fix the answer.

Worked solution

Background: this student's antiderivative is actually PERFECT — the derivative of x2 is 2x ✓. So the error must be in the plug-and-subtract stage. The square bracket [F(x)] with two limits is a to-do list with TWO items on it.
They computed F(2) = 4 and stopped. That's Mistake ①: forgetting to subtract F(a).
Fix: F(2) − F(1) = 4 − 1 = 3.
Sanity check with geometry: the region under y = 2x from 1 to 2 is a trapezoid with parallel sides 2 and 4 and width 1 → area = ½(2 + 4)(1) = 3 ✓.
Mistake ①, forgot to subtract F(1); correct answer: 3
17.spot the error · signs
A student wrote: "∫13 (2x − 3) dx: F(x) = x2 − 3x, so F(3) = 0 and F(1) = −2, and the answer is 0 − 2 = −2." Find the mistake, name it, and fix the answer.

Worked solution

Background: subtracting a NEGATIVE number is the same as adding: a − (−b) = a + b. The student found F(1) = −2 correctly, but then dropped the parentheses and subtracted 2 instead of subtracting (−2). This is the single most common sign error in all of calculus.
The antiderivative is right (derivative of x2 − 3x is 2x − 3 ✓), and F(3) = 9 − 9 = 0 ✓, F(1) = 1 − 3 = −2 ✓.
The last line is Mistake ③: F(3) − F(1) = 0 − (−2) = 0 + 2 = +2, not −2.
Mistake ③, minus a negative is plus; correct answer: 2
