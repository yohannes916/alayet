Lesson 15 — Two Special Functions: ex and 1/x

The number nature keeps choosing, and the power rule's missing case · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 15 of 20

📚 All lessons← Prev: Lesson 14Next: Lesson 16 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: meet two celebrity functions and their integrals: ∫ex dx = ex + C (the easiest antiderivative in the world) and ∫(1/x) dx = ln x + C (the case the power rule couldn't handle). Along the way, meet the number e and the natural logarithm ln from scratch.

1 A gap in the power rule

  • Your trusty power rule says: ∫xn dx = xn+1/(n+1) + C. Raise the power by one, divide by the new power. It has handled everything so far: x², x³, even x⁵.
  • But try it on 1/x, which is the same as x−1 (a power of −1). The rule says: raise the power to 0, then divide by the new power… divide by ZERO! x0/0 is meaningless.
  • This isn't a small technicality — the rule genuinely breaks for exactly one exponent: n = −1. Every other power of x has a power-of-x antiderivative. 1/x needs a completely different kind of answer.
  • So ∫(1/x) dx needs its own story — and the story stars a famous number. Meet it first.

2 Meet e — the number nature keeps choosing

  • There is a special number, written e, roughly e ≈ 2.718… — a never-ending decimal, like π. It shows up wherever things grow in proportion to their own size: populations (more rabbits make more rabbits), money with continuously-compounding interest (more money earns more interest), and much more.
  • Here's what makes e magical. Consider the function ex — the number e raised to the power x. This function has a property no other basic function has: it is its OWN derivative. At every point, its height equals its slope. When ex has height 5, it's climbing at slope 5. Height 100? Climbing at slope 100. Its growth rate always equals its size.
  • That is exactly what "growth proportional to size" means — which is why nature keeps choosing this function.
  • And for us integrators, this is fantastic news. An antiderivative of f is a function whose derivative is f. What has derivative ex? Why, ex itself!
The easiest antiderivative in the world: ∫ex dx = ex + C.
No powers to adjust, nothing to divide. The function just… stays itself. (Don't forget the + C — any constant shift also works, since constants have derivative 0.)

3 Meet ln x — the natural logarithm, from scratch

  • ln x (say it "ell-enn of x", the natural logarithm) answers one question: "e raised to WHAT power gives x?"
  • Practice reading it:
    • ln 1 = 0 — because e0 = 1 (anything to the 0 power is 1).
    • ln e = 1 — because e1 = e.
    • ln(e2) = 2 — because e to the power 2 gives e². (In general, ln undoes e-to-the-power.)
  • So ln x and ex are inverses — each undoes the other, the way squaring and square-rooting undo each other. ex turns a power into a value; ln turns the value back into the power.
  • Feel for its shape (for positive x): ln 1 = 0, ln e ≈ ln 2.72 = 1, ln(e²) ≈ ln 7.39 = 2. It climbs forever, but more and more slowly — to gain each extra +1, x has to multiply by e all over again.

4 The missing case, solved

  • Here is the beautiful fact that fills the power rule's hole: the derivative of ln x is exactly 1/x. (You'll prove this properly in a later course — for now, trust it and enjoy it.)
  • Plausibility check — the shapes match perfectly. ln x grows quickly when x is small (from x = 1 to x = 2.72 it gains a whole +1) and slowly when x is big (from 100 it needs to reach 272 to gain the next +1). Meanwhile 1/x is big for small x and small for big x. A function's derivative is its climbing speed — and ln's climbing speed behaves exactly like 1/x:
1/x — the slope of ln x 4 |· | · 1/x is TALL near 0 2 | · (ln climbs fast there) 1 | · · | · · · · …and LOW out here 0 +————————————————· · · · ·——→ x 0 1 2 3 4 5 ln x — always climbing, ever slower 2 | _ — ‾ 1 | _ — ‾ ‾ 0 +———·——————————————————————→ x | /1 (crosses zero at x = 1, −1 | | since ln 1 = 0)
The missing case: ∫(1/x) dx = ln x + C (for positive x).
The one power the power rule couldn't integrate turns out to have a logarithm as its antiderivative — a completely different species of function. That's why it needed its own lesson.

5 Worked examples — putting both to work

Example 1: ∫1e (1/x) dx = ln x evaluated from 1 to e = ln e − ln 1 = 1 − 0 = 1.
The area under 1/x from 1 to e is exactly 1 — a famous, beautiful fact.
Example 2: ∫01 ex dx = ex evaluated from 0 to 1 = e1 − e0 = e − 1 ≈ 2.718 − 1 = 1.72 (rounded).
Leave answers with e or ln in them in exact form first, then give a decimal if asked.
Example 3 (constant multiples ride along): ∫(5/x) dx = 5 ln x + C — because 5/x is just 5 × (1/x).
Likewise ∫2ex dx = 2ex + C. Check by differentiating: the derivative of 2ex is 2ex ✓.
  • Everything you know still applies: FTC (plug in top, subtract bottom), term-by-term integration, and checking answers by differentiating. Only the antiderivative lookup is new.

6 Where these two rule the world

  • Radioactive decay: the more atoms remain, the more decays happen per second — rate proportional to amount. The amount left follows an e-power curve.
  • Compound interest: money earning interest continuously grows in proportion to how much is there — banks literally compute with e.
  • Cooling coffee: the hotter your coffee is compared to the room, the faster it loses heat — so the temperature gap shrinks along an e-curve.
  • The pattern in all three: the rate of change is proportional to the amount present. Whenever a scientist writes that sentence, e walks in the door — and integrating those rates brings ln along too. These two functions aren't textbook curiosities; they're the mathematics of growth and decay itself.

1.ln warmup
Evaluate without a calculator: (a) ln 1   (b) ln e   (c) ln(e³).

Worked solution

Background: ln x asks one question: "e to WHAT power gives x?" To evaluate ln of something, rewrite that something as e-to-a-power; the answer is the power.
(a) e to what power gives 1? Power 0, since e0 = 1. So ln 1 = 0.
(b) e to what power gives e? Power 1. So ln e = 1.
(c) e to what power gives e³? Power 3. So ln(e³) = 3.
(a) 0   (b) 1   (c) 3

6.conceptual
Explain in a sentence or two: why can't the power rule ∫xn dx = xn+1/(n+1) + C handle 1/x?

Worked solution

Background: 1/x is a power of x in disguise: 1/x = x−1 (a negative exponent means "one over"). So it LOOKS like power-rule territory — until you actually turn the crank.
The power rule with n = −1 says: raise the exponent by one (getting x0) and divide by the new exponent, which is −1 + 1 = 0.
Dividing by zero is meaningless, so the rule produces nonsense for exactly this one exponent. The real antiderivative isn't a power of x at all — it's ln x, a different species of function.
n = −1 makes the rule divide by zero; 1/x needs ln x instead
7.mixed antiderivative
Find ∫(x2 + 1/x) dx.

Worked solution

Background: integrate a sum term by term, using whichever rule fits each term: the power rule handles x² (raise the power, divide by it), and the new ln rule handles 1/x. Old and new tools work side by side.
Term 1: ∫x2 dx = x3/3.
Term 2: ∫(1/x) dx = ln x.
Together (one + C covers everything): x³/3 + ln x + C.
x3/3 + ln x + C
8.check by differentiating
Your friend claims that F(x) = x³/3 + ln x is an antiderivative of f(x) = x² + 1/x. Verify the claim by differentiating F. (Recall: the derivative of x³/3 is x², and the derivative of ln x is 1/x.)

Worked solution

Background: every antiderivative claim can be checked with one move: differentiate the answer and see if you land back on the original function. This check is your safety net — it never lies, and it works no matter how the antiderivative was found.
Differentiate term by term: the derivative of x³/3 is 3x²/3 = x².
The derivative of ln x is 1/x.
Sum: x² + 1/x — exactly f(x). The claim is correct.
F′(x) = x² + 1/x = f(x) ✓ — the claim checks out
9.definite integral
Evaluate ∫1e (1/x) dx.

Worked solution

Background: the Fundamental Theorem: find an antiderivative, plug in the top limit, subtract the bottom limit. Antiderivative of 1/x: ln x. Then remember your ln values: ln e = 1 and ln 1 = 0.
∫1e (1/x) dx = ln x evaluated from 1 to e.
= ln e − ln 1 = 1 − 0 = 1.
Meaning: the area under the curve 1/x between x = 1 and x = e is exactly 1. (In fact, that's one way mathematicians define e!)
1
10.definite integral
Evaluate ∫1e2 (1/x) dx.

Worked solution

Background: same FTC recipe; the only new skill is evaluating ln at the top limit e². Remember: ln(e²) asks "e to what power gives e²?" — answer 2, because ln undoes e-to-the-power.
∫1e2 (1/x) dx = ln x evaluated from 1 to e².
= ln(e²) − ln 1 = 2 − 0 = 2.
Nice pattern: from 1 to e the area is 1; from 1 to e² it's 2. Each extra multiplication by e adds exactly 1 more unit of area.
2
11.definite integral
Evaluate ∫01 ex dx. Give the exact answer, then a decimal rounded to two places.

Worked solution

Background: antiderivative of ex is ex; then FTC. Useful values: e0 = 1 (anything to the 0 power is 1) and e1 = e ≈ 2.718. "Exact" means leave e in the answer; the decimal is an approximation.
∫01 ex dx = ex evaluated from 0 to 1 = e1 − e0 = e − 1.
Decimal: e − 1 ≈ 2.718 − 1 = 1.718 ≈ 1.72.
Exact: e − 1   ·   decimal ≈ 1.72
12.definite integral
Evaluate ∫02 ex dx. Exact answer first, then a decimal (use e² ≈ 7.39).

Worked solution

Background: same recipe as the last problem, wider interval. e² means e × e ≈ 2.718 × 2.718 ≈ 7.39. Notice how fast ex grows — doubling the interval much more than doubles the area.
∫02 ex dx = ex evaluated from 0 to 2 = e2 − e0 = e² − 1.
Decimal: e² − 1 ≈ 7.39 − 1 = 6.39.
Compare: [0, 1] gave ≈ 1.72; [0, 2] gives ≈ 6.39 — over 3½ times as much for twice the width. Exponential growth piles up fast!
Exact: e² − 1   ·   decimal ≈ 6.39

14.conceptual
In your own words: what is so special about the function ex, and why does that property make it the easiest function in the world to integrate?

Worked solution

Background: integrating means finding an antiderivative — a function whose derivative is the one you started with. Usually that's a hunt. For ex, the hunt ends before it starts.
The special property: ex is its own derivative. Its slope at every point equals its height at that point — its growth rate always equals its current size (which is why it models populations and compound interest).
Why that makes integration trivial: we need a function whose derivative is ex… and ex itself fits perfectly. The function is its own answer: ∫ex dx = ex + C. Nothing to adjust, nothing to divide.
ex is its own derivative (height = slope), so it's also its own antiderivative
15.definite · exact + decimal
Evaluate ∫14 (2/x) dx. Give the exact answer, then a decimal (use ln 4 ≈ 1.39).

Worked solution

Background: antiderivative of 2/x is 2 ln x (constant multiple rides along). At the bottom limit, ln 1 = 0 — a very convenient bottom limit! For decimals of ln values that aren't powers of e, a calculator (or a given approximation) is fine.
∫14 (2/x) dx = 2 ln x evaluated from 1 to 4.
= 2 ln 4 − 2 ln 1 = 2 ln 4 − 0 = 2 ln 4.
Decimal: 2 × 1.39 ≈ 2.77.
Exact: 2 ln 4   ·   decimal ≈ 2.77
16.definite · surprise answer
Evaluate ∫1e (1/x + 1) dx. Simplify your exact answer as far as possible — it comes out surprisingly clean.

Worked solution

Background: term-by-term: antiderivative of 1/x is ln x, antiderivative of 1 is x. Then FTC with limits 1 and e. Keep everything exact and watch the pieces combine.
Antiderivative: ln x + x. Evaluate from 1 to e:
Top: ln e + e = 1 + e. Bottom: ln 1 + 1 = 0 + 1 = 1.
Answer: (1 + e) − 1 = e. The whole integral collapses to just e ≈ 2.72!
e (≈ 2.72)
17.growth story
A bacteria colony grows so that new bacteria are added at the rate r(t) = et hundred bacteria per hour (the rate itself grows — because more bacteria make more bacteria!). How many bacteria are added between t = 0 and t = 2 hours? Exact, then approximate (e² ≈ 7.39).

Worked solution

Background: integrating a rate gives the total accumulated (Lesson 13) — that works for ANY rate function, including our new et. The units here are "hundred bacteria per hour," so the integral's answer is in hundreds of bacteria. This kind of self-feeding growth (rate proportional to size) is exactly where e lives in nature.
Total added = ∫02 et dt = et evaluated from 0 to 2 = e² − 1.
Approximate: e² − 1 ≈ 7.39 − 1 = 6.39 hundred bacteria.
6.39 hundred = about 639 bacteria.
Exact: e² − 1 hundred bacteria   ·   ≈ 639 bacteria
18.definite integral
Evaluate ∫1e (5/x) dx.

Worked solution

Background: constant multiple + the ln antiderivative + FTC. Remember the key values: ln e = 1, ln 1 = 0. When those are the limits, ln integrals come out beautifully clean.
∫1e (5/x) dx = 5 ln x evaluated from 1 to e.
= 5 ln e − 5 ln 1 = 5 × 1 − 5 × 0 = 5.

19.find the function
Find the function F such that F′(x) = 1/x AND F(1) = 4. (In words: among all the antiderivatives of 1/x, find the one whose graph passes through the point (1, 4).)

Worked solution

Background: ∫(1/x) dx = ln x + C describes a whole FAMILY of antiderivatives — one for each choice of C. An extra condition like F(1) = 4 pins down which family member you want: plug in the point and solve for C. (You did this "find the +C" move back in the antiderivative lessons; only the ln is new.)
General antiderivative: F(x) = ln x + C.
Use the condition: F(1) = ln 1 + C = 0 + C = C. We need this to equal 4, so C = 4.
Check: F(x) = ln x + 4 has derivative 1/x ✓ and F(1) = 0 + 4 = 4 ✓.
F(x) = ln x + 4
20.challenge
Evaluate ∫14 (2/x + x) dx. Give the exact answer, then a decimal rounded to two places (use ln 4 ≈ 1.39).

Worked solution

Background: a full mixed problem: one term needs the new ln rule (2/x → 2 ln x), one needs the old power rule (x → x²/2), and the FTC stitches it together. Keep exact values (with ln 4 left as ln 4) until the very last step.
Antiderivative: 2 ln x + x²/2. Evaluate from 1 to 4:
Top: 2 ln 4 + 16/2 = 2 ln 4 + 8. Bottom: 2 ln 1 + 1/2 = 0 + 0.5.
Exact: (2 ln 4 + 8) − 0.5 = 2 ln 4 + 7.5.
Decimal: 2 × 1.39 + 7.5 ≈ 2.77 + 7.5 = 10.27.
Exact: 2 ln 4 + 7.5   ·   decimal ≈ 10.27
