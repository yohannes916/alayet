ANSWER KEY — Integration Lesson 30 Test: Integration by Parts I

For the grader · full working shown · every answer verified by differentiation · give credit for correct method with small arithmetic slips

1. Set up the parts only. (a) ∫x e4x dx   (b) ∫x sin(3x) dx — state u, dv, du, v and the LIATE rung.
Work: LIATE = Logs, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trig, Exponential; u comes from the highest rung present.
(a) x is Algebraic, e4x is Exponential, and A sits above E → u = x, dv = e4x dx. Then du = dx and v = ∫e4x dx = (1/4)e4x (divide-by-a rule, a = 4; no +C needed for v).
(b) x is Algebraic, sin 3x is Trig, and A sits above T → u = x, dv = sin(3x) dx. Then du = dx and v = ∫sin(3x) dx = −(1/3)cos(3x).
Answer: (a) u = x (Algebraic), dv = e4x dx, du = dx, v = (1/4)e4x.   (b) u = x (Algebraic), dv = sin(3x) dx, du = dx, v = −(1/3)cos(3x).
Grader note: the two classic slips here are forgetting the 1/4 and the 1/3 when integrating dv, and losing the minus sign on v in (b) (∫sin = minus cos). No credit lost for omitting "+C" on v — that is correct practice.
2. Compute ∫(x − 3)ex dx and check by differentiating.
Work: u = x − 3, dv = ex dx → du = dx, v = ex.
Assemble uv − ∫v du: (x − 3)ex − ∫ex dx = (x − 3)ex − ex + C.
Simplify: (x − 3)ex − ex = x ex − 3ex − ex = (x − 4)ex.
Check: d/dx[(x − 4)ex] = 1·ex + (x − 4)ex = ex + x ex − 4ex = (x − 3)ex ✓ — exactly the integrand.
Answer: (x − 4)ex + C  (equivalently (x − 3)ex − ex + C).
Grader note: both forms are fully correct — do not deduct for leaving it unsimplified. The error to watch for is writing (x − 3)ex + ex (sign of the traded integral flipped), which differentiates to (x − 1)ex, not the integrand. Also watch for the student who stops at (x − 3)ex and never finishes −∫v du.
3. Compute ∫(x − 1)cos x dx and check by differentiating.
Work: u = x − 1 (Algebraic beats Trig), dv = cos x dx → du = dx, v = sin x.
Assemble: (x − 1)sin x − ∫sin x dx = (x − 1)sin x − (−cos x) + C = (x − 1)sin x + cos x + C.
Check: d/dx[(x − 1)sin x] = sin x + (x − 1)cos x; d/dx[cos x] = −sin x. Total: sin x + (x − 1)cos x − sin x = (x − 1)cos x ✓.
Answer: (x − 1)sin x + cos x + C.
Grader note: the #1 error is the double negative: ∫sin x dx = −cos x, and subtracting it gives +cos x. An answer of (x − 1)sin x − cos x + C means one minus sign was dropped; that is the standard sign slip. Half credit if the u/dv choice and assembly are right and only that sign is wrong.
4. Compute ∫4x sin x dx and check by differentiating.
Work: linearity first — slide the constant out: ∫4x sin x dx = 4∫x sin x dx.
For ∫x sin x dx: u = x, dv = sin x dx → du = dx, v = −cos x.
Assemble: x(−cos x) − ∫(−cos x) dx = −x cos x + ∫cos x dx = −x cos x + sin x.
Multiply back by 4: −4x cos x + 4 sin x + C.
Check: d/dx[−4x cos x] = −4 cos x + 4x sin x (product rule); d/dx[4 sin x] = 4 cos x. Total: −4 cos x + 4x sin x + 4 cos x = 4x sin x ✓.
Answer: −4x cos x + 4 sin x + C.
Grader note: classic errors — (i) multiplying only the first term by 4 (−4x cos x + sin x); (ii) taking u = 4x and dv = sin x dx, which is also perfectly correct (du = 4 dx) and must get full credit if carried out consistently: 4x(−cos x) − ∫(−cos x)(4 dx) = −4x cos x + 4 sin x, the same answer; (iii) the forgotten minus in front of ∫v du.
5. Why can't u-substitution finish ∫x e4x dx? Try u = 4x; name the rule the integrand came from.
Work: u-substitution only undoes the chain rule. It needs a "fingerprint": an inside function whose derivative sits nearby as a factor, ready to be absorbed into du.
Try u = 4x: then du = 4 dx, so dx = du/4, and x = u/4. The integral becomes ∫(u/4)eu(du/4) = (1/16)∫u eu du — still a plain-times-exponential product, exactly the same shape we started with. The substitution only rescaled the problem; it removed nothing.
Try u = e4x: du = 4e4x dx absorbs the e4x dx, but the leftover x has to be rewritten as x = (1/4)ln u, giving ∫(1/16)ln u du — a by-parts problem again, not simpler.
The real reason: x and e4x are a product of two unrelated functions — neither is the derivative of the other's inside, so there is no fingerprint. Nothing here was produced by a chain rule; it has the shape of a product rule output. A product needs a product-rule tool: integration by parts, ∫u dv = uv − ∫v du.
Answer: no fingerprint exists — neither factor's derivative appears as the other factor, so u-sub has nothing to absorb (u = 4x just rescales to (1/16)∫u eu du). The integrand came from the product rule, so reverse the product rule: integration by parts.
Grader note: full credit needs both halves — (1) u-sub reverses the chain rule / needs a derivative-fingerprint that is absent, and (2) by parts reverses the product rule. Accept any correct demonstration that a substitution attempt leaves a product behind.
6. Compute ∫x e4x dx and check by differentiating.
Work: u = x, dv = e4x dx → du = dx, v = (1/4)e4x.
Assemble: x·(1/4)e4x − ∫(1/4)e4x dx = (x/4)e4x − (1/4)·(1/4)e4x + C = (x/4)e4x − (1/16)e4x + C.
Where the fractions come from: one 1/4 when integrating dv to get v, a second 1/4 when integrating e4x in the traded integral → 1/16.
Check: d/dx[(x/4)e4x] = (1/4)e4x + (x/4)·4e4x = (1/4)e4x + x e4x; d/dx[−(1/16)e4x] = −(1/16)·4e4x = −(1/4)e4x. Total: x e4x ✓ — the two (1/4)e4x terms cancel.
Answer: (x/4)e4x − (1/16)e4x + C.
Grader note: the usual slips are −(1/4)e4x instead of −(1/16)e4x (the second divide-by-4 was skipped) and −(1/8)e4x (adding the 1/4's instead of multiplying). Both are caught instantly by the differentiation check — if the check was not shown, note it.
7. Compute ∫x sin(3x) dx and check by differentiating.
Work: u = x, dv = sin(3x) dx → du = dx, v = −(1/3)cos(3x).
Assemble: x·(−(1/3)cos 3x) − ∫(−(1/3)cos 3x) dx = −(x/3)cos 3x + (1/3)∫cos 3x dx.
Finish: (1/3)·(1/3)sin 3x = (1/9)sin 3x. So the answer is −(x/3)cos 3x + (1/9)sin 3x + C.
Check: d/dx[−(x/3)cos 3x] = −(1/3)cos 3x + (x/3)·3 sin 3x = −(1/3)cos 3x + x sin 3x; d/dx[(1/9)sin 3x] = (1/9)·3cos 3x = (1/3)cos 3x. Total: x sin 3x ✓.
Answer: −(x/3)cos 3x + (1/9)sin 3x + C.
Grader note: two independent minus signs live here — one inside v = −(1/3)cos 3x, one in front of ∫v du — and they cancel to give the + on the second term. Answers of −(x/3)cos 3x − (1/9)sin 3x + C (double-negative mishandled) or +(x/3)cos 3x (v's minus dropped) are the two expected wrong answers. Also watch 1/3 vs 1/9.
8. On ∫x cos x dx a student picks u = cos x, dv = x dx. Carry it one step; (a) false or just unhelpful? (b) the tell? (c) the choice that works.
Work: u = cos x, dv = x dx → du = −sin x dx, v = x²/2.
Assemble: ∫x cos x dx = (x²/2)cos x − ∫(x²/2)(−sin x) dx = (x²/2)cos x + ∫(x²/2)sin x dx.
(a) The equation is true — it is a valid application of ∫u dv = uv − ∫v du. Nothing illegal happened; it is simply unhelpful.
(b) The tell: the new integral ∫(x²/2)sin x dx contains x² where the original had only x — the power went UP, so the traded problem is harder than the one we started with. Rule of thumb: if the new integral looks worse, back up and swap u and dv.
(c) The choice that works is u = x, dv = cos x dx (LIATE: Algebraic sits above Trig) → du = dx, v = sin x, so ∫x cos x dx = x sin x − ∫sin x dx = x sin x + cos x + C.
Check: d/dx[x sin x + cos x] = (sin x + x cos x) − sin x = x cos x ✓.
Answer: (a) true, just unhelpful. (b) the power of x rose from x to x², so the new integral is harder. (c) u = x, dv = cos x dx → x sin x + cos x + C.
Grader note: the key insight being graded is (a) — a bad choice of u is not a mathematical error, only a wrong direction. Deduct if the student calls the student's equation "wrong." Also expect du = +sin x dx (missing minus on the derivative of cos x); note that in (c) subtracting −cos x is what produces the +cos x.
9. Evaluate ∫01 x e2x dx exactly, and say why the answer must be positive.
Work: first the antiderivative. u = x, dv = e2x dx → du = dx, v = (1/2)e2x.
∫x e2x dx = (x/2)e2x − ∫(1/2)e2x dx = (x/2)e2x − (1/4)e2x. Call this F(x).
Check F: d/dx[(x/2)e2x] = (1/2)e2x + (x/2)·2e2x = (1/2)e2x + x e2x; d/dx[−(1/4)e2x] = −(1/2)e2x. Total x e2x ✓.
Now the FTC: F(1) = (1/2)e² − (1/4)e² = (1/4)e².   F(0) = 0·(1/2)·1 − (1/4)·1 = −1/4.
Integral = F(1) − F(0) = (1/4)e² − (−1/4) = (e² + 1)/4.
Sense-check: e² ≈ 7.389, so the value is ≈ 8.389/4 ≈ 2.10. The integrand x e2x runs from 0 up to e² ≈ 7.4 across a width-1 interval, so a total near 2 is plausible ✓.
Why positive: on [0, 1] both x ≥ 0 and e2x > 0, so the integrand is never negative — the whole region sits on or above the axis.
Answer: (e² + 1)/4  (= e²/4 + 1/4 ≈ 2.10), positive because x e2x ≥ 0 on [0, 1].
Grader note: accept e²/4 + 1/4, (1/4)(e² + 1), or (1/4)e² + 1/4 — all identical. Classic errors: F(0) = 0 (forgetting that −(1/4)e0 = −1/4, since e0 = 1) which gives e²/4; and subtracting in the wrong order. Decimal-only answers lose the "exact" requirement.
10. (a) ∫(2x + 1)e−x dx, simplified. (b) Check by differentiating. (c) Evaluate ∫01 (2x + 1)e−x dx exactly.
Work (a): u = 2x + 1, dv = e−x dx → du = 2 dx, v = −e−x (since e−x is eax with a = −1, integrating costs a factor 1/(−1)).
Assemble: (2x + 1)(−e−x) − ∫(−e−x)(2 dx) = −(2x + 1)e−x + 2∫e−x dx = −(2x + 1)e−x − 2e−x + C.
Simplify: factor out −e−x: −(2x + 1 + 2)e−x = −(2x + 3)e−x.
(b) Check: d/dx[−(2x + 3)e−x] = −2e−x − (2x + 3)(−e−x) = −2e−x + (2x + 3)e−x = (2x + 3 − 2)e−x = (2x + 1)e−x ✓.
(c) FTC with F(x) = −(2x + 3)e−x: F(1) = −(2 + 3)e−1 = −5/e.   F(0) = −(0 + 3)e0 = −3.
Integral = F(1) − F(0) = −5/e − (−3) = 3 − 5/e.
Sense-check: 5/e ≈ 5 ÷ 2.71828 ≈ 1.839, so the value ≈ 1.16 > 0. The integrand runs from 1 down to 3/e ≈ 1.10 on [0, 1], all positive, so a total just above 1 is exactly right ✓.
Answer: (a) −(2x + 3)e−x + C   (c) 3 − 5/e ≈ 1.16.
Grader note: the minus signs pile up three deep here — in v, in front of ∫v du, and in the chain rule during the check. Expected wrong answers: −(2x + 1)e−x − 2e−x left unsimplified (that is CORRECT — give full credit), −(2x − 1)e−x (sign slip when combining), and 5/e − 3 (endpoints subtracted backwards). Any answer equal to 3 − 5/e, e.g. (3e − 5)/e, is fine.
