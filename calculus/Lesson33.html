Lesson 33 — Improper Integrals: Infinite Endpoints

Limits, Infinity & Finite Areas from Endless Regions · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 33 of 40

📚 All lessons← Lesson 32Next: Lesson 34 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: meet the idea of a limit — "what value is this heading toward?" — and use it to answer one of the most surprising questions in all of math: can a region that is INFINITELY LONG have a FINITE area? Astonishingly, sometimes yes — and you'll learn to tell exactly when.

1 Limits — the friendliest new idea in this course

  • You've never needed limits before, so let's start from zero. A limit asks one plain question: "as this number keeps growing (or shrinking), where is the expression heading?" Not "what is it AT infinity" — infinity isn't a place you can stand — but where is it heading as you keep zooming out.
  • Try it with 1/b. Let b grow: 1/10 = 0.1, then 1/100 = 0.01, then 1/1000 = 0.001... The values march steadily toward 0 and get as close as you like. We write: as b → ∞, 1/b → 0, or in symbols, limb→∞ 1/b = 0. Read "→" as "heads toward."
  • 1/b² rushes to 0 even faster (1/100, 1/10000, ...). But b itself just grows without bound — it heads to ∞. And ln b? It grows very slowly (ln 1000 ≈ 6.9), but it never stops climbing — it also → ∞. Slow growth is still growth!
  • Limits also work near ordinary numbers. As x → 0⁺ (x slides down toward 0 from the positive side): √x → 0 quietly, but 1/√x blows up: 1/√0.01 = 10, 1/√0.0001 = 100, ... → ∞.

The habit to build: when you see limb→∞, don't panic. Just ask: "if b were a million, roughly what would this be? A billion?" Keep zooming out — where is it heading? That heading IS the limit.

2 The wild question: infinite length, finite area?

  • Every area problem so far had two walls: ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx from a wall at a to a wall at b. Today we knock down the right-hand wall and let the region run forever: what is the area under y = 1/x² from x = 1 all the way to infinity?
  • Gut reaction: "it never ends, so the area must be infinite." Reasonable — but wrong, sometimes! If the curve's tail shrinks toward 0 fast enough, the skinny slivers you keep adding get so tiny that the total settles down to an ordinary finite number.
  • You've met this flavor before: 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + 1/16 + ... has infinitely many terms, yet the total is exactly 1 (each step covers half the remaining gap). Infinitely many pieces do NOT have to add to infinity.

3 The method: replace ∞ with b, integrate, then let b → ∞

  • We can't plug ∞ into a formula — it's not a number. So we sneak up on it in three steps:
    Step 1: put a temporary wall at x = b instead of ∞.
    Step 2: integrate normally from 1 to b (all your old skills apply).
    Step 3: slide the wall out: let b → ∞ and read off the limit.
The flagship example: ∫₁^∞ 1/x² dx = limb→∞ [−1/x]₁^b = limb→∞ (−1/b + 1) = 0 + 1 = 1.
Read it slowly: the area from 1 to b is 1 − 1/b. Wall at b = 10? Area 0.9. Wall at 100? 0.99. At 1000? 0.999. The areas are heading somewhere unmistakable: exactly 1. An infinitely long region with area exactly 1 square unit!
  • When the limit is a finite number, we say the improper integral CONVERGES (it "settles"). The number is the area, full stop.

4 The shocking contrast: 1/x fails

  • Now try the near-identical curve y = 1/x: ∫₁^∞ 1/x dx = limb→∞ [ln x]₁^b = limb→∞ (ln b − 0) = ∞.
  • ln b grows slowly — but it grows forever. The area never settles. We say this integral DIVERGES: the infinitely long region under 1/x really does have infinite area.
  • Stare at that: 1/x and 1/x² look almost the same, both sink to 0 — yet one tail encloses area 1 and the other encloses infinite area. 1/x's tail is just barely too fat.

5 The p-pattern — a rule you can discover yourself

  • Two more quick experiments. p = 3: ∫₁^∞ 1/x³ dx = limb→∞ [−1/(2x²)]₁^b = 0 + 1/2 = 1/2. Converges, even faster than 1/x².
  • p = 1/2: ∫₁^∞ 1/√x dx = limb→∞ [2√x]₁^b = lim (2√b − 2) = ∞. Diverges — 1/√x is even fatter than 1/x.
  • Line up the evidence: p = 2 ✓ (area 1), p = 3 ✓ (area 1/2), p = 1 ✗, p = 1/2 ✗. The pattern:
The p-rule: ∫₁^∞ 1/x^p dx converges exactly when p > 1, and its value is 1/(p−1). It diverges when p ≤ 1. (Check: p = 2 gives 1/(2−1) = 1 ✓; p = 3 gives 1/2 ✓.) The borderline curve is 1/x itself — and the borderline loses.

6 Two more improper flavors

  • Exponential tails: ∫₀^∞ e−x dx = limb→∞ [−e−x]₀^b = lim (−e−b + 1) = 1. Beautiful: an infinite tail with area exactly 1. This exact integral is the backbone of probability — waiting times, radioactive decay, battery lifetimes all use it. e−x dies out so fast that it beats every 1/x^p.
  • Unbounded integrand (the spike): sometimes the interval is finite but the FUNCTION blows up at an endpoint. Same trick, aimed at the other end — put a temporary wall at a small number a and let a → 0⁺: ∫₀¹ 1/√x dx = lima→0⁺ [2√x]ₐ¹ = lim (2 − 2√a) = 2. An infinitely tall spike enclosing just 2 square units of area!
  • But not every spike behaves: ∫₀¹ 1/x dx = lima→0⁺ (ln 1 − ln a) = lim (−ln a) = ∞ — diverges. Once again 1/x sits exactly on the losing side of the border.

7 Making sense of it all

  • One sentence to keep: an improper integral converges when the tail (or spike) shrinks FAST ENOUGH for the leftover slivers of area to fizzle out.
  • The universal borderline is 1/x. Shrink faster than 1/x at infinity (like 1/x², 1/x³, e−x) → converge. Shrink at 1/x's pace or slower (1/x, 1/√x) → diverge. Near a spike at 0 the roles flip: 1/√x is thinner than 1/x near zero, so it converges there, while 1/x² is fatter near zero and diverges.
  • Vocabulary check: improper integral = an integral with ∞ as an endpoint OR an integrand that blows up. Convergent = the limit is a finite number. Divergent = it isn't.

1.limit warmup
Find each limit by asking "where is this heading?": (a) limb→∞ 1/b   (b) limb→∞ 1/b²   (c) limb→∞ (5 + 3/b)

Worked solution

Background: a limit is not a plug-in — it's a heading. Test each expression with a huge b (say a million) and watch where the values are drifting. If they crowd closer and closer to one number, that number is the limit.
(a) 1/1000000 = 0.000001 — squeezing down to 0.
(b) 1/b² shrinks even faster (one over a trillion) — also 0.
(c) 5 + 3/b: the 5 just sits there; the 3/b piece fades to 0. Heading: 5.
(a) 0   (b) 0   (c) 5
2.limit warmup
Find each limit as b → ∞: (a) limb→∞ ln b   (b) limb→∞ e−b   (c) limb→∞ (7 − 1/b²)

Worked solution

Background: not everything settles! Some expressions grow without bound — we say they head to ∞ (the limit "does not exist as a number"). ln b is the classic trickster: it grows absurdly slowly (ln of a million is only about 13.8) but it NEVER stops climbing.
(a) ln b climbs forever, just slowly: ∞ (diverges — no finite limit).
(b) e−b = 1/eb, and eb explodes, so 1 over it dies: 0.
(c) the 1/b² piece fades away, leaving 7.
(a) ∞ (no finite limit)   (b) 0   (c) 7
3.limit warmup · x → 0⁺
Now slide x down toward 0 from the positive side. Find: (a) limx→0⁺ √x   (b) limx→0⁺ 1/x   (c) limx→0⁺ 1/√x

Worked solution

Background: limits work near ordinary numbers too. "x → 0⁺" means x takes values like 0.1, 0.01, 0.0001, ... always positive, sneaking toward 0. Watch each expression on that journey.
(a) √0.01 = 0.1, √0.0001 = 0.01 — gently down to 0.
(b) 1/0.01 = 100, 1/0.0001 = 10000 — blows up: ∞.
(c) 1/√0.0001 = 1/0.01 = 100 — also blows up (a bit more slowly): ∞.
(a) 0   (b) ∞   (c) ∞
4.the method
Using the three-step method (wall at b → integrate → let b → ∞), show carefully that ∫₁^∞ 1/x² dx = 1.

Worked solution

Background: this is the flagship of the whole lesson. ∞ is not a number you can plug in, so we integrate to a movable wall b and then ask where the answer heads as the wall slides out forever.
Step 1 — temporary wall: compute ∫₁^b x−2 dx.
Step 2 — integrate: the antiderivative of x−2 is −x−1 = −1/x. So ∫₁^b 1/x² dx = [−1/x]₁^b = −1/b − (−1/1) = 1 − 1/b.
Step 3 — slide the wall: as b → ∞, 1/b → 0, so 1 − 1/b → 1. (Check the heading: b = 10 gives 0.9, b = 100 gives 0.99, b = 1000 gives 0.999 — unmistakably heading to 1.)
∫₁^∞ 1/x² dx = 1 — convergent: infinite region, area exactly 1
5.infinite endpoint
Evaluate ∫₁^∞ 1/x³ dx.

Worked solution

Background: same three steps, new power. Power rule refresher (Lesson 21): ∫x−3 dx = x−2/(−2) = −1/(2x²). Since 1/x³ shrinks even faster than 1/x², expect convergence to something smaller than 1.
∫₁^b x−3 dx = [−1/(2x²)]₁^b = −1/(2b²) + 1/2.
As b → ∞, 1/(2b²) → 0, so the area heads to 0 + 1/2 = 1/2.
Sanity check with the p-rule: p = 3 > 1, value 1/(p−1) = 1/(3−1) = 1/2 ✓.
1/2 — convergent
6.infinite endpoint
Evaluate ∫₁^∞ 1/x⁴ dx.

Worked solution

Background: the pattern is forming — each higher power of x makes the tail thinner and the area smaller. Antiderivative: ∫x−4 dx = x−3/(−3) = −1/(3x³).
∫₁^b 1/x⁴ dx = [−1/(3x³)]₁^b = −1/(3b³) + 1/3.
As b → ∞, the first term → 0, leaving 1/3.
p-rule check: p = 4, value 1/(4−1) = 1/3 ✓. Notice the sequence: p = 2 → 1, p = 3 → 1/2, p = 4 → 1/3, ...
1/3 — convergent
7.different start
Evaluate ∫₂^∞ 1/x² dx. (Careful — the region now starts at x = 2, so don't expect the answer 1.)

Worked solution

Background: the p-rule value 1/(p−1) is only for integrals starting at x = 1. Starting farther out means you skip the fattest part of the tail, so the area is smaller. Always run the actual computation with your actual lower limit.
∫₂^b 1/x² dx = [−1/x]₂^b = −1/b + 1/2.
As b → ∞, −1/b → 0, so the limit is 1/2.
Sense check: from 1 to ∞ the area was 1; the strip from 1 to 2 holds area [−1/x]₁² = 1/2, and 1 − 1/2 = 1/2 ✓ — the two pieces add up perfectly.
1/2 — convergent
8.divergent
Show that ∫₁^∞ 1/√x dx diverges, and explain in one sentence WHY it diverges even though 1/√x → 0.

Worked solution

Background: shrinking to zero is NOT enough for convergence. What matters is shrinking fast enough. 1/√x = x−1/2 shrinks even more slowly than the borderline 1/x, so its tail is fatter than fat. Antiderivative: ∫x−1/2 dx = 2x1/2 = 2√x.
∫₁^b 1/√x dx = [2√x]₁^b = 2√b − 2.
As b → ∞, √b → ∞, so 2√b − 2 → ∞. The area never settles.
Why: the curve heads to 0 too slowly — each new stretch of tail still adds a chunky sliver of area, and those slivers never fizzle out. (p-rule: p = 1/2 ≤ 1 → diverge.)
Diverges — the tail shrinks too slowly (p = 1/2 ≤ 1)
9.fractional p
Evaluate ∫₁^∞ 1/x3/2 dx by the three-step method, then verify your answer against the p-rule.

Worked solution

Background: p doesn't have to be a whole number. x−3/2 sits between the divergent 1/x and the convergent 1/x² — which side of the border is it on? Since 3/2 > 1, the rule predicts convergence. Power rule: ∫x−3/2 dx = x−1/2/(−1/2) = −2/√x.
∫₁^b x−3/2 dx = [−2/√x]₁^b = −2/√b + 2.
As b → ∞, 2/√b → 0, so the limit is 2.
p-rule check: p = 3/2, value 1/(p−1) = 1/(1/2) = 2 ✓. Even a power just barely above 1 converges — but to a bigger area (the closer to the border, the fatter the tail).
2 — convergent
10.exponential tail
Evaluate ∫₀^∞ e−x dx.

Worked solution

Background: from Lesson 17, the antiderivative of e−x is −e−x (check: derivative of −e−x is −e−x·(−1) = e−x ✓). Exponential decay is the fastest shrinker in our library — it crushes every power of x — so expect a clean convergence. This integral is the foundation of the "exponential distribution" used everywhere in probability.
∫₀^b e−x dx = [−e−x]₀^b = −e−b + e⁰ = 1 − e−b.
As b → ∞, e−b → 0 (table fact), so the area heads to 1 − 0 = 1.
Marvel at it: the curve starts at height 1, trails off forever, and the entire infinite tail encloses exactly 1 square unit.
1 — convergent
11.exponential · u-sub skill
Evaluate ∫₀^∞ e−2x dx.

Worked solution

Background: Lesson 17 skill: ∫ekx dx = ekx/k. With k = −2, the antiderivative of e−2x is e−2x/(−2) = −½e−2x. Faster decay (the 2 in the exponent) should mean a smaller area than problem 10's answer of 1.
∫₀^b e−2x dx = [−½e−2x]₀^b = −½e−2b + ½.
As b → ∞, e−2b → 0, so the limit is ½.
Sense check: e−2x hugs zero sooner than e−x, so half the area is exactly right.
1/2 — convergent
12.constant multiple
Using your answer to problem 10 (no new integration needed!), evaluate ∫₀^∞ 2e−x dx.

Worked solution

Background: the constant-multiple property (Lesson 29) survives the trip to infinity: constants slide out of improper integrals just like ordinary ones, because they slide out of every ∫₀^b and out of the limit too.
∫₀^∞ 2e−x dx = 2 ∫₀^∞ e−x dx = 2 × 1.
Picture: the curve is twice as tall everywhere, so the (finite!) area doubles.
2 — convergent
13.p-rule drill
For each integral, say whether it converges or diverges — and if it converges, give its value using the p-rule: (a) ∫₁^∞ 1/x⁵ dx   (b) ∫₁^∞ 1/x0.9 dx   (c) ∫₁^∞ 1/x dx   (d) ∫₁^∞ 1/x11 dx

Worked solution

Background: the p-rule turns a whole limit computation into a one-glance decision: ∫₁^∞ 1/x^p dx converges exactly when p > 1, with value 1/(p−1). The border p = 1 belongs to the divergent side.
(a) p = 5 > 1: converges to 1/(5−1) = 1/4.
(b) p = 0.9 ≤ 1: diverges (just barely fatter than the border — barely still counts).
(c) p = 1: the borderline itself — diverges (this is ln b → ∞).
(d) p = 11 > 1: converges to 1/(11−1) = 1/10.
(a) converges, 1/4   (b) diverges   (c) diverges   (d) converges, 1/10
14.divergent · different start
Does ∫₃^∞ 1/x dx converge? Compute the limit to justify your answer. (Does starting at 3 instead of 1 rescue it?)

Worked solution

Background: divergence lives in the TAIL, not at the start. Chopping off the beginning of the region removes only a finite chunk of area — if what remains is infinite, it stays infinite. Convergence or divergence never depends on where you start (only the value does).
∫₃^b 1/x dx = [ln x]₃^b = ln b − ln 3.
As b → ∞, ln b → ∞, and subtracting the fixed number ln 3 doesn't slow it down: ln b − ln 3 → ∞.
Diverges — moving the starting wall can't fix a fat tail
15.unbounded integrand
The function 1/√x blows up at x = 0, yet ∫₀¹ 1/√x dx is a finite number. Use a wall at x = a with a → 0⁺ to find it.

Worked solution

Background: the second improper flavor — finite interval, infinite SPIKE. Same strategy, aimed at the bad endpoint: integrate from a small a up to 1, then slide a down to 0 and watch the heading. Antiderivative: ∫x−1/2 dx = 2√x.
∫ₐ¹ 1/√x dx = [2√x]ₐ¹ = 2√1 − 2√a = 2 − 2√a.
As a → 0⁺, √a → 0 (table fact), so the area heads to 2 − 0 = 2.
Try it numerically: a = 0.01 gives 2 − 0.2 = 1.8; a = 0.0001 gives 2 − 0.02 = 1.98. Heading: 2.
2 — an infinitely tall spike enclosing area exactly 2
16.unbounded integrand
Evaluate ∫₀⁴ 1/√x dx.

Worked solution

Background: same spike at 0 as the last problem, wider window. The improper part is still only at the left end — from a to 4 everything is ordinary.
∫ₐ⁴ 1/√x dx = [2√x]ₐ⁴ = 2√4 − 2√a = 4 − 2√a.
As a → 0⁺, 2√a → 0, so the limit is 4.
Consistency check: area from 0 to 1 is 2 (problem 15); area from 1 to 4 is [2√x]₁⁴ = 4 − 2 = 2; total 2 + 2 = 4 ✓.
4 — convergent
17.divergent spike
Show that ∫₀¹ 1/x² dx diverges. Then explain the strange flip: 1/x² beat 1/√x out at infinity, but near zero 1/√x wins. Why?

Worked solution

Background: convergence is a local contest: out at infinity you need a THIN TAIL; near a spike you need a THIN SPIKE. A function can win one contest and lose the other. Compare heights near x = 0.01: 1/√x = 10, but 1/x² = 10000 — the 1/x² spike is enormously fatter.
∫ₐ¹ 1/x² dx = [−1/x]ₐ¹ = −1 + 1/a.
As a → 0⁺, 1/a → ∞, so the area blows up. Divergent.
The flip: at infinity, bigger powers of x in the denominator mean FASTER shrinking (good). Near zero, bigger powers mean FASTER blowing up (bad). 1/x is the borderline in both contests — and loses both.
Diverges — the spike at 0 is too fat (1/a → ∞)
18.proper or improper?
For each integral, decide: ordinary (proper) definite integral, or improper? If improper, say exactly why. (a) ∫₁¹⁰ 1/x² dx   (b) ∫₀¹ 1/x dx   (c) ∫₁^∞ e−x dx   (d) ∫₋₁¹ 1/x² dx

Worked solution

Background: an integral is improper if (i) an endpoint is ∞, or (ii) the integrand blows up somewhere on the interval — INCLUDING in the middle, which is the sneaky case. Always scan the whole interval for places the function explodes before you integrate.
(a) Limits 1 to 10 are finite and 1/x² is perfectly tame there (it only misbehaves at 0). Proper — integrate normally.
(b) Finite interval, but 1/x → ∞ as x → 0⁺, and 0 is an endpoint. Improper (spike at the left endpoint) — and in fact divergent.
(c) Upper endpoint is ∞. Improper (infinite endpoint) — convergent, by the way, since e−x dies fast.
(d) Looks innocent... but 1/x² blows up at x = 0, which sits INSIDE [−1, 1]. Improper (hidden interior spike) — you must split it at 0, and each half diverges.
(a) proper   (b) improper: spike at 0   (c) improper: ∞ endpoint   (d) improper: spike hiding inside
19.spot the error
A student writes: "∫₀^∞ cos x dx = [sin x]₀^∞ = sin(∞) − sin(0) = 0 − 0 = 0." The final answer is wrong — but the deeper problem is HOW the student treated ∞. Find both problems.

Worked solution

Background: ∞ is not a number, so "sin(∞)" is meaningless — you can't take the sine of infinity. The ONLY legal move is the limit: compute [sin x]₀^b = sin b and ask where sin b heads as b → ∞. A limit exists only if the values settle toward one number.
Error 1 (the method): plugging ∞ in like a number. The correct setup is limb→∞ ∫₀^b cos x dx = limb→∞ sin b.
Error 2 (the conclusion): sin b never settles — it swings between −1 and 1 forever (sin of 10 is ≈ −0.54, of 100 ≈ −0.51, of 1000 ≈ 0.83, ...). There is no heading, so the limit does not exist.
Geometric picture: the areas above and below the axis keep alternating; the running total sloshes back and forth forever and never converges.
The integral diverges (the limit of sin b does not exist); "sin(∞)" was never a legal move
20.challenge · partial fractions
Evaluate ∫₁^∞ 1/(x(x+1)) dx. Hint: Lesson 32! Split with partial fractions first — and notice something remarkable: each piece alone would diverge, yet together they converge.

Worked solution

Background: partial fractions (Lesson 32): 1/(x(x+1)) = A/x + B/(x+1). Multiply out: 1 = A(x+1) + Bx. At x = 0: A = 1. At x = −1: 1 = −B, so B = −1. So the integrand is 1/x − 1/(x+1). Each piece integrates to a divergent ln... but subtraction tames them: for big x the two curves nearly cancel, leaving a thin convergent tail (roughly 1/x², since 1/x − 1/(x+1) = 1/(x(x+1))).
∫₁^b [1/x − 1/(x+1)] dx = [ln x − ln(x+1)]₁^b = [ln(x/(x+1))]₁^b (log rule: ln u − ln v = ln(u/v)).
= ln(b/(b+1)) − ln(1/2).
As b → ∞, b/(b+1) → 1 (try b = 1000: 1000/1001 ≈ 0.999), so ln(b/(b+1)) → ln 1 = 0.
Total: 0 − ln(1/2) = −(−ln 2) = ln 2. (≈ 0.693 — a perfectly finite area from two "infinite" ingredients!)
ln 2 — convergent
