Lesson 19 — Initial Values & Net Change

The +C Finally Earns Its Keep · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 19 of 40

📚 All lessons← Prev: Lesson 18Next: Lesson 20 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: for eighteen lessons you've dutifully written "+ C" and it never seemed to matter. Today it does. You'll learn how ONE piece of extra information (a starting value) turns an antiderivative family into one specific function — and meet the net change theorem, the formula for predicting the future from a rate: later amount = starting amount + accumulated change.

1 The +C finally earns its keep

  • When you integrate, you don't get one answer — you get a whole family: ∫ 2x dx = x² + C means x², x²+1, x²+4, x²−7... infinitely many functions, ALL with derivative 2x.
  • Picture the family as a stack of parallel curves — the same shape shifted up and down. They all have identical slopes at every x; they just start at different heights.
the family y = x² + C — same shape, different heights y 9 | · · ← x² + 4 7 | · · · · 5 | · · · · · ← x² + 1 ★ passes through (1, 5)? no... 4 | ······· ← x² + 4 bottoms out here 2 | · · · · 1 | ····· ← x² + 1 bottoms out here 0 +----------------------→ x 0 every curve has the SAME slope pattern (derivative 2x); knowing ONE point the curve passes through picks ONE curve.
  • So the family answers "what shape?" but not "which height?" To pin down the height you need one piece of information — one known point, usually a starting value like f(0) = 5. That single fact selects exactly one curve from the stack.

2 Initial value problems — the recipe

  • A problem that gives you a derivative PLUS one known value is called an initial value problem. The recipe:
The recipe:
StepWhat you do
1Integrate the derivative to get the whole family (don't forget + C)
2Plug the known point into the family
3Solve for C
4Write the specific function, with the found C in place
Worked example: f'(x) = 2x and f(1) = 5. Find f(x).
Step 1: f(x) = x² + C (the family).
Step 2: plug in x = 1: f(1) = 1² + C = 1 + C, and we're told this equals 5.
Step 3: 1 + C = 5 → C = 4.
Step 4: f(x) = x² + 4.
Check: derivative of x²+4 is 2x ✓, and f(1) = 1 + 4 = 5 ✓. Both given facts confirmed.

3 Real costumes — the same problem in four disguises

  • Initial value problems love to dress up as stories. In every costume, the plot is identical: a rate + a starting amount → the full amount-function.
Position from velocity: a scooter moves at v(t) = 4t m/s and starts at position s(0) = 6 m. Find s(t).
Family: s(t) = 2t² + C. Plug in t = 0: s(0) = 0 + C = 6, so C = 6.
s(t) = 2t² + 6. (Check: derivative 4t ✓, s(0) = 6 ✓.)
Tank from flow rate: a tank holds 12 L and fills at r(t) = 3 L/min. Amount A(t)?
Family: A(t) = 3t + C. Plug in t = 0: C = 12. A(t) = 3t + 12. After 4 minutes: A(4) = 24 L.
Temperature from cooling rate: cocoa starts at 80°C and cools at T'(t) = −2 °C/min. Family: T(t) = −2t + C; T(0) = 80 gives C = 80, so T(t) = 80 − 2t. After 10 minutes: 60°C. (A negative rate means the amount is going DOWN — the integral handles that automatically.)
Money from earning rate: you have $25 and earn at e(t) = 8 dollars/hour. Balance: M(t) = 8t + 25. After 3 hours: $49. The C is literally the money you started with.
  • Notice: in every costume, C turned out to be the starting amount. That's the pattern to remember — when the known value is at t = 0, C IS the initial value.

4 The NET CHANGE theorem — predicting the future from a rate

  • Here's the same idea one more way, and it's a famous one. The FTC (Lesson 7) says ∫ab F'(t) dt = F(b) − F(a) — "the integral of the rate equals the total change."
  • Read that equation in reverse — solve it for F(b):
THE NET CHANGE THEOREM F(b) = F(a) + ∫ₐᵇ F'(t) dt later starting accumulated amount = amount + change "where you end up = where you started + everything that happened in between"
  • Said humanly: later amount = starting amount + accumulated change. It's how you predict the future from a rate: know where things stand now, integrate the rate over the time in between, add.
  • Your bank does this every month: new balance = old balance + (all deposits and withdrawals in between). Your odometer, a reservoir gauge, a thermometer — all net-change machines.

5 Worked example — the filling tank

Problem: a tank holds 30 L at t = 0 and fills at r(t) = 4 + 2t L/min. How much is in the tank at t = 5?

Net change setup: amount at 5 = amount at 0 + ∫₀⁵ r(t) dt.
Integrate the rate: ∫₀⁵ (4 + 2t) dt = [4t + t²]₀⁵ = (20 + 25) − 0 = 45.
(Check the antiderivative: derivative of 4t + t² is 4 + 2t ✓.)
So: amount at 5 = 30 + 45 = 75 L.

Sanity check: the rate starts at 4 L/min and grows to 14 L/min by t = 5, so the average rate is around 9 L/min → about 45 L added in 5 minutes. ✓ Matches.

6 "Find C" vs "start + integral" — same idea, two outfits

  • You now own two ways to solve the same problem. Watch them agree:
Problem: velocity v(t) = 2t, starting position s(0) = 3. Where is the object at t = 4?

Outfit 1 — find C: family s(t) = t² + C; s(0) = 3 gives C = 3, so s(t) = t² + 3. Then s(4) = 16 + 3 = 19.

Outfit 2 — start + integral: s(4) = s(0) + ∫₀⁴ 2t dt = 3 + [t²]₀⁴ = 3 + 16 = 19.

Same answer: 19. Outfit 1 builds the whole function first (great when you'll need s at many times, or need to know WHEN something happens). Outfit 2 jumps straight to one moment (great when you only need one number). Choose whichever fits the question.

1.find C
Given f'(x) = 3x² and f(0) = 2, find f(x).

Worked solution

Background: integrating a derivative gives a FAMILY of answers (+ C). One known value — here f(0) = 2 — picks one member of the family. Recipe: integrate, plug in the known point, solve for C, write the specific function.
Integrate: f(x) = x³ + C (since the derivative of x³ is 3x²).
Plug in x = 0: f(0) = 0 + C = C, and we're told f(0) = 2, so C = 2.
Check: derivative of x³ + 2 is 3x² ✓ and f(0) = 2 ✓.
f(x) = x³ + 2
2.find C
Given f'(x) = 2x + 1 and f(2) = 10, find f(x).

Worked solution

Background: the known point doesn't have to be at x = 0! Plug in whatever x-value you're given — the algebra to find C is one small equation either way.
Integrate: f(x) = x² + x + C. (Check: derivative is 2x + 1 ✓.)
Plug in x = 2: f(2) = 4 + 2 + C = 6 + C. Set equal to 10: 6 + C = 10 → C = 4.
Check: f(2) = 4 + 2 + 4 = 10 ✓.
f(x) = x² + x + 4
3.find C
Given f'(x) = 4 and f(1) = 7, find f(x).

Worked solution

Background: a constant derivative means f is a straight line with slope 4. Finding C here is just finding where that line sits — the line through the point (1, 7) with slope 4.
Integrate: f(x) = 4x + C.
Plug in x = 1: 4 + C = 7 → C = 3.
Check: derivative of 4x + 3 is 4 ✓, f(1) = 7 ✓.
f(x) = 4x + 3
4.find C
Given f'(x) = 2x and f(3) = 11, find f(x).

Worked solution

Background: same family as the lesson's picture — x² + C, the stack of parallel parabolas. The point (3, 11) selects exactly one of them.
Integrate: f(x) = x² + C.
Plug in x = 3: 9 + C = 11 → C = 2.
Check: f(3) = 9 + 2 = 11 ✓.
f(x) = x² + 2
5.velocity → position
A robot moves at a steady v(t) = 10 meters per second and starts at position s(0) = 3 meters. Find s(t), then find where the robot is at t = 4 seconds.

Worked solution

Background: velocity is the derivative of position (Lesson 11). So position = antiderivative of velocity, and the starting position s(0) is the known value that pins down C.
Integrate: s(t) = 10t + C.
Plug in t = 0: s(0) = C = 3. So s(t) = 10t + 3.
At t = 4: s(4) = 40 + 3 = 43 meters.
Sanity check: 10 m/s for 4 s covers 40 m; starting from 3 m lands at 43 m ✓.
s(t) = 10t + 3; the robot is at 43 meters
6.find C
Given f'(x) = 6x² − 2 and f(1) = 5, find f(x).

Worked solution

Background: integrate term by term (Lesson 5): the antiderivative of 6x² is 2x³ and the antiderivative of −2 is −2x. One + C covers the whole thing — never write two C's.
Integrate: f(x) = 2x³ − 2x + C. (Check: derivative is 6x² − 2 ✓.)
Plug in x = 1: 2 − 2 + C = C. Set equal to 5: C = 5.
Check: f(1) = 2 − 2 + 5 = 5 ✓.
f(x) = 2x³ − 2x + 5
7.velocity → position
A cart has velocity v(t) = 2t + 3 m/s and starts at s(0) = 5 m. Find s(t), then find s(2).

Worked solution

Background: two-step problems like this are the standard shape: first build the position function (find C), then answer a question with it (evaluate). Build once, use many times.
Integrate: s(t) = t² + 3t + C. (Check: derivative is 2t + 3 ✓.)
s(0) = C = 5, so s(t) = t² + 3t + 5.
At t = 2: s(2) = 4 + 6 + 5 = 15 meters.
s(t) = t² + 3t + 5; s(2) = 15 meters
8.known point not at 0
A drone's velocity is v(t) = 6t² m/s, and at time t = 1 its position is s(1) = 4 m. Find s(t), then find s(2).

Worked solution

Background: the known value is at t = 1, not t = 0 — so C is NOT simply the given number. You must plug in t = 1 and actually solve the little equation.
Integrate: s(t) = 2t³ + C. (Check: derivative is 6t² ✓.)
Plug in t = 1: 2(1) + C = 2 + C = 4 → C = 2. So s(t) = 2t³ + 2.
At t = 2: s(2) = 2(8) + 2 = 18 meters.
s(t) = 2t³ + 2; s(2) = 18 meters
9.tank story
A tank holds 20 liters at t = 0 and fills at a rate of r(t) = 3 + 2t liters per minute. How much water is in the tank at t = 4 minutes?

Worked solution

Background: net change theorem: later amount = starting amount + integral of the rate. The 20 L is the starting amount; the integral of r(t) from 0 to 4 is everything that flowed in.
Accumulated change: ∫₀⁴ (3 + 2t) dt = [3t + t²]₀⁴ = (12 + 16) − 0 = 28.
(Check the antiderivative: derivative of 3t + t² is 3 + 2t ✓.)
Amount at t = 4: 20 + 28 = 48 liters.
48 liters
10.money story
You start the week with $50. Your little business earns money at a rate of e(t) = 10 + 2t dollars per day, where t is in days. How much money do you have after 5 days?

Worked solution

Background: same net-change pattern with a money costume: balance later = balance now + integral of the earning rate. The rate 10 + 2t means the business earns faster each day ($10/day at the start, $20/day by day 5).
Accumulated earnings: ∫₀⁵ (10 + 2t) dt = [10t + t²]₀⁵ = 50 + 25 = 75.
(Check: derivative of 10t + t² is 10 + 2t ✓.)
Balance: 50 + 75 = 125 dollars.
$125
11.temperature story
A cup of tea is 90°C at t = 0 and cools at a rate of T'(t) = −2t degrees per minute (cooling faster as time passes). What is its temperature at t = 5 minutes?

Worked solution

Background: a NEGATIVE rate means the amount is decreasing (Lesson 9's signed-area idea). The net change theorem doesn't care about the sign — the integral of a negative rate comes out negative, and adding a negative change lowers the total. That's cooling.
Accumulated change: ∫₀⁵ (−2t) dt = [−t²]₀⁵ = −25.
(Check: derivative of −t² is −2t ✓.)
Temperature: 90 + (−25) = 65°C.
65°C
12.in-then-out tank
A tank holds 40 liters at t = 0. Its NET flow rate is r(t) = 6 − 2t liters per minute (positive = filling, negative = draining). How much water is in the tank at t = 5? What happened partway through?

Worked solution

Background: the rate 6 − 2t starts positive (filling) and turns negative after t = 3 (draining). The net change theorem handles both phases in one integral, because signed area adds the gains and subtracts the losses automatically.
Accumulated net change: ∫₀⁵ (6 − 2t) dt = [6t − t²]₀⁵ = 30 − 25 = 5.
(Check: derivative of 6t − t² is 6 − 2t ✓.)
Amount: 40 + 5 = 45 liters.
The story: the tank filled from t = 0 to t = 3 (rate positive), then drained from t = 3 to t = 5 (rate negative) — but the filling outweighed the draining, so the net change was still +5 L.
45 liters — it filled for 3 minutes, then drained, netting +5 L
13.verify consistency
A friend solved an initial value problem with f'(x) = 3x² and f(1) = 5, and got f(x) = x³ + 4. Verify BOTH given facts to decide if the answer is right.

Worked solution

Background: an initial value problem gives two facts — a derivative and a point — and a correct answer must satisfy BOTH. Checking is quick: differentiate once, plug in once. Do both checks; an answer can pass one and fail the other.
Fact 1 (derivative): d/dx of x³ + 4 = 3x² ✓ — matches f'(x).
Fact 2 (point): f(1) = 1 + 4 = 5 ✓ — matches f(1) = 5.
Both facts check out.
Yes — the answer is correct: derivative ✓ and point ✓
14.two outfits
Velocity v(t) = 4t m/s, starting position s(0) = 6 m. Find s(3) TWO ways: (a) build s(t) by finding C, then evaluate; (b) use start + integral directly. Confirm they agree.

Worked solution

Background: "find C" and "start + integral" are the same idea in different outfits. Outfit (a) builds the whole function — useful for many questions. Outfit (b) jumps to one number — faster for a single question. They must always agree.
(a) Family: s(t) = 2t² + C; s(0) = C = 6 → s(t) = 2t² + 6. Then s(3) = 18 + 6 = 24.
(b) s(3) = s(0) + ∫₀³ 4t dt = 6 + [2t²]₀³ = 6 + 18 = 24.
(Check the antiderivative both times: derivative of 2t² is 4t ✓.)
Both ways give s(3) = 24 meters ✓
15.what does C mean?
In the story "a car's velocity is v(t) = 3t² and its position is s(t) = t³ + C," what does the C mean in plain language? Why can't the velocity alone tell you C?

Worked solution

Background: this is a words question, and it's the heart of the lesson. The derivative describes how something CHANGES; it says nothing about where things STOOD at the beginning. Two cars driving identically can be in different cities.
C is the car's starting position — where it was at t = 0 (plug in t = 0: s(0) = 0 + C = C).
The velocity only records movement. A car starting at mile 0 and a car starting at mile 100, both following v(t) = 3t², move exactly the same way — same speed at every moment — but their positions differ by 100 forever.
That's why every antiderivative carries a +C, and why one extra fact (a starting value) is exactly enough to pin it down.
C = the starting position s(0); velocity describes only change, so it can never reveal where you began
16.e-flavored
Given f'(x) = ex and f(0) = 3, find f(x).

Worked solution

Background: from Lesson 15, ex is its own antiderivative: ∫ ex dx = ex + C. One caution when plugging in x = 0: e0 = 1, not 0! Anything to the power 0 is 1.
Integrate: f(x) = ex + C.
Plug in x = 0: f(0) = e⁰ + C = 1 + C. Set equal to 3: 1 + C = 3 → C = 2.
Check: derivative of ex + 2 is ex ✓, and f(0) = 1 + 2 = 3 ✓.
f(x) = ex + 2
17.reach the target
A tank holds 10 liters at t = 0 and fills at r(t) = 2t + 2 liters per minute. (a) Find the amount function A(t). (b) At what time does the tank reach exactly 25 liters?

Worked solution

Background: a two-stage problem: first build the function (initial value problem), then set it equal to a target and solve for t — algebra from your regular math class (factoring a quadratic). This "find WHEN" question is exactly why building the whole function beats computing one integral.
(a) Family: A(t) = t² + 2t + C; A(0) = C = 10 → A(t) = t² + 2t + 10. (Check: derivative is 2t + 2 ✓.)
(b) Set A(t) = 25: t² + 2t + 10 = 25 → t² + 2t − 15 = 0.
Factor: (t + 5)(t − 3) = 0 → t = −5 or t = 3. Time can't be negative, so t = 3.
Check: A(3) = 9 + 6 + 10 = 25 ✓.
(a) A(t) = t² + 2t + 10   (b) at t = 3 minutes
18.trig-flavored
Given f'(x) = cos x and f(0) = 5, find f(x), then find f(π/2).

Worked solution

Background: from Lesson 16, ∫ cos x dx = sin x + C (because the derivative of sin x is cos x). Useful values: sin 0 = 0 and sin(π/2) = 1.
Integrate: f(x) = sin x + C.
Plug in x = 0: f(0) = sin 0 + C = 0 + C = 5 → C = 5. So f(x) = sin x + 5.
At x = π/2: f(π/2) = sin(π/2) + 5 = 1 + 5 = 6.
Check: derivative of sin x + 5 is cos x ✓, f(0) = 5 ✓.
f(x) = sin x + 5; f(π/2) = 6
19.population · net change
A town has 2000 people at year t = 0 and grows at a rate of p'(t) = 100 + 20t people per year. Use the net change theorem to predict the population at year t = 3.

Worked solution

Background: the net change theorem in its cleanest form: P(3) = P(0) + ∫₀³ p'(t) dt — "future population = current population + all the growth in between." This is genuinely how forecasts work: measure the rate, integrate it, add to today's value.
Growth: ∫₀³ (100 + 20t) dt = [100t + 10t²]₀³ = 300 + 90 = 390.
(Check: derivative of 100t + 10t² is 100 + 20t ✓.)
Population: 2000 + 390 = 2390 people.
2390 people
20.challenge · turning point
An object moves along a straight track with velocity v(t) = 6t − 12 m/s, starting at position s(0) = 9 m (position measured from "home" at 0). (a) Find s(t). (b) The object slows, stops for an instant, and turns around — when, and where is it at that moment? (c) Its position there is negative — what does that mean? (d) Bonus: when does the object pass exactly through home?

Worked solution

Background: the object turns around at the instant its velocity is ZERO (moving backward = negative v, forward = positive v; the switch happens at v = 0). And position can be negative: it just means the object is on the OTHER side of home — like a number line, home at 0, negative positions to the left.
(a) Integrate: s(t) = 3t² − 12t + C; s(0) = C = 9 → s(t) = 3t² − 12t + 9. (Check: derivative is 6t − 12 ✓, s(0) = 9 ✓.)
(b) Set v = 0: 6t − 12 = 0 → t = 2. Position there: s(2) = 3(4) − 24 + 9 = 12 − 24 + 9 = −3 meters. This is the lowest position the object ever reaches (its position graph is an upward parabola with its vertex at t = 2).
(c) s = −3 means the object is 3 meters PAST home, on the far side — it started 9 m from home on the positive side, drove through home, overshot by 3 m, and turned around there.
(d) Set s(t) = 0: 3t² − 12t + 9 = 0 → divide by 3: t² − 4t + 3 = 0 → (t − 1)(t − 3) = 0 → t = 1 and t = 3. It passes home twice: once heading out (t = 1) and once coming back (t = 3).
(a) s(t) = 3t² − 12t + 9   (b) turns at t = 2, at position −3 m   (c) it's 3 m beyond home on the other side   (d) passes home at t = 1 and t = 3
