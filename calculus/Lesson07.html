Lesson 7 — The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

The Miracle Shortcut · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 7 of 20

📚 All lessons← Prev: Lesson 6Next: Lesson 8 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: learn the single most important fact in this course — ∫ab f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a), where F is any antiderivative of f. It turns "add up infinitely many skinny rectangles" into "plug in two numbers and subtract." This is the payoff for everything you've built in Lessons 1–6.

1 The cliffhanger: our tools run out

  • Where we stand: geometry (Lesson 6) computes integrals EXACTLY... but only when the graph is made of straight lines — rectangles, triangles, trapezoids.
  • For a curve like x², straight-edge shapes don't fit. Lesson 3's rectangle-slicing works on curves — but it never quite finishes: skinnier slices, better estimates, forever. Endless arithmetic for an answer that's still approximate.
  • So here's the situation: exact but only for straight lines, or works-on-curves but never exact. We want both. Today we get both.
  • There is a miracle shortcut — so central that it's called the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus. And you already own every piece needed to understand it.

2 The idea in a story: the odometer

  • Think about a car's two dashboard displays. The speedometer shows the rate right now. The odometer shows total miles ever driven — it accumulates the speed, mile by mile.
  • Question: how far did you drive between time a and time b? Easy — read the odometer twice and subtract: distance = (odometer at b) − (odometer at a). No adding-up needed; the odometer already did all the adding for you, continuously, as you drove.
  • Now the key connection. What IS the odometer, mathematically? It's the function whose rate of change is your speed — the function whose derivative is the speedometer reading. From Lessons 4–5 you know what that's called: the odometer is an ANTIDERIVATIVE of the speed!
  • Put the pieces together: the total accumulated from a to b (that's the integral!) equals an antiderivative read at b, minus the same antiderivative read at a.

3 The theorem, and the three-step recipe

  • Here it is — the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus:
  • ∫ab f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)
  • ...where F is ANY antiderivative of f (any function whose derivative is f).
  • Read it slowly: the exact area under the rate graph f, from a to b, is found by taking an accumulator function F and subtracting its start value from its end value. The infinitely-many-rectangles sum collapses into ONE subtraction.
  • The recipe, every single time:
    • Step 1: find an antiderivative F of the integrand (Lessons 4–5: power rule backwards).
    • Step 2: plug in the upper limit → F(b). Plug in the lower limit → F(a).
    • Step 3: subtract: F(b) − F(a). Done.
Tiny first example: ∫03 2x dx. Step 1: an antiderivative of 2x is F(x) = x² (check: derivative of x² is 2x ✓). Step 2: F(3) = 9, F(0) = 0. Step 3: 9 − 0 = 9. Geometry agrees: triangle, base 3, height 6, area ½ × 3 × 6 = 9 ✓.

4 Why the +C doesn't matter here

  • Lessons 4–5 drilled into you: antiderivatives come in families, always write + C. So which C do we use in the theorem? Watch what happens if we keep it:
  • (F(b) + C) − (F(a) + C) = F(b) − F(a) + C − C = F(b) − F(a)
  • The C's cancel — whatever C you pick, it gets added at b and subtracted right back at a. Every member of the antiderivative family gives the exact same answer.
  • Odometer version: it doesn't matter what your odometer read when the car left the factory — the DIFFERENCE between two readings is the same regardless.
  • Practical rule: for definite integrals, you may drop the +C. (For indefinite antiderivative questions like Lessons 4–5, keep writing it!)

5 Worked examples — with truth-checks

Example 1 (check against geometry): ∫04 3x dx. Step 1: F(x) = 3x²/2 (check by differentiating: derivative of 3x²/2 is 3x ✓). Step 2: F(4) = 3 × 16 / 2 = 24, and F(0) = 0. Step 3: 24 − 0 = 24. Now the geometry: the region is a triangle with base 4 and height 3 × 4 = 12, so area = ½ × 4 × 12 = 24 ✓. The shortcut agrees with the truth. That's why we did Lesson 6 first — so you can catch the theorem telling the truth.
Example 2 (a real curve — geometry can't do this one): ∫02 x² dx. Step 1: F(x) = x³/3 (check: derivative of x³/3 is 3x²/3 = x² ✓). Step 2: F(2) = 8/3, F(0) = 0. Step 3: 8/3 − 0 = 8/3 ≈ 2.67. Remember Lesson 3? Your rectangle sandwich squeezed this exact area between 1.75 and 3.75, and tightened as the slices got skinnier. The number it was closing in on — the answer to all that slicing — is exactly 8/3. Three lines of algebra just replaced infinite rectangle-counting. That's the miracle.

6 Handy notation: the evaluation bracket

  • Writing "F(b) − F(a)" over and over gets clumsy, so there's a standard shorthand: square brackets with the limits attached, [F(x)]ab, which means "plug the top number into F, plug the bottom number in, subtract."
  • Example written the professional way: ∫02 x² dx = [x³/3]02 = 8/3 − 0 = 8/3.
  • The bracket is a to-do note: "antiderivative found — evaluation still to do." You'll see it in every calculus book ever printed, so let's make friends with it now.

1.FTC · constant
Use the Fundamental Theorem (all three steps) to evaluate ∫03 2 dx. Then check your answer with a rectangle.

Worked solution

Background: the FTC recipe: (1) find F, an antiderivative of the integrand (a function whose derivative is the integrand); (2) plug in the top limit and the bottom limit; (3) subtract, top minus bottom. An antiderivative of the constant 2 is 2x, since the derivative of 2x is 2.
Step 1: F(x) = 2x.
Step 2: F(3) = 6, F(0) = 0.
Step 3: 6 − 0 = 6.
Geometry check: rectangle, width 3, height 2 → 2 × 3 = 6 ✓.
∫03 2 dx = 6
2.FTC · constant
Evaluate ∫15 4 dx with the FTC, then check with a rectangle.

Worked solution

Background: this time the lower limit isn't 0, so F(a) won't be 0 — don't skip the subtraction! F(x) = 4x works because the derivative of 4x is 4.
Step 1: F(x) = 4x.
Step 2: F(5) = 20, F(1) = 4.
Step 3: 20 − 4 = 16.
Geometry check: rectangle, width 5 − 1 = 4, height 4 → 16 ✓.

3.FTC · line
Evaluate ∫04 x dx with the FTC, then check with a triangle.

Worked solution

Background: the power rule backwards (Lesson 5): to antidifferentiate xn, raise the power by one and divide by the new power. So an antiderivative of x (which is x¹) is x²/2 — check: the derivative of x²/2 is 2x/2 = x ✓.
Step 1: F(x) = x²/2.
Step 2: F(4) = 16/2 = 8, F(0) = 0.
Step 3: 8 − 0 = 8.
Geometry check: triangle, base 4, height 4 → ½ × 4 × 4 = 8 ✓.
∫04 x dx = 8
4.FTC · line
Evaluate ∫02 6x dx with the FTC, then check with a triangle.

Worked solution

Background: constants ride along: an antiderivative of 6x is 6 × (x²/2) = 3x². Always verify by differentiating: derivative of 3x² is 6x ✓. This differentiate-to-check habit catches almost every mistake.
Step 1: F(x) = 3x².
Step 2: F(2) = 3 × 4 = 12, F(0) = 0.
Step 3: 12 − 0 = 12.
Geometry check: triangle, base 2, height 6 × 2 = 12 → ½ × 2 × 12 = 12 ✓.
∫02 6x dx = 12
5.FTC · first curve
Evaluate ∫01 x² dx — an exact area under a curve, something geometry alone could never give you.

Worked solution

Background: power rule backwards: an antiderivative of x² is x³/3 (check: derivative of x³/3 is 3x²/3 = x² ✓). No triangle or trapezoid fits under a curve — but the FTC doesn't care whether the graph is straight or curvy.
Step 1: F(x) = x³/3.
Step 2: F(1) = 1/3, F(0) = 0.
Step 3: 1/3 − 0 = 1/3.
Sanity check: the region fits inside the 1 × 1 square and clearly fills less than half of it — 1/3 ≈ 0.33 is believable ✓.
∫01 x² dx = 1/3
6.concept · why C cancels
Your friend says: "You told me antiderivatives always need a +C. So isn't the answer to every definite integral unknown, since we don't know C?" Explain, with one line of algebra, why the friend can relax.

Worked solution

Background: an antiderivative family is F(x) + C for every possible constant C — infinitely many functions, all with the same derivative. The FTC says to compute F(b) − F(a). The question is whether different C's give different answers.
Keep the C and compute: (F(b) + C) − (F(a) + C) = F(b) − F(a) + C − C = F(b) − F(a).
The C is added at b and subtracted right back at a — it cancels no matter what it is. Every member of the family gives the same answer, so we may simply use the C = 0 member.
Odometer picture: the factory setting of the odometer doesn't change the difference between two readings.
The C's cancel in the subtraction: (F(b)+C) − (F(a)+C) = F(b) − F(a)
7.FTC · curve
Evaluate ∫13 x² dx. Give the exact fraction and a decimal.

Worked solution

Background: same antiderivative as before, F(x) = x³/3, but now the lower limit is 1, so F(1) is NOT zero. The most common FTC error in the world is forgetting to subtract F(a) — don't be that person.
Step 1: F(x) = x³/3 (check: derivative is x² ✓).
Step 2: F(3) = 27/3 = 9, F(1) = 1/3.
Step 3: 9 − 1/3 = 27/3 − 1/3 = 26/3.
∫13 x² dx = 26/3 ≈ 8.67

9.FTC · polynomial
Evaluate ∫02 (3x² + 2) dx.

Worked solution

Background: antidifferentiate a sum term by term (Lesson 5): 3x² becomes x³ (since the derivative of x³ is 3x²), and the constant 2 becomes 2x. So F(x) = x³ + 2x. Check: derivative of x³ + 2x is 3x² + 2 ✓.
Step 1: F(x) = x³ + 2x.
Step 2: F(2) = 8 + 4 = 12, F(0) = 0.
Step 3: 12 − 0 = 12.
∫02 (3x² + 2) dx = 12
10.FTC + geometry check
Evaluate ∫14 2x dx with the FTC, then verify with the trapezoid method from Lesson 6.

Worked solution

Background: straight-line integrands are our testing lab: geometry gives the certified-true answer, so we can confirm the FTC is honest. An antiderivative of 2x is x² (derivative of x² is 2x ✓).
Step 1: F(x) = x².
Step 2: F(4) = 16, F(1) = 1.
Step 3: 16 − 1 = 15.
Geometry check: heights at the ends are 2(1) = 2 and 2(4) = 8; trapezoid = ½(2 + 8) × 3 = 15 ✓. Same answer, both roads.

11.find the mistake
A student evaluates ∫25 2x dx like this: "Antiderivative of 2x is x². Plug in 5: x² = 25. Answer: 25." Find the mistake and fix it.

Worked solution

Background: the FTC has THREE steps, and the third one is a subtraction: F(b) − F(a). The odometer story explains why: reading the odometer only at the end tells you the car's lifetime mileage, not the length of THIS trip — you must subtract the starting reading.
The student found F correctly and computed F(5) = 25 correctly — but never subtracted F(2).
F(2) = 4, so the true answer is 25 − 4 = 21.
Geometry confirms: trapezoid with heights 4 and 10, width 3 → ½(4 + 10) × 3 = 21 ✓ (and 25 is wrong).
Forgot to subtract F(2) = 4. Correct answer: 21
12.word problem
Water flows into a tank at the rate r(t) = 6t liters per minute (the flow speeds up over time). Using the FTC, find the total water added from t = 1 to t = 3 minutes.

Worked solution

Background: total accumulated from a rate = a definite integral (Lesson 6), and the FTC computes it: total = ∫13 6t dt = F(3) − F(1). An antiderivative of 6t is 3t² (check: derivative of 3t² is 6t ✓). The antiderivative F plays the role of a "water odometer" — total liters ever delivered.
Step 1: F(t) = 3t².
Step 2: F(3) = 3 × 9 = 27, F(1) = 3 × 1 = 3.
Step 3: 27 − 3 = 24. Units: (liters/minute) × minutes = liters.
24 liters

15.FTC + geometry check
Evaluate ∫05 (4x + 3) dx with the FTC, then verify with a trapezoid.

Worked solution

Background: term by term: 4x antidifferentiates to 2x² (derivative: 4x ✓), and 3 antidifferentiates to 3x. F(x) = 2x² + 3x. Because the integrand is a straight line, geometry can double-check us: heights ½(start + end) × width.
Step 1: F(x) = 2x² + 3x.
Step 2: F(5) = 2 × 25 + 15 = 50 + 15 = 65, F(0) = 0.
Step 3: 65 − 0 = 65.
Geometry check: at x = 0 height = 3; at x = 5 height = 23. Trapezoid = ½(3 + 23) × 5 = 13 × 5 = 65 ✓.
∫05 (4x + 3) dx = 65
16.FTC · polynomial
Evaluate ∫12 (6x² − 2x) dx.

Worked solution

Background: subtraction works term by term just like addition. 6x² antidifferentiates to 2x³ (derivative: 6x² ✓); 2x antidifferentiates to x². So F(x) = 2x³ − x². Always differentiate to confirm: derivative of 2x³ − x² is 6x² − 2x ✓.
Step 1: F(x) = 2x³ − x².
Step 2: F(2) = 2 × 8 − 4 = 16 − 4 = 12; F(1) = 2 − 1 = 1.
Step 3: 12 − 1 = 11.
∫12 (6x² − 2x) dx = 11

18.FTC · bigger powers
Evaluate ∫04 (x³ − x) dx.

Worked solution

Background: term by term with the power rule backwards: x³ → x⁴/4, and x → x²/2. F(x) = x⁴/4 − x²/2. Check: derivative of x⁴/4 − x²/2 is x³ − x ✓. With bigger powers, keep the arithmetic tidy — compute each term separately before combining.
Step 1: F(x) = x⁴/4 − x²/2.
Step 2: F(4) = 256/4 − 16/2 = 64 − 8 = 56; F(0) = 0.
Step 3: 56 − 0 = 56.
∫04 (x³ − x) dx = 56
19.concept · any antiderivative
Ana uses F(x) = x² and Ben uses G(x) = x² + 7 to evaluate ∫13 2x dx. Work out BOTH computations and show they agree. What does this demonstrate?

Worked solution

Background: x² and x² + 7 are both antiderivatives of 2x (both have derivative 2x — the +7 dies when you differentiate). The theorem promises ANY antiderivative works. Let's catch it keeping that promise with actual numbers.
Ana: F(3) − F(1) = 9 − 1 = 8.
Ben: G(3) − G(1) = (9 + 7) − (1 + 7) = 16 − 8 = 8.
Identical! Ben's +7 appeared in both readings and vanished in the subtraction — a live demonstration of why the +C never matters for definite integrals.
Both get 8 — any antiderivative gives the same F(b) − F(a)
20.challenge
Evaluate ∫14 (x² − 2x + 5) dx. Work cleanly — three terms, two plug-ins, one subtraction.

Worked solution

Background: everything from this lesson in one problem. Antidifferentiate term by term: x² → x³/3, −2x → −x², 5 → 5x. So F(x) = x³/3 − x² + 5x. Check by differentiating: x² − 2x + 5 ✓. Expect fractions — keep everything over 3 until the end.
Step 1: F(x) = x³/3 − x² + 5x.
Step 2: F(4) = 64/3 − 16 + 20 = 64/3 + 4 = 64/3 + 12/3 = 76/3.
F(1) = 1/3 − 1 + 5 = 1/3 + 4 = 1/3 + 12/3 = 13/3.
Step 3: 76/3 − 13/3 = 63/3 = 21.
∫14 (x² − 2x + 5) dx = 21
