🎯 Goal of this lesson: take the rectangle-slicing you invented back in Lesson 3 and dress it in its official uniform: sigma notation and Riemann sums. You'll also meet the midpoint sum (usually the most accurate), handle tables with unequal spacing, and learn to JUSTIFY whether an estimate is too big or too small — the exact skills AP exams test again and again.

1 Back to Lesson 3 — but official this time

  • Remember Lesson 3? To find the area under a curve, you covered it with skinny rectangles, added their areas, and noticed the estimate improved as the rectangles got skinnier. You've been using that idea ever since.
  • Today we give that idea its professional name and notation. It's called a Riemann sum (after Bernhard Riemann, who pinned the idea down in the 1850s), and the adding-up gets written with the symbol Σ.
  • Why bother, if we already have the FTC to compute areas exactly? Two reasons: (1) AP exams use this notation constantly, especially for functions given as TABLES of data, where there's no formula to antidifferentiate; (2) the notation IS the definition — it says precisely what a definite integral means.

2 Sigma (Σ) from scratch: a machine for adding

  • Σ is the Greek capital letter sigma — the Greek "S", for Sum. It's an instruction: "add up copies of the expression that follows."
  • Three pieces of information ride along with it: the index variable (a counter, often k), its starting value (written below the Σ), and its ending value (written above).
Reading one aloud: Σk=14 k says: "let k run 1, 2, 3, 4; write down the expression (just k) for each; add them all."
Σk=14 k = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 10.
With a formula inside: Σk=13 k²: plug k = 1, 2, 3 into k² and add:
1² + 2² + 3² = 1 + 4 + 9 = 14.
  • Writing sums IN sigma notation is the reverse skill: spot the pattern, express it with k. Example: 2 + 4 + 6 + 8 is "2k for k from 1 to 4," so it's Σk=14 2k.
  • The index letter is a private counter — Σk=14 k and Σi=14 i mean exactly the same thing. And the start needn't be 1: Σk=35 k = 3 + 4 + 5 = 12.

3 The formal Riemann sum — left, right, and (new!) midpoint

  • To estimate ∫ab f(x) dx with n rectangles: slice [a, b] into n equal pieces, each of width Δx = (b − a)/n. (Δ is Greek capital delta — "a chunk of." Δx = a chunk of x.)
  • Every rectangle's area is height × Δx. The only question is where you measure the height on each slice:
    • Left sum — height at each slice's left edge.
    • Right sum — height at each slice's right edge.
    • Midpoint sum (new today!) — height at each slice's exact middle. Usually the most accurate of the three, because on each slice the function is typically a bit above the midpoint height on one side and a bit below on the other — the errors partly cancel.
Worked example: f(x) = x² on [0, 2] with n = 4, so Δx = (2−0)/4 = 0.5. Slice edges: 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2. Midpoints: 0.25, 0.75, 1.25, 1.75.
Left (heights at 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5): 0.5(0 + 0.25 + 1 + 2.25) = 0.5(3.5) = 1.75 — familiar from Lesson 3!
Right (heights at 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2): 0.5(0.25 + 1 + 2.25 + 4) = 0.5(7.5) = 3.75.
Midpoint: 0.5(0.25² + 0.75² + 1.25² + 1.75²) = 0.5(0.0625 + 0.5625 + 1.5625 + 3.0625) = 0.5(5.25) = 2.625.
True value (FTC): ∫02 x² dx = 8/3 ≈ 2.667. The midpoint sum (2.625) is FAR closer than left (1.75) or right (3.75).

  • In sigma notation, with xk meaning the k-th sample point: Riemann sum = Σ f(xk) · Δx — literally "add up height × width for every slice." The notation just compresses what you've done since Lesson 3.

4 The definition: the integral IS the limit of Riemann sums

  • Watch what happens as n grows: n = 4 gives 1.75 (left) … n = 100 gives ≈ 2.647 … n = 1000 gives ≈ 2.665 … creeping toward 8/3. The staircase hugs the curve tighter and tighter.
  • Mathematicians define the definite integral as exactly this destination: ∫ab f(x) dx is the limit of Σ f(xk)Δx as n → ∞ — the value the Riemann sums close in on as the slices become infinitely skinny.
  • Now the integral symbol itself makes sense (remember the Lesson 6 story?): ∫ is a stretched-out S — the Σ pushed to its limit — and dx is what Δx becomes: the "infinitely skinny width." The notation ∫ f(x) dx literally says "sum of height × skinny width."
  • So nothing is circular: Riemann sums aren't just an approximation TOOL, they're the very meaning of the integral. The FTC is the miracle shortcut for computing that limit without ever building a staircase.

5 AP's favorite: Riemann sums from tables (unequal widths!)

  • Real data comes as tables, not formulas — and the measurement times are often unevenly spaced. No formula means no FTC: a Riemann sum is the ONLY way, which is why AP loves this question.
  • Key move: each rectangle's width is the gap between consecutive t-values — compute each gap separately, no shared Δx.
Worked example: a car's velocity is measured five times:
t (seconds)025610
v(t) (m/s)38645
Estimate the distance traveled, ∫010 v(t) dt, with a left Riemann sum using all four subintervals.
Widths: 2−0 = 2, 5−2 = 3, 6−5 = 1, 10−6 = 4. Left heights: the value at each subinterval's LEFT end: 3, 8, 6, 4 (the last value, 5, goes unused — a left sum never uses the final entry).
Left sum = 3·2 + 8·3 + 6·1 + 4·4 = 6 + 24 + 6 + 16 = 52 meters.
  • A right sum on the same table uses heights 8, 6, 4, 5 (skipping the first entry): 8·2 + 6·3 + 4·1 + 5·4 = 16 + 18 + 4 + 20 = 58 meters.
  • Bookkeeping tip: with 5 table entries you get 4 subintervals — always one fewer rectangle than data points.

6 Over or under? The justification AP wants

  • You noticed this pattern in Lesson 3 — now let's make the reasoning airtight, because AP graders award points for the justification, not just the word "over" or "under."
  • If f is increasing on the interval: on each slice the left edge is the LOWEST point, so left rectangles sit under the curve → left sum underestimates; the right edge is the HIGHEST point → right sum overestimates.
  • If f is decreasing, everything flips: the left edge is now the highest point on each slice → left overestimates, right underestimates.
f increasingf decreasing
Left sumunderestimateoverestimate
Right sumoverestimateunderestimate
  • A full-credit sentence sounds like: "Because v(t) is increasing on [0, 10], each left-endpoint height is the smallest value of v on its subinterval, so every rectangle lies below the curve and the left sum underestimates the integral." Name the behavior (increasing/decreasing), connect it to the rectangle heights, conclude.
  • If f goes up AND down on the interval, no blanket conclusion is allowed — say so.

1.read a sigma
Evaluate: Σk=15 k.

Worked solution

Background: Σ is an adding machine: the index k starts at the bottom value (1), counts up to the top value (5), and you add one copy of the expression (here just k) for each value.
k runs 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 → add: 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5.
1+2 = 3; 3+3 = 6; 6+4 = 10; 10+5 = 15.
15
2.read a sigma
Evaluate: Σk=14 k².

Worked solution

Background: when a formula follows the Σ, plug each index value INTO the formula before adding. Here the formula is k²: square each k, then add the squares.
1² + 2² + 3² + 4² = 1 + 4 + 9 + 16.
1+4 = 5; 5+9 = 14; 14+16 = 30.
30
3.formula inside
Evaluate: Σk=14 (k + 1).

Worked solution

Background: the expression can be anything built from k. For (k + 1), each term is one more than its index — so you're really adding 2, 3, 4, 5. Careful not to add the 1 only once; it appears in EVERY term.
k = 1 → 2; k = 2 → 3; k = 3 → 4; k = 4 → 5.
2 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 14.
14
4.start ≠ 1
Evaluate: Σk=36 2k.

Worked solution

Background: the index doesn't have to start at 1 — it starts wherever the bottom label says. Here k begins at 3 and ends at 6, giving four terms (count them: 3, 4, 5, 6 — that's 6 − 3 + 1 = 4 terms).
2·3 + 2·4 + 2·5 + 2·6 = 6 + 8 + 10 + 12.
6+8 = 14; 14+10 = 24; 24+12 = 36.
36
5.write the sigma
Write in sigma notation (don't evaluate): 2 + 4 + 6 + 8 + 10.

Worked solution

Background: to write a sum in sigma notation, find the pattern as a formula in k: the terms here are the even numbers, and the k-th even number is 2k. Then read off the start (first term ↔ k = 1) and end (last term ↔ k = 5, since 2·5 = 10).
Terms: 2·1, 2·2, 2·3, 2·4, 2·5 → expression 2k, k from 1 to 5.
Check by expanding: Σk=15 2k = 2+4+6+8+10 ✓.
Σk=15 2k

Write in sigma notation: 1² + 2² + 3² + ⋯ + 10².

Worked solution

Background: the "⋯" means the pattern continues — you don't write every term, the formula carries the pattern. Every term is (its position)², so the expression is k², from position 1 to position 10.
Expression: k². Start: k = 1. End: k = 10.

7.left & right sums
For f(x) = x + 1 on [0, 4] with n = 4, compute the left sum and the right sum. (The true area, by geometry, is 12 — a trapezoid. Compare!)

Worked solution

Background: Δx = (b − a)/n = (4 − 0)/4 = 1, so the slice edges are 0, 1, 2, 3, 4. A left sum reads heights at edges 0–3; a right sum at edges 1–4. Each sum = Δx × (sum of its heights).
Heights: f(0)=1, f(1)=2, f(2)=3, f(3)=4, f(4)=5.
Left = 1·(1 + 2 + 3 + 4) = 10. Right = 1·(2 + 3 + 4 + 5) = 14.
True area 12 sits exactly halfway between — makes sense, since f is a straight line and left/right miss by the same little triangles.
Left = 10, Right = 14 (true value 12)
8.midpoint sum
Same setup — f(x) = x + 1 on [0, 4], n = 4 — but now compute the MIDPOINT sum. Something special happens; explain why.

Worked solution

Background: the midpoint sum reads each height at the CENTER of its slice. Slices [0,1], [1,2], [2,3], [3,4] have midpoints 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5. For a straight-line function, the little triangle the rectangle misses on one side of the midpoint exactly equals the extra triangle it gains on the other side.
Heights: f(0.5)=1.5, f(1.5)=2.5, f(2.5)=3.5, f(3.5)=4.5.
Midpoint sum = 1·(1.5 + 2.5 + 3.5 + 4.5) = 12.
That's EXACTLY the true area! For straight lines the midpoint errors cancel perfectly — which hints why midpoint is so strong on curves too: the cancellation is merely approximate there instead of perfect.
Midpoint sum = 12 — exactly right, because the over/under triangles cancel on a straight line
9.all three sums
For f(x) = x² on [1, 3] with n = 2, compute the left, right, and midpoint sums. The true value is ∫13 x² dx = 26/3 ≈ 8.67 — which estimate wins?

Worked solution

Background: Δx = (3 − 1)/2 = 1. Slices: [1,2] and [2,3]. Left edges 1, 2; right edges 2, 3; midpoints 1.5, 2.5. Only two rectangles, so this is quick — the point is comparing the three methods on equal footing.
Left = 1·(f(1) + f(2)) = 1 + 4 = 5.
Right = 1·(f(2) + f(3)) = 4 + 9 = 13.
Midpoint = 1·(f(1.5) + f(2.5)) = 2.25 + 6.25 = 8.5.
Distances from 26/3 ≈ 8.667: left misses by ≈ 3.67, right by ≈ 4.33, midpoint by only ≈ 0.17.
Left = 5, Right = 13, Midpoint = 8.5 — midpoint wins by a mile
10.table · unequal widths
Values of f(x) = √x at perfect squares: x: 0, 1, 4, 9  →  f(x): 0, 1, 2, 3. Using these three (unequal!) subintervals, compute the left and right sums estimating ∫09 √x dx. (True value: 18, from Lesson 21's techniques!)

Worked solution

Background: with unequal spacing there is no single Δx — each rectangle's width is the gap to the next x-value: 1 − 0 = 1, 4 − 1 = 3, 9 − 4 = 5. Then it's still height × width for each slice, added up.
Widths: 1, 3, 5.
Left (heights 0, 1, 2): 0·1 + 1·3 + 2·5 = 0 + 3 + 10 = 13.
Right (heights 1, 2, 3): 1·1 + 2·3 + 3·5 = 1 + 6 + 15 = 22.
True value 18 lies between, as it must: √x is increasing, so left under, right over.
Left = 13, Right = 22 (true value 18)
11.table · equal widths
Water flows into a tank; the flow rate is measured every 3 minutes: t (min): 0, 3, 6, 9, 12 · R(t) (L/min): 10, 8, 7, 5, 4. Estimate the total liters added over the 12 minutes with (a) a left sum and (b) a right sum.

Worked solution

Background: the integral of a flow rate is total volume (the course's oldest idea). Five readings → four subintervals, each 3 minutes wide. Left sum uses the first four readings; right sum uses the last four.
(a) Left = 3·(10 + 8 + 7 + 5) = 3·30 = 90 liters.
(b) Right = 3·(8 + 7 + 5 + 4) = 3·24 = 72 liters.
Units check: (L/min)·min = L ✓. Since R is decreasing, left is the overestimate here — the flip side of the usual pattern!
(a) 90 liters   (b) 72 liters
12.table · unequal widths
A runner's velocity is measured at uneven times: t (s): 0, 1, 3, 7, 8 · v(t) (m/s): 2, 5, 7, 4, 3. Estimate the distance run over [0, 8] with (a) a left sum and (b) a right sum.

Worked solution

Background: uneven times are the AP classic. First compute each gap: 1−0 = 1, 3−1 = 2, 7−3 = 4, 8−7 = 1. Then pair each width with the height at that subinterval's left (or right) end. Writing the four width·height products in a row prevents mix-ups.
(a) Left = 2·1 + 5·2 + 7·4 + 4·1 = 2 + 10 + 28 + 4 = 44 meters.
(b) Right = 5·1 + 7·2 + 4·4 + 3·1 = 5 + 14 + 16 + 3 = 38 meters.
Note: v rises then falls, so neither sum is guaranteed over or under — no justification is possible here, and saying so is the smart answer.
(a) 44 meters   (b) 38 meters
13.table · biology
A bacteria colony grows at these measured rates: t (hours): 0, 2, 4, 10 · r(t) (bacteria/hour): 100, 150, 200, 300. Estimate how many bacteria are added from t = 0 to t = 10 using (a) a left sum and (b) a right sum. (c) Which is the underestimate, if r(t) keeps increasing between readings?

Worked solution

Background: integrating a growth RATE (bacteria per hour) gives total new bacteria — same rate→total logic as velocity→distance. Widths: 2−0 = 2, 4−2 = 2, 10−4 = 6. Watch that last wide gap: it carries the most weight in the sum.
(a) Left = 100·2 + 150·2 + 200·6 = 200 + 300 + 1200 = 1700 bacteria.
(b) Right = 150·2 + 200·2 + 300·6 = 300 + 400 + 1800 = 2500 bacteria.
(c) r is increasing, so each left height is the smallest on its subinterval → rectangles sit below the curve → the left sum, 1700, underestimates.
(a) 1700   (b) 2500   (c) the left sum underestimates (r increasing)
14.midpoint vs. the rest
For f(x) = x² on [0, 4] with n = 2, compute the left, right, and midpoint sums, and compare to the true value ∫04 x² dx = 64/3 ≈ 21.33.

Worked solution

Background: Δx = (4 − 0)/2 = 2 — wide slices, a real stress test. Slices [0,2] and [2,4]; midpoints 1 and 3. With only two fat rectangles, left and right will be terrible; the question is how well midpoint holds up.
Left = 2·(f(0) + f(2)) = 2·(0 + 4) = 8.
Right = 2·(f(2) + f(4)) = 2·(4 + 16) = 40.
Midpoint = 2·(f(1) + f(3)) = 2·(1 + 9) = 20.
Against ≈ 21.33: left misses by ≈ 13.3, right by ≈ 18.7, midpoint by only ≈ 1.3 — even with just two slices!
Left = 8, Right = 40, Midpoint = 20 (true ≈ 21.33)
15.over/under · justify
Let f(x) = x³ on [1, 2] (an increasing function there). Without computing anything, state whether a right Riemann sum overestimates or underestimates ∫12 x³ dx — and justify it in a full sentence, AP style.

Worked solution

Background: AP graders award the point for the REASONING chain: (1) name the behavior (increasing/decreasing), (2) connect it to where the sampled height sits relative to the curve on each slice, (3) conclude over/under. The word "overestimate" alone earns nothing.
f(x) = x³ is increasing on [1, 2] (bigger x → bigger cube).
On each slice, the right endpoint is where f is LARGEST, so every rectangle pokes above the curve.
Full-credit sentence: "Since f is increasing on [1, 2], each right-endpoint height is the maximum of f on its subinterval, so each rectangle contains area above the curve; therefore the right Riemann sum overestimates the integral."
Right sum overestimates — because f is increasing, each right-edge height tops the curve on its slice
16.over/under · decreasing
Now let f(x) = 1/x on [1, 3] (a decreasing function). Does a LEFT Riemann sum overestimate or underestimate ∫13 1/x dx? Justify with a full sentence.

Worked solution

Background: for decreasing functions the table flips: the LEFT edge of each slice is now where the function is tallest. Don't memorize the four cases blindly — re-derive them each time by asking "on one slice, is the sampled edge the high point or the low point?"
f(x) = 1/x is decreasing on [1, 3] (bigger x → smaller 1/x).
On each slice, the left endpoint gives the LARGEST height, so each rectangle pokes above the curve.
Sentence: "Since f is decreasing on [1, 3], each left-endpoint height is the maximum of f on its subinterval, so the rectangles contain extra area above the curve; the left sum therefore overestimates the integral."
Left sum overestimates — decreasing function, so left edges are the high points
17.which is best? · reasoning
In the notes, for x² on [0, 2] with n = 4, we got left = 1.75, right = 3.75, midpoint = 2.625, true value 8/3 ≈ 2.667. (a) Explain in words WHY the midpoint sum usually beats both left and right. (b) A friend proposes a different fix: average the left and right sums. Compute that average here and compare it to the midpoint sum.

Worked solution

Background: left and right sums make ONE-SIDED errors on a monotone stretch — all-under or all-over. A midpoint rectangle splits its slice: the curve is below the sampled height on part of the slice and above it on the rest, so its errors partially cancel WITHIN each slice. Averaging left and right instead cancels errors ACROSS the two whole sums — that's a preview of Lesson 23's trapezoidal rule!
(a) Midpoint: each rectangle overshoots on one side of the midpoint and undershoots on the other, so most of the error cancels slice by slice. Left/right rectangles err in the same direction on every slice, so their errors pile up instead of cancelling.
(b) Average = (1.75 + 3.75)/2 = 5.5/2 = 2.75. Compare: midpoint 2.625 is off by ≈ 0.042; the average 2.75 is off by ≈ 0.083.
Both are big upgrades, but midpoint wins here — and that ranking (midpoint slightly beating the left/right average) is typical for curves like x².
(a) midpoint errors cancel within each slice   (b) average = 2.75; midpoint (2.625) is closer to 8/3
18.Gauss's shortcut
Evaluate Σk=1100 k WITHOUT adding 100 numbers. (Hint — a trick young Carl Gauss reportedly found in school: pair the first term with the last, the second with the second-to-last…)

Worked solution

Background: story time: Gauss's teacher assigned 1 + 2 + ⋯ + 100 to keep the class busy; Gauss answered almost instantly. His idea: clever GROUPING beats brute-force adding. Pairing tricks like this are why sigma notation matters — you can manipulate a whole sum at once instead of term by term.
Pair up: (1 + 100), (2 + 99), (3 + 98), … every pair totals 101.
100 numbers make 50 pairs → 50 × 101 = 5050.
General formula worth remembering: Σk=1n k = n(n+1)/2. Check: 100·101/2 = 5050 ✓, and for n = 5 it gives 15, matching exercise 1 ✓.
5050
19.the definition · conceptual
Write the right-endpoint Riemann sum, in sigma notation, whose limit as n → ∞ equals ∫13 x² dx. (Give Δx, the k-th sample point xk, and the full Σ expression — no evaluating!)

Worked solution

Background: to build a Riemann sum from scratch you need two ingredients: the width Δx = (b − a)/n, and a formula for the k-th sample point. Starting at a and taking k steps of size Δx lands you at a + k·Δx — that's the k-th RIGHT endpoint (k = 1 gives the end of slice 1, … , k = n gives b). This "build the sum" direction appears on AP exams in reverse too: they show the sum, you name the integral.
Width: Δx = (3 − 1)/n = 2/n.
Right endpoints: xk = 1 + 2k/n for k = 1 to n. (Check: k = n gives 1 + 2 = 3 = b ✓.)
Sum: Σk=1n (1 + 2k/n)² · (2/n) — and the definition says ∫13 x² dx = limn→∞ Σk=1n (1 + 2k/n)² · (2/n).
Δx = 2/n, xk = 1 + 2k/n, sum = Σk=1n (1 + 2k/n)²·(2/n)
20.challenge · full AP style
Water is pumped out of a flooded basement. The pumping rate R(t), in gallons per hour, is measured at selected times:
t (hours)025712
R(t) (gal/hr)4030252015
(a) Estimate ∫012 R(t) dt with a left Riemann sum using all four subintervals. (b) Estimate it with a right Riemann sum. (c) Using correct units, explain what ∫012 R(t) dt represents. (d) R(t) is decreasing throughout. Which of your two estimates is an overestimate? Justify.

Worked solution

Background: this is the anatomy of a real AP free-response question: compute (a)–(b), interpret with units (c), justify (d). Graders score each part separately, so ALWAYS attempt every part. Widths first, always: 2−0 = 2, 5−2 = 3, 7−5 = 2, 12−7 = 5.
(a) Left (heights 40, 30, 25, 20): 40·2 + 30·3 + 25·2 + 20·5 = 80 + 90 + 50 + 100 = 320 gallons.
(b) Right (heights 30, 25, 20, 15): 30·2 + 25·3 + 20·2 + 15·5 = 60 + 75 + 40 + 75 = 250 gallons.
(c) ∫012 R(t) dt is the total number of GALLONS of water pumped out of the basement during the first 12 hours — a rate (gal/hr) integrated over hours yields gallons.
(d) Since R is decreasing, each left-endpoint height is the largest value of R on its subinterval, so every left rectangle contains area above the curve — the left sum, 320 gallons, is the overestimate (and the right sum, 250, underestimates).
(a) 320 gallons   (b) 250 gallons   (c) total gallons pumped in 12 hours   (d) the left sum — R decreasing makes left heights the maxima
