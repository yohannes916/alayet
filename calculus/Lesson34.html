Lesson 34 — Volumes I: Disks & Washers

Slicing Solids & Solids of Revolution · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 34 of 40

🎯 Goal of this lesson: take the slicing idea into the third dimension. You'll compute the volume of 3-D solids by slicing them into thin disks and rings — and along the way, calculus will hand you the cone and sphere formulas you once had to memorize, derived from scratch.

1 From area to volume — the same story, one dimension up

  • Every big idea in this course has been chop into thin pieces, measure each piece, add them up. Area under a curve: skinny rectangles, height × tiny width. Today: volume — and the pieces become skinny slabs, like slices of a loaf of bread.
  • Slice a solid perpendicular to the x-axis at position x. That slice has some cross-section area A(x), and a tiny thickness dx. Its tiny volume is A(x) dx — area of the slice × skinny thickness.
  • Add up all the slices: V = ∫ₐᵇ A(x) dx. That's the master formula for ALL of volume-by-slicing.
  • Recognize the shape of this idea? It's Lesson 1's rate × time, added up wearing 3-D clothes: here A(x) plays the "rate" (cubic units gained per unit of thickness) and dx plays the "little chunk of time."

2 Solids of revolution — spin a flat region

  • Where do nice solids come from? Take a flat region under a curve y = f(x) and spin it around the x-axis, like clay on a pottery wheel or wood on a lathe. The flat shape sweeps out a solid — vases, cones, spheres, trumpets all arise this way.
  • The magic of spinning: every cross-section (slice perpendicular to the axis) is a perfect circle. At position x, the slice is a DISK whose radius is the height of the curve: r(x) = f(x).
  • Area of a disk: A(x) = π r² = π [f(x)]². Feed that into the master formula:
The disk formula: spin the region under y = f(x), a ≤ x ≤ b, around the x-axis → V = π ∫ₐᵇ [f(x)]² dx. Golden rule: square FIRST, then integrate.

3 Flagship example: y = √x spun around the x-axis

Problem: the region under y = √x from x = 0 to 4 is revolved about the x-axis (it sweeps out a shape like a trumpet bell). Find the volume.
Step 1 — radius: at position x, the disk's radius is r(x) = √x.
Step 2 — square first: [f(x)]² = (√x)² = x. (The square un-does the root — lovely.)
Step 3 — integrate: V = π ∫₀⁴ x dx = π [x²/2]₀⁴ = π · 16/2 = 8π.
Answer: exactly 8π ≈ 25.13 cubic units. The most common error in this whole topic is integrating √x and THEN squaring — always square the function before you integrate.

4 Sanity checks — calculus meets geometry class

  • Cylinder check: revolve the constant y = 3 on [0, 5]. Every disk has radius 3: V = π ∫₀⁵ 3² dx = π ∫₀⁵ 9 dx = 45π. Geometry says a cylinder is πr²h = π · 9 · 5 = 45π ✓. The formulas agree.
  • Cone check: revolve y = x on [0, 3]. The radius grows from 0 to 3 — that's a cone lying on its side, tip at the origin, with r = 3 and h = 3: V = π ∫₀³ x² dx = π [x³/3]₀³ = 9π. Geometry's memorized formula: (1/3)πr²h = (1/3)π · 9 · 3 = 9π ✓.
  • Pause on that: calculus just DERIVED the cone formula — the mysterious 1/3 you memorized in geometry is really the ∫x² dx = x³/3 showing through. (Exercise 10 does the same for the sphere!)

5 Washers — when the region has a gap

  • Spin the region between two curves (Lesson 10's area setup) and each slice isn't a solid disk — it's a ring: a big disk with a round hole, like a hardware-store washer or a donut slice.
  • Ring area = big circle minus hole: A = πR² − πr² = π(R² − r²), where R = outer radius (the upper curve) and r = inner radius (the lower curve).
The washer formula: V = π ∫ₐᵇ (R² − r²) dx.
Flagship: the region between y = x and y = x² on [0, 1] (they cross at 0 and 1; between, x is on top), revolved about the x-axis. Outer R = x, inner r = x²:
V = π ∫₀¹ (x² − x⁴) dx = π [x³/3 − x⁵/5]₀¹ = π(1/3 − 1/5) = 2π/15.
  • ⚠️ THE classic error: π(R² − r²) is NOT π(R − r)². Squares don't distribute over subtraction: with R = 5, r = 3: R² − r² = 25 − 9 = 16, but (R − r)² = 4. Subtract the squared radii — never square the difference.

6 Setup discipline — the 4-step ritual

  • 1. Sketch the region (even roughly). You cannot set up what you cannot see.
  • 2. Identify the radii: disk → r(x) = the function's height. Washer → R(x) = far curve from the axis, r(x) = near curve.
  • 3. Limits from intersections (Lesson 10 skill): if the problem doesn't hand you a and b, solve f(x) = g(x) to find where the region begins and ends.
  • 4. Square, then integrate, and keep the π outside the whole way. On the AP exam, a correctly written setup earns most of the points — even before you integrate anything.
✏️ Exercises — 20 problems (write your answer on paper, then reveal)
1.disk
The region under y = x from x = 0 to x = 2 is revolved about the x-axis. Find the volume of the resulting cone.

Worked solution

Background: disk method: each slice at position x is a circle of radius f(x) = x, so its area is πx². The volume is V = π∫[f(x)]² dx — square first, then integrate.
V = π ∫₀² x² dx = π [x³/3]₀² = π · 8/3.
Cone check: this is a cone with r = 2, h = 2: (1/3)πr²h = (1/3)π · 4 · 2 = 8π/3 ✓.
8π/3 cubic units
2.disk · cylinder check
Revolve y = 2 (a constant) on [0, 6] about the x-axis. Find the volume with the disk formula, then confirm it against geometry's cylinder formula πr²h.

Worked solution

Background: a constant height spins into a cylinder — every disk is identical, radius 2. Checking calculus against a shape you already know is the best way to trust a new formula.
V = π ∫₀⁶ 2² dx = π ∫₀⁶ 4 dx = π · 4 · 6 = 24π.
Geometry: cylinder with radius 2, height (length) 6: πr²h = π · 4 · 6 = 24π ✓. Perfect agreement.
24π cubic units (≈ 75.40)
3.disk
The region under y = x² from x = 0 to x = 1 is revolved about the x-axis. Find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: square first! The radius is x², so the disk area is π(x²)² = πx⁴ — the exponents multiply: (x²)² = x⁴, not x². This solid is a slim trumpet hugging the axis.
V = π ∫₀¹ (x²)² dx = π ∫₀¹ x⁴ dx = π [x⁵/5]₀¹ = π/5.
π/5 cubic units
4.disk
The region under y = √x from x = 0 to x = 9 is revolved about the x-axis. Find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: same trumpet-bell family as the flagship (√x on [0,4] gave 8π), but with a wider window. Squaring √x hands you the easiest possible integrand: (√x)² = x.
V = π ∫₀⁹ (√x)² dx = π ∫₀⁹ x dx = π [x²/2]₀⁹ = π · 81/2.
81π/2 cubic units (= 40.5π)
5.disk · shifted window
Revolve y = 3 on [1, 4] about the x-axis. Find the volume, and say what solid you built.

Worked solution

Background: the limits don't have to start at 0 — the slab count just runs from x = 1 to x = 4, a length of 3. Constant radius → cylinder, wherever it sits on the axis.
V = π ∫₁⁴ 9 dx = 9π (4 − 1) = 27π.
The solid: a cylinder of radius 3 and height 3. Check: πr²h = π · 9 · 3 = 27π ✓.
27π cubic units — a cylinder
6.disk
The region under y = √(x+1) from x = 0 to x = 3 is revolved about the x-axis. Find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: squaring dissolves the square root even when there's more inside: (√(x+1))² = x + 1. What's left is a polynomial integral you could do in your sleep.
V = π ∫₀³ (x + 1) dx = π [x²/2 + x]₀³ = π (9/2 + 3) = π · 15/2.
15π/2 cubic units
7.disk
The region under y = x³ from x = 0 to x = 1 is revolved about the x-axis. Find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: power rule for the square: (x³)² = x⁶. Compare with problem 3: x² gave π/5, and x³ hugs the axis even tighter on [0,1], so expect something smaller than π/5.
V = π ∫₀¹ (x³)² dx = π ∫₀¹ x⁶ dx = π [x⁷/7]₀¹ = π/7.
Sense check: π/7 < π/5 ✓ — skinnier region, smaller solid.
π/7 cubic units
8.disk · cone derived
Revolve y = 2x on [0, 3] about the x-axis. Find the volume with calculus, then verify against geometry's cone formula (1/3)πr²h.

Worked solution

Background: a line through the origin spins into a cone lying on its side: tip at the origin, height h = 3 along the axis, and base radius = the height of the line at x = 3, which is 2·3 = 6.
V = π ∫₀³ (2x)² dx = π ∫₀³ 4x² dx = 4π [x³/3]₀³ = 4π · 9 = 36π.
Geometry check: (1/3)πr²h = (1/3)π · 36 · 3 = 36π ✓. The 1/3 in the cone formula IS the 3 in x³/3.
36π cubic units
9.disk · exponential
The region under y = ex from x = 0 to x = 1 is revolved about the x-axis. Find the exact volume.

Worked solution

Background: squaring an exponential doubles the exponent: (ex)² = e2x. From Lesson 17, ∫e2x dx = e2x/2. "Exact" means leave e in the answer — no decimals needed.
V = π ∫₀¹ e2x dx = π [e2x/2]₀¹ = π (e²/2 − 1/2).
= π(e² − 1)/2. (≈ π · 3.194 ≈ 10.04 if you're curious.)
π(e² − 1)/2 cubic units
10.disk · the sphere!
The curve y = √(9 − x²) on [−3, 3] is the top half of a circle of radius 3. Revolve it about the x-axis and find the volume. (Hint: square first, and use the symmetry shortcut from Lesson 29.) Compare with geometry's sphere formula (4/3)πr³.

Worked solution

Background: spinning a semicircle sweeps out a full SPHERE. Squaring kills the root: (√(9 − x²))² = 9 − x², a plain polynomial! And 9 − x² is an even function (Lesson 29), so ∫ from −3 to 3 equals 2 × ∫ from 0 to 3.
V = π ∫₋₃³ (9 − x²) dx = 2π ∫₀³ (9 − x²) dx (symmetry shortcut).
∫₀³ (9 − x²) dx = [9x − x³/3]₀³ = 27 − 9 = 18.
V = 2π · 18 = 36π.
Geometry check: (4/3)πr³ = (4/3)π · 27 = 36π ✓. Calculus just DERIVED the sphere formula — nothing memorized, everything earned.
36π cubic units — the sphere formula, derived
11.washer
The region between y = 2x and y = x² (they cross at x = 0 and x = 2, with 2x on top between) is revolved about the x-axis. Find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: a region with a gap below it spins into slices that are RINGS: area π(R² − r²), outer minus inner, each radius squared separately. Here the outer radius is the top curve R = 2x and the inner is r = x². (Check the crossing: 2x = x² → x² − 2x = 0 → x = 0, 2; at x = 1, 2x = 2 is above x² = 1 ✓.)
V = π ∫₀² [(2x)² − (x²)²] dx = π ∫₀² (4x² − x⁴) dx.
= π [4x³/3 − x⁵/5]₀² = π (32/3 − 32/5).
= 32π (1/3 − 1/5) = 32π · 2/15 = 64π/15.
64π/15 cubic units
12.washer
The region between y = √x and y = x on [0, 1] (√x is on top) is revolved about the x-axis. Find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: outer radius R = √x (the top curve), inner radius r = x. Squaring first turns both into easy powers: (√x)² = x and x² = x². The curves cross at x = 0 and 1 (√x = x → x = x² → x = 0, 1), matching the given limits.
V = π ∫₀¹ [(√x)² − x²] dx = π ∫₀¹ (x − x²) dx.
= π [x²/2 − x³/3]₀¹ = π (1/2 − 1/3) = π/6.
π/6 cubic units
13.washer · pipe
The region between y = 3 and y = 1 on [0, 4] is revolved about the x-axis, making a solid pipe (a cylinder with a cylindrical hole). Find its volume two ways: with the washer formula, and with geometry.

Worked solution

Background: both radii are constant, so every washer is identical — outer R = 3, inner r = 1. This is the cleanest possible test that the washer formula measures "big cylinder minus the hole."
Washer: V = π ∫₀⁴ (3² − 1²) dx = π ∫₀⁴ 8 dx = 32π.
Geometry: outer cylinder π · 9 · 4 = 36π, minus hole π · 1 · 4 = 4π → 32π ✓.
32π cubic units
14.setup only · AP style
Write — but do NOT evaluate — an integral for the volume when the region under y = ln x from x = 1 to x = e is revolved about the x-axis. (On the AP exam, a correct setup like this earns points all by itself.)

Worked solution

Background: "setup only" questions test whether you can build the integral: identify the method (region touches the axis → disks), the radius (r = ln x), square it, and read off the limits. Evaluating π∫(ln x)² dx needs integration by parts twice — the setup is the skill being graded here.
Method: region under one curve down to the axis → disk method.
Radius at position x: r(x) = ln x. Square it: (ln x)².
Limits: given directly, x = 1 to x = e.
V = π ∫₁^e (ln x)² dx
15.washer · find the limits
The region enclosed by y = √x and y = x/2 is revolved about the x-axis. First find where the curves intersect (Lesson 10 skill!), then find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: no limits given — they're hiding in the intersections. Solve √x = x/2 by squaring both sides: x = x²/4 → x² − 4x = 0 → x(x − 4) = 0 → x = 0 and x = 4. Between them, test x = 1: √1 = 1 vs 1/2 — so √x is the outer curve.
R = √x, r = x/2. Squares: x and x²/4.
V = π ∫₀⁴ (x − x²/4) dx = π [x²/2 − x³/12]₀⁴.
= π (16/2 − 64/12) = π (8 − 16/3) = π · 8/3.
8π/3 cubic units
16.spot the error
To find the volume when the region between y = x and y = x² on [0, 1] spins about the x-axis, a student writes V = π ∫₀¹ (x − x²)² dx and gets π/30. The correct answer (from this lesson's flagship) is 2π/15. Show the two answers really disagree, and explain exactly what the student did wrong.

Worked solution

Background: THE classic washer error: writing π(R − r)² instead of π(R² − r²). A ring's area is the big circle MINUS the hole — π(R − r)² is instead the area of a small circle whose radius is the gap width, a completely different (and smaller) thing. Algebra agrees: (R − r)² = R² − 2Rr + r² ≠ R² − r².
The student's integral: ∫₀¹ (x − x²)² dx = ∫₀¹ (x² − 2x³ + x⁴) dx = 1/3 − 1/2 + 1/5 = 10/30 − 15/30 + 6/30 = 1/30 → π/30.
Correct: π ∫₀¹ (x² − x⁴) dx = π(1/3 − 1/5) = 2π/15 = 4π/30.
π/30 vs 4π/30 — the wrong method loses three-quarters of the volume! Numbers confirm the formulas differ: at x = 1/2, R² − r² = 1/4 − 1/16 = 3/16, but (R − r)² = (1/4)² = 1/16.
Student squared the difference; must subtract the squares: π∫(R² − r²) dx = 2π/15
17.units
A solid's cross-section area at position x is A(x) square meters, and x is measured in meters. In V = ∫ A(x) dx, explain why the answer comes out in cubic meters — and compute the volume if A(x) = 6 m² constantly from x = 0 to x = 2 m.

Worked solution

Background: the units-cancellation habit from Lesson 1 still runs the show in 3-D. Each slab contributes (area) × (thickness) = m² × m = m³, and adding slabs doesn't change the units. If the units don't come out cubic, the setup is wrong.
Units: A(x) dx carries m² · m = m³ ✓ — every slice is a genuine little volume.
Constant cross-section: V = ∫₀² 6 dx = 6 · 2 = 12.
(That's a prism: base area 6 m², length 2 m — geometry agrees: 6 × 2 = 12.)
12 cubic meters — m² × m = m³
18.setup only · washer
Write — but do NOT evaluate — an integral for the volume when the region between y = ex and y = 1 from x = 0 to x = 1 is revolved about the x-axis. State your R and r clearly.

Worked solution

Background: on [0, 1], ex runs from 1 up to e, so it sits on top; y = 1 is the floor of the region (and since the floor isn't the x-axis, there's a hole — washers, not disks). Outer radius = distance from axis to far curve, inner = to near curve.
R(x) = ex (outer), r(x) = 1 (inner).
Squares: (ex)² = e2x and 1² = 1.
V = π ∫₀¹ (e2x − 1) dx   with R = ex, r = 1
19.disk · hemisphere
Revolve y = √(4 − x²) on [0, 2] (a quarter-circle of radius 2) about the x-axis. Find the volume, and explain what fraction of a full sphere you built.

Worked solution

Background: the full semicircle on [−2, 2] would spin into a whole sphere; using only [0, 2] spins the right quarter-circle into the right HALF of that sphere — a hemisphere (dome). Square first: (√(4 − x²))² = 4 − x².
V = π ∫₀² (4 − x²) dx = π [4x − x³/3]₀² = π (8 − 8/3) = 16π/3.
Full sphere of radius 2: (4/3)π · 2³ = 32π/3. Our answer is exactly half of that ✓.
16π/3 cubic units — exactly half a sphere
20.challenge · two solids
Two solids are made by revolving regions on [0, 1] about the x-axis: solid 1 from the region under y = √x, solid 2 from the region under y = x. (a) Find both volumes. (b) Which is bigger, and why does that make sense from a sketch? (c) Subtract them and compare with exercise 12 — explain the connection.

Worked solution

Background: on [0, 1], √x ≥ x (at x = 1/4: √x = 1/2 vs x = 1/4), so the √x solid completely contains the y = x cone. A washer volume is literally "outer solid minus inner solid" — this problem lets you SEE that by computing all three numbers.
(a) Solid 1: V₁ = π ∫₀¹ (√x)² dx = π ∫₀¹ x dx = π/2. Solid 2: V₂ = π ∫₀¹ x² dx = π/3.
(b) V₁ = π/2 > π/3 = V₂. Makes sense: √x is the higher curve on [0, 1], so every disk of solid 1 has the bigger radius, and it encloses solid 2 entirely.
(c) V₁ − V₂ = π/2 − π/3 = π/6 — exactly exercise 12's washer answer for the region BETWEEN √x and x. The washer formula π∫(R² − r²) dx is nothing but "volume of the outer solid minus volume of the inner one," computed in a single integral.
(a) π/2 and π/3   (b) the √x solid — bigger radius at every slice   (c) π/2 − π/3 = π/6 = the washer volume ✓
