Lesson 28 — Reading Accumulation Graphs

The AP Classic · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 28 of 40

📚 All lessons← Prev: Lesson 27Next: Lesson 29 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: master the single most classic AP Calculus problem — you're shown the graph of f (line segments and half-circles), and asked everything about g(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt. Two superpowers crack it: values of g come from geometry (areas of triangles, rectangles, half-circles), and g′ = f (Lesson 27) turns every shape question about g into a sign-reading question about f. Plus: the exact justification sentences graders want.

1 The AP setup, from scratch

  • Here's the game. The exam hands you a picture of some function f — almost always built from straight line segments and half-circles, so every area is computable with school geometry. Then it defines g(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt and asks a barrage of questions about g: values, maxima, concavity, sketches.
  • The trap: you can't "solve" for g with algebra — you were never given a formula for f! The whole point is to reason from the picture. You have exactly two superpowers:
  • Superpower 1 — geometry for VALUES. g(5) is the area between f and the t-axis from 0 to 5. Chop the region into triangles (½·base·height), rectangles (base·height), and half-circles, and remember Lesson 10's rule: regions below the axis count as NEGATIVE.
  • Superpower 2 — FTC 2 for SHAPE. By Lesson 27, g′(x) = f(x): the graph you're LOOKING at is the derivative of the function they're ASKING about. Every "where is g increasing / peaking / bending?" question becomes "where is f positive / crossing zero / rising?"

2 The master translation table

  • Since g′ = f (and therefore g″ = f′), every fact about g's shape translates into a fact you can read straight off f's graph. This table is worth memorizing cold:
The graph of g ...... exactly where the graph of f ...
is increasingis above the axis (f > 0)
is decreasingis below the axis (f < 0)
has a local MAXcrosses zero going down (+ to −)
has a local MINcrosses zero going up (− to +)
is concave UPis increasing (rising)
is concave DOWNis decreasing (falling)
has an inflection pointchanges direction (f's own local max or min)
  • Why the concavity rows? g″ = f′, and "concave up" means g″ > 0, i.e. f′ > 0, i.e. f rising. Notice the level-shift: questions about g's bending are answered by f's slope, one derivative deeper.
  • Careful with the max/min rows: a local max of g is NOT where f is highest! It's where f crosses zero from + to − — the moment the inflow stops and the outflow begins. (Tank story: the water level peaks the instant the net flow switches from filling to draining.)

3 Fully worked flagship example

Setup: f is piecewise-linear on [0, 8]: it rises in a straight line from (0, 0) to (2, 4), stays flat at height 4 until t = 4, then descends in a straight line from (4, 4) through (6, 0) down to (8, −4). Let g(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt.
f(t) 4 | ●━━━━━━━● (2,4) flat to (4,4) | ╱ ╲ 2 | ╱ ╲ | ╱ ╲ (crosses 0 at t = 6) 0 ●───┬───┬───┬───┬───●───┬───→ t 0 1 2 3 4 6╲ 7 −2 | ╲ −4 | ● (8,−4)
  • Values by geometry:
    • g(2) = triangle from 0 to 2: ½ · 2 · 4 = 4.
    • g(4) = g(2) + rectangle from 2 to 4: 4 + (2 · 4) = 12.
    • g(6) = g(4) + triangle from 4 to 6 (base 2, height 4): 12 + ½ · 2 · 4 = 16.
    • g(8) = g(6) + region from 6 to 8, which is a triangle BELOW the axis (base 2, height 4, area 4, counted negative): 16 − 4 = 12.
  • Where is g maximized? g′ = f is positive on (0, 6) and negative on (6, 8), so g climbs all the way to x = 6 and then falls. Justification sentence: "g has a maximum at x = 6 because g′ = f changes from positive to negative there." Its value: g(6) = 16 (bigger than the endpoint values g(0) = 0 and g(8) = 12, so it's the absolute max too).
  • A derivative value: g′(1) = f(1) = 2 (just read the height of f: the segment from (0,0) to (2,4) has slope 2, so f(1) = 2).
  • Concavity: g″ = f′. On (0, 2), f is rising → g concave up. On (2, 4), f is constant → g is a straight line (slope 4, no bending). On (4, 8), f is falling → g concave down.
  • Sketch of g in words: starts at (0, 0) rising ever more steeply (concave up) to (2, 4); climbs as a straight ramp of slope 4 to (4, 12); keeps rising but leveling off (concave down) to a rounded peak at (6, 16); then falls, steeper and steeper, to (8, 12).

4 Justification language for the AP

  • On the AP exam, a correct answer with a vague reason ("because the graph goes down") scores ZERO justification points. The grader is looking for a sentence that names g′ (or f) and its sign behavior. Memorize these model sentences:
Model sentences:
• "g has a relative maximum at x = 6 because g′ = f changes from positive to negative there."
• "g has a relative minimum at x = 2 because g′ = f changes from negative to positive there."
• "g is increasing on (0, 6) because g′ = f > 0 on that interval."
• "g is concave down on (4, 8) because g′ = f is decreasing there."
• "g has an inflection point at x = 4 because g′ = f changes from increasing to decreasing there (so g″ changes sign)."
  • The pattern in every sentence: name the tool (g′ = f), state its sign or direction, name the place. Facts you state must be visible on the given graph — never claim to know a formula for f.

5 Half-circles: the area you must know

  • AP graph problems love half-circles, so let's nail the area from scratch. A full circle of radius r has area πr² (that's the famous formula). So:
    half-circle area = ½ π r²  ·  quarter-circle area = ¼ π r²
  • Example: a half-circle of radius 2 sitting on the t-axis has area ½ · π · 2² = 2π. If it bulges BELOW the axis, it contributes −2π to the integral. Answers stay exact: write 2π, not 6.28.
Quarter-circle example: suppose f traces a quarter-circle of radius 3 from (0, 3) down to (3, 0) (a quarter of the circle of radius 3 centered at the origin, staying above the axis). Then ∫₀³ f(t) dt = ¼ · π · 3² = 9π/4.

6 The initial-condition twist

  • A favorite variation: h(x) = 3 + ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt. Don't panic — this is just g(x) shifted UP by 3. The tank started with 3 liters already in it.
  • What changes: every VALUE goes up by 3 (h(0) = 3, not 0; h(5) = 3 + g(5)).
  • What doesn't change: everything about shape. h′ = g′ = f, so h increases, peaks, and bends at exactly the same x-values as g. Shifting a graph up doesn't move its hills sideways.

GRAPH A (used in #1–7): f is piecewise-linear on [0, 6]: flat at height 4 from t = 0 to t = 2, then a straight line from (2, 4) down to (6, −4), crossing zero at t = 4. Let g(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt.
f(t) 4 ●━━━━━━━● (0,4) flat to (2,4) | ╲ 2 | ╲ | ╲ (crosses 0 at t = 4) 0 +───┬───┬───●───┬───→ t 0 1 2 4╲ 5 −2 | ╲ −4 | ● (6,−4)
1.Graph A · values
Find g(0) and g(2).

Worked solution

Background: g(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt is the area collected under f from 0 to x. Two tools: ∫₀⁰ = 0 always (zero width), and steady heights make rectangles (area = base × height).
g(0) = ∫₀⁰ f(t) dt = 0 — nothing accumulated at the starting line.
g(2): from t = 0 to 2, f is flat at height 4 → rectangle, area = 2 × 4 = 8.
g(0) = 0,   g(2) = 8
2.Graph A · values
Find g(4).

Worked solution

Background: build on what you have: g(4) = g(2) + (area from 2 to 4). From (2, 4) the line descends to zero at t = 4, making a triangle with the axis. Triangle area = ½ × base × height.
Area from 2 to 4: triangle with base 4 − 2 = 2 and height 4 → ½ · 2 · 4 = 4. It's above the axis, so it counts positive.
g(4) = g(2) + 4 = 8 + 4 = 12.
g(4) = 12
3.Graph A · negative area
Find g(6).

Worked solution

Background: from t = 4 to 6, f is BELOW the axis (it drops from 0 down to −4). Below-axis regions subtract from the accumulation — the tank is draining. Compute the geometric area, then attach a minus sign.
Region from 4 to 6: triangle with base 2 and height 4 (from 0 down to −4) → area ½ · 2 · 4 = 4, counted as −4.
g(6) = g(4) − 4 = 12 − 4 = 8.
g(6) = 8
4.Graph A · g′ values
Find g′(1) and g′(5).

Worked solution

Background: FTC 2 (Lesson 27): g′(x) = f(x). So g′ at a point is just the HEIGHT of the given graph there — no computing, just reading. For t in [2, 6] the line from (2, 4) to (6, −4) has slope −8/4 = −2, so f(t) = 4 − 2(t − 2).
g′(1) = f(1) = 4 (the flat part).
g′(5) = f(5) = 4 − 2(5 − 2) = 4 − 6 = −2. (Check against the picture: at t = 5 the line is halfway between 0 at t = 4 and −4 at t = 6 → −2. ✓)
g′(1) = 4,   g′(5) = −2
5.Graph A · increase/decrease
On what interval is g increasing, and on what interval is g decreasing? Justify with an AP-style sentence.

Worked solution

Background: the translation table: g increasing ⟺ f > 0 (positive stuff being added), g decreasing ⟺ f < 0. Just find where the given graph is above/below the axis. The justification must name g′ = f and its sign.
f is above the axis from t = 0 until it crosses zero at t = 4, and below the axis from 4 to 6.
Sentence: "g is increasing on (0, 4) because g′ = f is positive there, and decreasing on (4, 6) because g′ = f is negative there."
increasing on (0, 4); decreasing on (4, 6) — because g′ = f changes sign at x = 4
6.Graph A · max
At what x does g have its maximum on [0, 6]? Justify, and give the maximum value.

Worked solution

Background: a local max of g sits where g′ = f crosses zero from + to − (inflow becomes outflow). For an ABSOLUTE max on a closed interval, also compare against the endpoints. The max is NOT where f is tallest — it's where f crosses zero downward.
f changes from positive to negative at x = 4, so g has its only local max there. Value: g(4) = 12 (from #2).
Endpoints: g(0) = 0 and g(6) = 8. Both smaller than 12.
Sentence: "g has its absolute maximum at x = 4 because g′ = f changes from positive to negative there, and g(4) = 12 exceeds g(0) = 0 and g(6) = 8."
maximum at x = 4; maximum value g(4) = 12
7.Graph A · concavity
Describe the shape of g on (0, 2). Then: is g concave up or concave down on (2, 6)? Find g″(3).

Worked solution

Background: g″ = f′ — the second derivative of g is the SLOPE of the given graph. Where f is constant, g′ is constant, so g is a straight line. Where f falls, g″ < 0: concave down.
On (0, 2): f is constant at 4, so g climbs at a steady slope 4 — a straight line segment (from (0,0) up to (2,8)), no bending.
On (2, 6): f is a falling line, so g′ = f is decreasing → g is concave down. (It arcs over its peak at x = 4.)
g″(3) = f′(3) = slope of f at t = 3 = −2 (the segment from (2,4) to (6,−4) drops 8 over 4).
straight line of slope 4 on (0, 2); concave down on (2, 6); g″(3) = −2

8.Graph B · half-circle
Find g(4). Give an exact answer (leave π in it).

Worked solution

Background: a full circle of radius r has area πr², so a half-circle has area ½πr². This half-circle has radius 2 (it spans t = 0 to 4, diameter 4) and sits above the axis, so its area counts positive. Keep answers exact: 2π, not 6.28.
g(4) = area of the half-circle = ½ · π · 2² = ½ · π · 4 = 2π.
g(4) = 2π
9.Graph B · mixed areas
Find g(6) and g(8), exactly.

Worked solution

Background: keep a running total from g(4) = 2π. From t = 4 to 8 the graph makes a triangle-shaped dip below the axis: down from (4,0) to (6,−4), back up to (8,0). Below the axis → negative contributions. Split it at t = 6 into two triangles.
From 4 to 6: triangle, base 2, height 4 → area 4, below the axis → contributes −4. So g(6) = 2π − 4.
From 6 to 8: another triangle, base 2, height 4 → contributes −4 more. So g(8) = 2π − 4 − 4 = 2π − 8.
(Whole dip at once: triangle base 4, height 4 → ½·4·4 = 8 below the axis. Same thing.)
g(6) = 2π − 4,   g(8) = 2π − 8
10.Graph B · g′ values
Find g′(2) and g′(7).

Worked solution

Background: g′(x) = f(x) — read heights off the picture. The top of a half-circle of radius 2 centered at (2, 0) is the point (2, 2). The segment from (6, −4) to (8, 0) rises 4 over 2, slope 2, so f(t) = −4 + 2(t − 6) there.
g′(2) = f(2) = 2 (the very top of the half-circle).
g′(7) = f(7) = −4 + 2(1) = −2 (halfway back up from −4 to 0).
g′(2) = 2,   g′(7) = −2
11.Graph B · absolute max
Find the absolute maximum value of g on [0, 8], and justify your answer AP-style.

Worked solution

Background: absolute max on a closed interval: find where g′ = f changes + to − (the only candidates for an interior peak), then compare g there with g at the endpoints. Note f is 0 at t = 0 and t = 8 too, but no sign CHANGE happens at an endpoint — endpoints are compared by value.
f is positive on (0, 4) (the half-circle) and negative on (4, 8) (the dip). So f changes from + to − only at x = 4.
Candidates: g(4) = 2π; endpoints g(0) = 0 and g(8) = 2π − 8 (negative, since 2π ≈ 6.28 < 8).
Sentence: "g has its absolute maximum at x = 4 because g′ = f changes from positive to negative there, and g(4) = 2π is greater than g(0) = 0 and g(8) = 2π − 8."
absolute maximum g(4) = 2π, at x = 4
12.Graph B · g = 0 · justify
Explain why there must be some x between 6 and 8 where g(x) = 0. (You computed g(6) and g(8) in #9. You may use the fact that 2π ≈ 6.28.)

Worked solution

Background: a continuous function that is positive at one point and negative at another must cross zero somewhere in between — it can't teleport across the axis. (This is the Intermediate Value Theorem; accumulation functions are always continuous.) So the whole argument is: show a sign change.
g(6) = 2π − 4 ≈ 6.28 − 4 = 2.28 > 0.
g(8) = 2π − 8 ≈ 6.28 − 8 = −1.72 < 0.
Sentence: "g is continuous, g(6) > 0, and g(8) < 0; therefore g must equal 0 at some x between 6 and 8." (Meaning: the dip after t = 4 eventually cancels all the half-circle's area — the tank empties partway between 6 and 8.)
g(6) = 2π − 4 > 0 and g(8) = 2π − 8 < 0, and g is continuous, so g crosses 0 in (6, 8)
13.Graph B · initial condition
Let h(x) = 3 + ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt. Find h(0) and h(4). At what x does h have its maximum on [0, 8] — and did the +3 move it?

Worked solution

Background: h is just g shifted up 3 (the tank starts with 3 liters already in it). Shifting up changes every VALUE by +3, but h′ = f is unchanged — so all increasing/decreasing/peak locations stay exactly where they were.
h(0) = 3 + ∫₀⁰ = 3 + 0 = 3.
h(4) = 3 + g(4) = 3 + 2π.
h′ = f changes from + to − at x = 4, same as before, so the max is still at x = 4. The +3 lifts the whole graph without sliding any hill sideways.
h(0) = 3,   h(4) = 3 + 2π,   max still at x = 4 — shifting up never moves the peak's location

14.Graph C · negative start
Find g(2) and g(4).

Worked solution

Background: this graph starts BELOW the axis, so g goes negative first — the tank is draining before it fills. From (0, −2) up to zero at t = 2 makes a below-axis triangle; from 2 to 4 a matching above-axis triangle.
From 0 to 2: triangle, base 2, height 2 → area 2, below the axis → g(2) = −2.
From 2 to 4: triangle, base 2, height 2 → +2. So g(4) = −2 + 2 = 0. The loss and the gain cancel exactly.
g(2) = −2,   g(4) = 0
15.Graph C · values
Find g(7), g(8), and g(10).

Worked solution

Background: keep the running total going from g(4) = 0. Pieces: a rectangle (4 to 7), a small triangle above the axis (7 to 8, where f falls from 2 to 0), and a triangle below the axis (8 to 10, where f falls from 0 to −4).
4 to 7: rectangle 3 × 2 = 6 → g(7) = 0 + 6 = 6.
7 to 8: triangle, base 1, height 2 → ½·1·2 = 1 → g(8) = 6 + 1 = 7.
8 to 10: triangle, base 2, height 4 → ½·2·4 = 4, below the axis → g(10) = 7 − 4 = 3.
g(7) = 6,   g(8) = 7,   g(10) = 3
16.Graph C · classify extremes
Find all x in (0, 10) where g has a local minimum or local maximum, classify each, and justify each with an AP-style sentence.

Worked solution

Background: interior candidates are where g′ = f crosses zero. This f crosses zero twice: at t = 2 (going up, − to +) and t = 8 (going down, + to −). Direction of the crossing decides: − to + is a local MIN (draining turns to filling); + to − is a local MAX.
At x = 2: "g has a local minimum at x = 2 because g′ = f changes from negative to positive there." (Value: g(2) = −2.)
At x = 8: "g has a local maximum at x = 8 because g′ = f changes from positive to negative there." (Value: g(8) = 7.)
local min at x = 2 (f goes − to +); local max at x = 8 (f goes + to −)
17.Graph C · concavity
On what intervals is g concave up? Concave down? What is g on (4, 7)? Also compute g″(1) and g″(9).

Worked solution

Background: g″ = f′ = the slope of the pictured graph. Rising f → concave up; falling f → concave down; constant f → g is a straight line (zero concavity). Read slopes segment by segment.
On (0, 4): f rises (slope 1) → g concave up, and g″(1) = f′(1) = 1.
On (4, 7): f constant → g is a straight line of slope 2 (no concavity).
On (7, 10): f falls (slope −2) → g concave down, and g″(9) = f′(9) = −2.
concave up on (0, 4); straight on (4, 7); concave down on (7, 10); g″(1) = 1, g″(9) = −2
18.Graph C · absolute extremes
Find the absolute maximum and absolute minimum values of g on [0, 10]. Show the candidate comparison, AP-style.

Worked solution

Background: on a closed interval the absolute extremes live either at interior critical points (where f changes sign) or at the endpoints. So build the full candidate list — x = 0, 2, 8, 10 — evaluate g at each, and compare. Writing the table IS the justification.
Candidates: g(0) = 0, g(2) = −2 (local min), g(8) = 7 (local max), g(10) = 3.
Largest: 7 at x = 8. Smallest: −2 at x = 2.
Sentence: "The candidates for absolute extremes are the critical points x = 2, 8 (where g′ = f changes sign) and the endpoints x = 0, 10. Comparing g(0) = 0, g(2) = −2, g(8) = 7, g(10) = 3: the absolute maximum is 7 at x = 8 and the absolute minimum is −2 at x = 2."
absolute max g(8) = 7; absolute min g(2) = −2
19.Graph C · sketch g + zeros
(a) Describe the graph of g on [0, 10] in words — where it starts, falls, rises, bends, peaks, and ends. (b) Find every x in [0, 10] where g(x) = 0, and explain why there are no others.

Worked solution

Background: to sketch g, combine everything: values (#14–15), increase/decrease (sign of f), and concavity (#17). For zeros of g, use computed values plus the increasing/decreasing story to rule out extra crossings.
(a) g starts at (0, 0), dips DOWN (f < 0) to a valley at (2, −2), curving concave-up the whole way to t = 4; climbs back to (4, 0), then rises as a straight ramp of slope 2 to (7, 6); keeps rising but bending over (concave down) to a rounded peak at (8, 7); then falls, steepening, to end at (10, 3).
(b) Zeros: g(0) = 0 and g(4) = 0. No others: on (0, 2) and (2, 4), g is below zero except at the two endpoints of that stretch (it dips to −2 and comes back); after x = 4, g climbs to 7 and only falls back to 3, so it stays strictly positive on (4, 10].
(a) fall to (2, −2), rise through (4, 0) to peak (8, 7), fall to (10, 3)   (b) g = 0 only at x = 0 and x = 4
20.challenge · full AP FRQ
GRAPH D: f on [0, 8]: a half-circle of radius 2 BELOW the axis from t = 0 to t = 4 (centered at (2, 0), dipping to (2, −2)), then a straight line rising from (4, 0) to (5, 3), then flat at height 3 from t = 5 to t = 8. Let g(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt.
f(t) 3 | ●━━━━━━━● (5,3) flat to (8,3) | ╱ 0 ●─┬───┬───●─┬───┬───┬──→ t 0╲1 3 ╱4 5 6 7 8 −2 | ·──· half-circle, radius 2, BELOW axis
(a) Find g(4) and g(8), exactly.   (b) On what intervals is g increasing and decreasing? Justify.   (c) Find the x-coordinate of the absolute minimum of g on [0, 8], and justify.   (d) Find all x in (0, 8] with g(x) = 0, exactly. (Use 2π ≈ 6.28 to check your candidate is in the right piece.)

Worked solution

Background: a complete AP-style FRQ. Strategy: (a) geometry with signs; (b) sign of f; (c) the − to + crossing plus endpoint comparison; (d) write a formula for g on the piece where the crossing happens — on a flat-at-3 stretch, g grows linearly: g(x) = g(5) + 3(x − 5).
(a) g(4) = −(½π·2²) = −2π (half-circle below the axis). Then 4 to 5: triangle, base 1, height 3 → +3/2, so g(5) = −2π + 3/2. Then 5 to 8: rectangle 3 × 3 = 9. g(8) = −2π + 3/2 + 9 = 21/2 − 2π. (≈ 10.5 − 6.28 = 4.22.)
(b) f < 0 on (0, 4) and f > 0 on (4, 8). "g is decreasing on (0, 4) because g′ = f < 0 there, and increasing on (4, 8) because g′ = f > 0 there."
(c) "g has its absolute minimum at x = 4 because g′ = f changes from negative to positive there (the only critical point), and the endpoint values g(0) = 0 and g(8) = 21/2 − 2π > 0 both exceed g(4) = −2π." Minimum value: −2π.
(d) g is negative from just after 0 all the way past x = 5 (g(5) = 3/2 − 2π ≈ −4.78 < 0), then climbs linearly on (5, 8]: g(x) = g(5) + 3(x − 5). Set it to zero: 3(x − 5) = 2π − 3/2 → x = 5 + (2π − 3/2)/3 = 9/2 + 2π/3 ≈ 4.5 + 2.09 = 6.59, which is indeed in (5, 8). ✓ On (0, 5] g stays below 0 (it only falls then partially recovers), so this is the only zero in (0, 8].
(a) g(4) = −2π, g(8) = 21/2 − 2π   (b) decreasing (0,4), increasing (4,8) by sign of f   (c) absolute min at x = 4, value −2π   (d) x = 9/2 + 2π/3 ≈ 6.59
