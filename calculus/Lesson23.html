Lesson 23 — The Trapezoidal Rule

Slanted Tops that Hug the Curve · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 23 of 40

📚 All lessons← Prev: Lesson 22Next: Lesson 24 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: upgrade your Riemann-sum rectangles to trapezoids — shapes whose slanted tops hug a curve far better than flat tops ever could. You'll learn the trapezoid area formula from scratch, a beautiful shortcut (trapezoid sum = average of left and right sums), how to handle the AP-favorite unequal-width table, and how concavity tells you whether your estimate is too big or too small.

1 The idea: flat tops miss, slanted tops hug

  • In Lesson 22 you approximated areas with rectangles. A rectangle's top is flat, but the curve underneath it is usually rising or falling — so every rectangle either pokes above the curve or leaves a gap under it.
  • Fix: replace the flat top with a slanted top that connects the curve's height on the left edge straight to its height on the right edge. The shape you get is a trapezoid — and its slanted top hugs the curve much more closely.
RECTANGLE (flat top) TRAPEZOID (slanted top) | _,-• ← curve | _,-• ← curve | _,-' gap! | _,-' | •────────┐ | •_,-' ← top follows | │████████│ | │████████│ the curve! | │████████│ | │████████│ +--+--------+---→ x +--+--------+---→ x
  • Trapezoid area, from scratch. A trapezoid standing on the x-axis has two parallel vertical sides: a left side of height y_left and a right side of height y_right, with width Δx between them. Here's the trick: slice off the triangle on top. What's left is a rectangle of height y_left (area y_left · Δx), and the triangle on top has base Δx and height y_right − y_left (area ½(y_right − y_left)·Δx). Add them: y_left·Δx + ½(y_right − y_left)·Δx = ½(y_left + y_right)·Δx.
  • So the formula is beautifully simple: area = (average of the two parallel sides) × width:
Trapezoid area: A = ½ (y_left + y_right) · Δx — average the two heights, multiply by the width. It makes sense: a trapezoid is like a rectangle whose height is the average of its two edge heights.
Worked example: a trapezoid with left side 4, right side 10, width 3. Area = ½(4 + 10)(3) = ½(14)(3) = 21. (Check: a rectangle of the average height 7 and width 3 also has area 21 ✓.)

3 The shortcut: trapezoid = average of left and right

  • Look at one slice: the left rectangle uses y_left·Δx, the right rectangle uses y_right·Δx, and the trapezoid uses ½(y_left + y_right)·Δx — exactly the average of the two rectangles. Since that's true for every slice, it's true for the whole sum:
Shortcut (equal widths only): Trapezoid sum = (Left sum + Right sum) / 2
  • Verify with our x² numbers: (1.75 + 3.75)/2 = 5.5/2 = 2.75 ✓ — exactly what the slice-by-slice work gave.
  • ⚠️ This shortcut only works when all slices have equal widths. With unequal widths (next section) the left/right sums pair up with the wrong widths, so you must do each trapezoid separately.

4 Tables with UNEQUAL widths — the AP classic

  • On the AP exam, data often comes as a table with unevenly spaced times. No shortcut here — just do each trapezoid on its own: for each pair of neighboring columns, average the two readings and multiply by that gap's width.
Worked example: water flows into a tank at rate r(t) liters per minute, measured at five times:

Meaning: approximately 158 liters of water flowed into the tank during the 12 minutes. Units check: (L/min) × min = L ✓.

5 Too big or too small? Concavity decides

  • New word, from scratch: concavity. A curve is concave up if it bends upward like a cup ∪ (its slope keeps increasing — think of y = x²). It's concave down if it bends downward like a frown ∩ (slope keeps decreasing — think of y = √x).
  • A trapezoid's slanted top is a chord — a straight line connecting two points on the curve. Where does a chord sit?
CONCAVE UP (cup ∪) CONCAVE DOWN (frown ∩) | • | _--•--_ ← curve bulges | chord / | •'~~~~~~~'• ABOVE chord | ABOVE / ← straight line | chord sags BELOW | /_.-' | | •--'' ← curve sags below | +---------------→ x +---------------→ x chord ABOVE curve chord BELOW curve → trapezoids OVERestimate → trapezoids UNDERestimate
  • Concave up: the curve sags below its chords, so every trapezoid top sits above the curve → the trapezoid sum OVERestimates. (That's why we got 2.75 > 8/3 for x², which is concave up.)
  • Concave down: the curve bulges above its chords, so trapezoid tops sit below the curve → the trapezoid sum UNDERestimates.
  • Memory hook: cup ∪ catches extra area → over; frown ∩ hides area → under.

6 When would you actually use this?

  • When you have a formula, the FTC gives exact answers — no approximation needed. But real life often hands you measurements instead: a nurse records a patient's heart rate every few minutes, a weather station logs rainfall rates, a chemist measures a reaction rate at scattered times. No formula exists — only data.
  • The trapezoidal rule is the standard tool for turning a table of rate measurements into a total. That's why AP loves it: it's the honest, practical face of integration.

1.single trapezoid
A trapezoid standing on the x-axis has a left side of height 4, a right side of height 6, and width 2. Find its area.

Worked solution

Background: a trapezoid's area is the average of its two parallel sides times the width: A = ½(y_left + y_right)·Δx. It works because a trapezoid is a rectangle whose height is the average of the two edge heights.
A = ½(4 + 6)(2) = ½(10)(2) = 10.
Check with the average-height idea: average height = 5, width = 2, so 5 × 2 = 10 ✓.

2.single trapezoid
A function has f(1) = 3 and f(3) = 7. Use ONE trapezoid to estimate ∫₁³ f(x) dx.

Worked solution

Background: one trapezoid needs just three numbers: the height at the left end, the height at the right end, and the width of the interval. The width here is the length of [1, 3], which is 3 − 1 = 2 (end minus start, just like rectangle widths in Lesson 22).
Heights: y_left = f(1) = 3, y_right = f(3) = 7. Width: Δx = 3 − 1 = 2.
Estimate = ½(3 + 7)(2) = ½(10)(2) = 10.
∫₁³ f(x) dx ≈ 10
3.single trapezoid · motion
A car's velocity is measured as v(0) = 20 m/s and v(10) = 30 m/s. Use one trapezoid to estimate the distance traveled from t = 0 to t = 10 seconds, and say what your answer means.

Worked solution

Background: from Lessons 1 and 12: distance = area under the velocity graph = ∫ v(t) dt. With only two measurements, our best simple estimate is a single trapezoid — it assumes the velocity changed steadily (a straight line) between the readings.
Estimate = ½(20 + 30)(10) = ½(50)(10) = 250.
Units: (m/s) × s = meters ✓.
≈ 250 meters — the approximate distance traveled in those 10 seconds
4.exact for lines!
Use TWO trapezoids (Δx = 1) to estimate ∫₀² x dx, then compute the true value with the FTC. What do you notice, and why does it happen?

Worked solution

Background: a trapezoid's top is a straight line. If the curve itself IS a straight line (like y = x), the slanted top doesn't just hug the curve — it lies exactly ON it, so there is no error at all.
Heights: f(0) = 0, f(1) = 1, f(2) = 2.
Trapezoids: ½(0 + 1)(1) + ½(1 + 2)(1) = 0.5 + 1.5 = 2.
True value: ∫₀² x dx = x²/2 |₀² = 4/2 − 0 = 2.
They match exactly! The trapezoid tops sit right on the line y = x, so the trapezoids fill the region perfectly. The trapezoidal rule is exact for straight-line functions.
Both give 2 — trapezoids are exact when the graph is a straight line
5.equal widths
Use two trapezoids (Δx = 0.5) to estimate ∫₀¹ x² dx. Compare with the true value 1/3. Too big or too small?

Worked solution

Background: the slice edges are x = 0, 0.5, 1, and the heights come from y = x². True value from the FTC (Lesson 8): ∫₀¹ x² dx = x³/3 |₀¹ = 1/3 ≈ 0.333.
Heights: f(0) = 0, f(0.5) = 0.25, f(1) = 1.
Trapezoid 1: ½(0 + 0.25)(0.5) = 0.0625. Trapezoid 2: ½(0.25 + 1)(0.5) = 0.3125.
Total = 0.0625 + 0.3125 = 0.375.
0.375 > 0.333… — an overestimate, as expected: x² is concave up (cup shape), so the chords sit above the curve.
≈ 0.375, an overestimate of 1/3
6.shortcut
For a certain function on [0, 4] with equal-width slices, the left Riemann sum is 10 and the right Riemann sum is 14. What is the trapezoid sum?

Worked solution

Background: for EQUAL widths, each trapezoid is the average of the left rectangle and the right rectangle on its slice — so the whole trapezoid sum is the average of the whole left sum and the whole right sum: T = (L + R)/2.
T = (10 + 14)/2 = 24/2 = 12.

Worked solution

9.verify the shortcut
For ∫₀³ x² dx with three slices (Δx = 1): (a) compute the left sum and right sum; (b) average them; (c) compute the trapezoid sum slice by slice and confirm it matches.

Worked solution

Background: Lesson 22 sums: the left sum uses heights at each slice's left edge, the right sum uses the right edges. The claim to verify: with equal widths, T = (L + R)/2 — not just approximately, but exactly.
Heights at x = 0, 1, 2, 3: 0, 1, 4, 9.
(a) Left sum = (0 + 1 + 4)(1) = 5. Right sum = (1 + 4 + 9)(1) = 14.
(b) Average = (5 + 14)/2 = 19/2 = 9.5.
(c) Trapezoids: ½(0+1)(1) + ½(1+4)(1) + ½(4+9)(1) = 0.5 + 2.5 + 6.5 = 9.5 ✓ — a perfect match.
(For the curious: the true value is 3³/3 = 9, so 9.5 overestimates — x² is concave up.)
L = 5, R = 14, average = 9.5 = trapezoid sum ✓

12.shortcut · backwards
For equal-width slices on [0, 6], the trapezoid sum is 20 and the left sum is 17. (a) Find the right sum. (b) Why would this reasoning FAIL if the slices had unequal widths?

Worked solution

Background: T = (L + R)/2 is an equation with three players — know any two, solve for the third. It's exactly the "average of two numbers" relationship: if the average of 17 and R is 20, R must sit as far above 20 as 17 sits below.
(a) 20 = (17 + R)/2 → 40 = 17 + R → R = 23.
(b) The shortcut came from pairing each slice's left rectangle with the SAME slice's right rectangle, both with the SAME width Δx, and averaging. With unequal widths, each height gets multiplied by different widths in the left and right sums, so averaging the two totals no longer averages each slice correctly. You'd have to go trapezoid by trapezoid.
(a) right sum = 23   (b) the slice-by-slice averaging needs one common width
13.concavity
The curve y = x² is concave up on [0, 2]. (a) Explain, using chords, whether a trapezoidal sum for ∫₀² x² dx overestimates or underestimates. (b) Check your answer against the numbers from the notes (trapezoid sum 2.75, true value 8/3).

Worked solution

Background: concave up = bends like a cup ∪ (slope increasing). A trapezoid's top is a chord — a straight segment joining two points on the curve. For a concave-up curve, the curve sags BELOW each chord, like a rope hanging between two posts.
(a) Each trapezoid top (chord) lies above the curve, so each trapezoid contains a bit MORE area than the region under the curve → the sum overestimates.
(b) Check: 2.75 > 8/3 ≈ 2.667 ✓ — the estimate is indeed too big.
Overestimate — chords sit above a concave-up curve; 2.75 > 2.667 confirms it
14.concavity + direction
On [1, 4], the function f(x) = √x is increasing and concave down. Without computing anything, state whether each estimate of ∫₁⁴ √x dx is an overestimate or underestimate, with a one-line reason each: (a) left sum, (b) right sum, (c) trapezoid sum.

Worked solution

Background: two SEPARATE rules, don't mix them up. Left/right rectangles care about increasing vs decreasing (Lesson 22): for an increasing function, left edges are the short edges. Trapezoids care about concavity: chords vs curve.
(a) Left sum: f is increasing, so each left-edge height is the smallest on its slice → rectangles sit under the curve → underestimate.
(b) Right sum: right-edge heights are the biggest on each slice → rectangles poke above → overestimate.
(c) Trapezoid sum: f is concave down, so the curve bulges above its chords → trapezoid tops sit below the curve → underestimate.
(a) under (increasing)   (b) over (increasing)   (c) under (concave down)
15.four-way showdown
For ∫₀² x² dx with just TWO slices (Δx = 1), compute (a) the left sum, (b) the right sum, (c) the trapezoid sum, (d) the midpoint sum. (e) The true value is 8/3 ≈ 2.667 — rank the four estimates from closest to farthest.

Worked solution

Background: the midpoint sum (Lesson 22) uses the height at the CENTER of each slice: midpoints of [0,1] and [1,2] are 0.5 and 1.5. This showdown reveals a famous fact: midpoint is usually about twice as accurate as trapezoid, and they err on opposite sides.
Heights at edges: f(0) = 0, f(1) = 1, f(2) = 4. At midpoints: f(0.5) = 0.25, f(1.5) = 2.25.
(a) L = (0 + 1)(1) = 1.
(b) R = (1 + 4)(1) = 5.
(c) T = ½(0+1)(1) + ½(1+4)(1) = 0.5 + 2.5 = 3. (Shortcut check: (1+5)/2 = 3 ✓.)
(d) M = (0.25 + 2.25)(1) = 2.5.
(e) Errors from 8/3 ≈ 2.667: M is off by ≈ 0.167 (under), T by ≈ 0.333 (over), L by ≈ 1.667, R by ≈ 2.333.
L = 1, R = 5, T = 3, M = 2.5; ranking: midpoint, trapezoid, left, right

Water is pumped into a tank at rate R(t) liters per minute, measured at selected times:
t (min)04101520
R(t) (L/min)5912106
(a) Estimate ∫₀²⁰ R(t) dt with a LEFT Riemann sum using the four subintervals in the table. (b) Estimate ∫₀²⁰ R(t) dt with a trapezoidal sum using the same subintervals. (d) amount at t = 20 ≈ starting amount + water added ≈ 50 + 186 = 236 liters.
