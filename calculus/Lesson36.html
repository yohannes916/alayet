Lesson 36 — Arc Length

How Long Is a Curvy Path? · Infinitely Many Tiny Hypotenuses · Lesson 36 of 40

📚 All lessons← Lesson 35Next: Lesson 37 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: measure the LENGTH of a curve — not the area under it, but how far you'd walk if you traveled along it. We'll build the formula L = ∫ₐᵇ √(1 + (f′(x))²) dx from nothing but Pythagoras and slicing, check it against a triangle you already trust, and learn the honest truth: most arc-length integrals can't be finished by hand, so setting them up perfectly is the real skill.

1 The question: how long is a curvy path?

  • A hiking trail wiggles across a map. A cable sags between two poles. A roller-coaster track dives and climbs. Question: how many meters of trail / cable / track is that?
  • This is NOT area. Area asks "how much region is trapped under the curve?" Arc length asks "if an ant walked along the curve itself, how far would it walk?" — the length of the wire, not the space below it.
  • Straight paths are easy — a ruler (or the distance formula) handles them. Curves are the problem: no ruler bends. But we own a machine for exactly this situation: chop the hard thing into tiny pieces that ARE easy, then integrate.

2 Building the formula — tiny hypotenuses

  • Pythagoras refresher (our only tool): in a right triangle with legs a and b, the diagonal (hypotenuse) c satisfies a² + b² = c², so c = √(a² + b²). Legs 3 and 4 → diagonal √(9 + 16) = 5.
  • Now zoom WAY in on a tiny piece of the curve. Zoomed in far enough, any smooth curve looks straight — a tiny slanted segment. It runs a tiny distance dx across and a tiny distance dy up. It's the hypotenuse of a tiny right triangle!
zoomed in on one tiny piece: ___——● ●——‾‾ | tiny piece | dy (tiny rise) of curve | ●——————————————● dx (tiny run) tiny length = √(dx² + dy²) ← Pythagoras on a tiny triangle
  • Tiny length: √(dx² + dy²). Now the key algebra move — factor dx² out from under the root, slowly:
    √(dx² + dy²) = √(dx²·(1 + dy²/dx²)) = √(dx²) · √(1 + dy²/dx²) = √(1 + (dy/dx)²) · dx.
    Why do this? Because dy/dx is something we KNOW — it's the derivative, f′(x)!
  • So each tiny piece has length √(1 + (f′(x))²) dx, and the whole curve is the sum of all the pieces:
  • Arc length formula: L = ∫ₐᵇ √(1 + (f′(x))²) dx — "add up infinitely many tiny hypotenuses."
  • Recipe: (1) find f′, (2) square it, (3) add 1, (4) put it all under a square root, (5) integrate from a to b. Steps 1–4 are just algebra; step 5 is where the drama lives.

3 Sanity check on a straight line — calculus agrees with Pythagoras

Worked example: f(x) = (3/4)x on [0, 4] — a straight ramp from (0, 0) to (4, 3).

f′(x) = 3/4  →  (f′)² = 9/16  →  1 + 9/16 = 25/16.
L = ∫₀⁴ √(25/16) dx = ∫₀⁴ (5/4) dx = (5/4)·4 = 5.

Now check with pure geometry: the path runs 4 across and 3 up — a 3-4-5 right triangle. Hypotenuse = 5. ✓ The formula reproduces Pythagoras exactly. Always run this kind of check when you meet a new formula: make it re-prove something you already trust.
  • Notice why it worked so smoothly: for a straight line, f′ is a constant, so the square root is a constant, and the integral is just (constant) × (width). Curves are harder only because f′ keeps changing.

4 The honest truth — and the famous clean family

  • Here's a secret teachers eventually admit: most arc-length integrals are impossible by hand. "1 + (f′)²" under a square root is usually a mess with no elementary antiderivative — even for innocent curves like y = x² or y = sin x.
  • So textbook (and AP) problems come in two flavors: (1) curves specially designed so the mess under the root simplifies, and (2) setup-only problems — write the integral, then hand it to a calculator. Both are honorable. AP loves both.
  • The most famous designed curve: f(x) = (2/3)x^(3/2). Watch the magic:
Worked example: f(x) = (2/3)x^(3/2) on [0, 3].

f′(x) = (2/3)·(3/2)x^(1/2) = x^(1/2) = √x  →  (f′)² = x  →  1 + (f′)² = 1 + x.
The square of a square root un-did itself — the root becomes √(1 + x), which we CAN integrate (Lesson 17's linear-inside rule: inside has slope 1, so just use the power rule on the whole package):

L = ∫₀³ √(1 + x) dx = [(2/3)(1 + x)^(3/2)]₀³ = (2/3)(4^(3/2) − 1^(3/2)) = (2/3)(8 − 1) = 14/3.

5 A messier cousin — and exact-form courage

Worked example: f(x) = x^(3/2) on [0, 4].

f′ = (3/2)x^(1/2)  →  (f′)² = (9/4)x  →  1 + (f′)² = 1 + 9x/4.
L = ∫₀⁴ √(1 + 9x/4) dx. Linear inside with slope 9/4, so divide by it:
L = [(4/9)·(2/3)(1 + 9x/4)^(3/2)]₀⁴ = (8/27)[(1 + 9x/4)^(3/2)]₀⁴.
At x = 4: 1 + 9 = 10. At x = 0: 1. So L = (8/27)(10^(3/2) − 1).
  • And now... we stop. 10^(3/2) = 10√10 is not a nice number, and that is fine. Exact-form courage: an exact answer like (8/27)(10√10 − 1) is BETTER than a rounded decimal — it's the true value, not an approximation. Leave it. (If someone needs decimals, that's what calculators are for: ≈ 9.07.)

6 The setup-only norm — write it and STOP

  • Standard exam request: "Write an integral for the length of y = sin x on [0, π]. Do not evaluate."
  • Run the recipe: f′ = cos x → (f′)² = cos²x → answer: L = ∫₀^π √(1 + cos²x) dx. Then STOP. That square root has no elementary antiderivative — no technique in this course (or any course!) will produce one, because none exists. This is normal, not failure.
  • On the AP exam, a calculator evaluates such integrals numerically (this one ≈ 3.82). Your job is the part calculators can't do: producing the correct integral.
  • Self-check habit for any arc-length answer: the length must be at least the straight-line distance between the endpoints (shortest path is a straight line), and the integrand √(1 + (f′)²) is always ≥ 1, so L is always ≥ the width of the interval. If your answer is smaller than either — something broke.
✏️ Exercises — 20 problems (write your answer on paper, then reveal)
1.straight-line check
Use the arc length formula on f(x) = (3/4)x over [0, 8]. Then verify your answer with the distance formula between the endpoints.

Worked solution

Background: the smartest first move with any new formula is to test it where you already know the answer. A straight segment's length comes from the distance formula √((Δx)² + (Δy)²) — the arc length integral must match it exactly.
f′ = 3/4, so 1 + (f′)² = 1 + 9/16 = 25/16, and √(25/16) = 5/4.
L = ∫₀⁸ (5/4) dx = (5/4)(8) = 10.
Check: endpoints (0, 0) and (8, 6). Distance = √(8² + 6²) = √100 = 10. ✓ (A scaled-up 3-4-5 triangle.)
L = 10
2.straight-line check
Find the length of f(x) = (12/5)x over [0, 5] with the arc length formula, and name the famous right triangle hiding in the answer.

Worked solution

Background: 3-4-5 isn't the only "Pythagorean triple" (whole-number right triangle). The next famous one is 5-12-13: 5² + 12² = 25 + 144 = 169 = 13². A line of slope 12/5 walked 5 units across rises 12 — that exact triangle.
f′ = 12/5 → (f′)² = 144/25 → 1 + 144/25 = 169/25 → √ = 13/5.
L = ∫₀⁵ (13/5) dx = (13/5)(5) = 13.
Endpoints (0,0) and (5,12): legs 5 and 12, hypotenuse 13. ✓
L = 13 — the 5-12-13 triangle
3.constant slope
Find the exact length of f(x) = 3x + 1 over [0, 2]. (The answer won't be a whole number — leave it exact.)

Worked solution

Background: two lessons in one small problem. First: the +1 shifts the line up but can't change its length (watch f′ eat the constant). Second: not every exact answer is pretty — √10 is a perfectly finished answer. Exact-form courage starts here.
f′ = 3 (the +1 vanished — constants have derivative 0). 1 + (f′)² = 10.
L = ∫₀² √10 dx = 2√10.
Check: endpoints (0, 1) and (2, 7): √(4 + 36) = √40 = 2√10. ✓
L = 2√10
4.the clean family
Find the length of f(x) = (2/3)x^(3/2) over [0, 8].

Worked solution

Background: the (2/3)x^(3/2) curve is designed so the mess disappears: its derivative is √x, and squaring a square root gives back plain x. Then 1 + x under the root is the linear-inside pattern from Lesson 17 — power rule on the package, no dividing needed since the inside slope is 1.
f′ = √x → 1 + (f′)² = 1 + x.
L = ∫₀⁸ √(1 + x) dx = [(2/3)(1 + x)^(3/2)]₀⁸.
At x = 8: (2/3)(9^(3/2)) = (2/3)(27). At x = 0: (2/3)(1). L = (2/3)(27 − 1) = 52/3.
L = 52/3
5.setup only
Write — but do not evaluate — an integral for the length of y = x² over [0, 2].

Worked solution

Background: the parabola is the poster child for "innocent curve, impossible arc length." The setup takes ten seconds; the evaluation takes techniques beyond this course (the exact answer involves logarithms of square roots). Setup-only is the intended skill.
f′ = 2x → (f′)² = 4x².
L = ∫₀² √(1 + 4x²) dx. Stop here.
L = ∫₀² √(1 + 4x²) dx
6.constants don't count
Without computing anything new, find the length of f(x) = (2/3)x^(3/2) + 7 over [0, 8], and explain how you know.

Worked solution

Background: adding a constant slides the whole curve straight up — like lifting a piece of rope 7 cm higher off the table. The rope doesn't stretch! The formula agrees: the +7 dies when we take f′, and f′ is the only thing the integrand sees.
f′ = √x — identical to #4's derivative, so the integrand √(1 + x) is identical, so the length is identical.
L = 52/3, same as #4.
L = 52/3 — vertical shifts never change arc length
7.clean family · new interval
Find the length of f(x) = (2/3)x^(3/2) over [3, 8].

Worked solution

Background: same curve as #4, different window — only the limits change. Careful with the lower limit: it's no longer 0, so BOTH endpoint evaluations matter. (Quick sense check from #4: [0,8] gave 52/3 ≈ 17.3, so [3,8] must give less.)
L = ∫₃⁸ √(1 + x) dx = [(2/3)(1 + x)^(3/2)]₃⁸.
At x = 8: (2/3)(9^(3/2)) = (2/3)(27). At x = 3: (2/3)(4^(3/2)) = (2/3)(8).
L = (2/3)(27 − 8) = (2/3)(19) = 38/3 ≈ 12.7 < 52/3. ✓
L = 38/3
8.setup only
Write — but do not evaluate — an integral for the length of y = eˣ over [0, 1].

Worked solution

Background: eˣ is its own derivative (Lesson 12's star fact) — the one function whose slope equals its height. Squaring it: (eˣ)² = e²ˣ, by the exponent rule (add exponents when multiplying: x + x = 2x).
f′ = eˣ → (f′)² = e²ˣ.
L = ∫₀¹ √(1 + e²ˣ) dx. Stop — no elementary antiderivative in our toolkit.
L = ∫₀¹ √(1 + e²ˣ) dx
9.setup only
Write — but do not evaluate — an integral for the length of y = ln x over [1, e].

Worked solution

Background: the derivative of ln x is 1/x (Lesson 13). Its square is 1/x². Keep the algebra tidy under the root — 1 + 1/x² could also be written (x² + 1)/x², but either form earns full credit on a setup.
f′ = 1/x → (f′)² = 1/x².
L = ∫₁ᵉ √(1 + 1/x²) dx. Stop.
L = ∫₁ᵉ √(1 + 1/x²) dx
10.simplify the root
A curve has derivative f′(x) = √(x² + 2x). Simplify 1 + (f′)² until the square root disappears, then find the curve's length over [0, 3].

Worked solution

Background: designed problems hide a PERFECT SQUARE under the root: 1 + (f′)² collapses to (something)², and √(something)² = something (when it's positive). Spotting perfect squares like x² + 2x + 1 = (x + 1)² is the whole game.
(f′)² = x² + 2x, so 1 + (f′)² = x² + 2x + 1 = (x + 1)².
√((x + 1)²) = x + 1 (positive on [0, 3], so no absolute-value worry).
L = ∫₀³ (x + 1) dx = [x²/2 + x]₀³ = 9/2 + 3 = 15/2.
L = 15/2
11.exact-form courage
Find the exact length of f(x) = x^(3/2) over [0, 1]. Leave your answer in exact form — no decimals.

Worked solution

Background: same family as the notes' [0, 4] example, smaller window. The linear-inside rule (Lesson 17) handles √(1 + 9x/4): power rule on the package, then divide by the inside's slope 9/4 — dividing by 9/4 is multiplying by 4/9.
f′ = (3/2)√x → (f′)² = (9/4)x → integrand √(1 + 9x/4).
L = [(4/9)(2/3)(1 + 9x/4)^(3/2)]₀¹ = (8/27)[(1 + 9x/4)^(3/2)]₀¹.
At x = 1: (13/4)^(3/2) = 13^(3/2)/4^(3/2) = 13√13/8. At x = 0: 1.
L = (8/27)(13√13/8 − 1) = 13√13/27 − 8/27 = (13√13 − 8)/27. Done — that IS the answer.
L = (13√13 − 8)/27
12.arc vs. chord
The parabola y = x² runs from (0, 0) to (2, 4). (a) Find the straight-line distance between those endpoints. (b) Is the arc length of the parabola between them bigger or smaller than that? Explain without computing the integral.

Worked solution

Background: the straight segment between two points (the "chord") is the shortest possible path between them — that's practically the definition of a straight line. Any curve connecting the same two points must be at least as long, and strictly longer if it bends at all.
(a) Distance = √((2−0)² + (4−0)²) = √(4 + 16) = √20 = 2√5 ≈ 4.47.
(b) Bigger. The parabola bows away from the chord (it starts flat and steepens), so the ant walking the curve takes a detour — arc length > 2√5. (Calculator: ≈ 4.65, indeed bigger.)
(a) 2√5   (b) the arc is longer — a straight line is the shortest path
13.setup only · trig
Write — but do not evaluate — an integral for the length of y = cos x over [0, π/2], and say in one sentence why we stop at the setup.

Worked solution

Background: derivative of cos x is −sin x (Lesson 19). Squaring kills the minus sign: (−sin x)² = sin²x. The resulting integrand √(1 + sin²x) is famous for having NO elementary antiderivative — mathematicians proved none exists (it's called an elliptic integral).
f′ = −sin x → (f′)² = sin²x.
L = ∫₀^(π/2) √(1 + sin²x) dx.
We stop because no function built from powers, trig, exponentials, and logs has this as its derivative — a calculator evaluates it numerically (≈ 1.91).
L = ∫₀^(π/2) √(1 + sin²x) dx — no elementary antiderivative exists
14.perimeter
A triangular garden plot is bounded by the ramp y = (3/4)x, the x-axis, and the vertical line x = 4. Find the total length of fence needed to surround it (all three sides).

Worked solution

Background: perimeter = the whole boundary. Arc length handles the slanted/curvy edges; ordinary rulers handle flat and vertical edges. Real problems mix both — add all the pieces at the end.
Slanted edge: f′ = 3/4 → integrand √(25/16) = 5/4 → L = ∫₀⁴ (5/4) dx = 5.
Bottom edge along the x-axis from x = 0 to 4: length 4.
Right edge from (4, 0) up to (4, 3): length 3.
Total fence = 5 + 4 + 3 = 12.
Perimeter = 12
15.spot the error
Asked for the length of f(x) = (3/4)x on [0, 4], a student writes L = ∫₀⁴ √((f′)²) dx = ∫₀⁴ (3/4) dx = 3. Find the error, explain what the student actually computed, and give the correct answer.

Worked solution

Background: the "1" in √(1 + (f′)²) is not decoration — it's the dx² leg of the tiny triangle, the HORIZONTAL travel. Drop it and √((f′)²) = |f′| just integrates the slope, which accumulates only the vertical climb (that's Lesson 28's total-change idea, not length).
The student computed ∫₀⁴ |f′| dx = total RISE of the curve: the path climbs 3 units. True — but that ignores the 4 units of sideways walking.
Correct: L = ∫₀⁴ √(1 + 9/16) dx = ∫₀⁴ (5/4) dx = 5.
Sanity: an answer of 3 is even shorter than the 4-unit-wide interval — impossible, since the integrand √(1 + (f′)²) ≥ 1 forces L ≥ 4.
The 1 was dropped; 3 is only the vertical rise. Correct: L = 5
16.given f′
A curve has derivative f′(x) = √(4x + 8). Find its length over [0, 4].

Worked solution

Background: arc length only ever asks for f′, never f itself — so a problem may hand you the derivative directly. Then 1 + (f′)² = 1 + (4x + 8) = 4x + 9, and √(4x + 9) is a linear-inside integral: power rule on the package, divide by the inside slope 4.
L = ∫₀⁴ √(4x + 9) dx = [(1/4)(2/3)(4x + 9)^(3/2)]₀⁴ = (1/6)[(4x + 9)^(3/2)]₀⁴.
At x = 4: 25^(3/2) = (√25)³ = 125. At x = 0: 9^(3/2) = 27.
L = (1/6)(125 − 27) = 98/6 = 49/3.
L = 49/3
17.trap the answer
Without evaluating L = ∫₀^π √(1 + cos²x) dx (the length of y = sin x on [0, π]), prove that L is between π and π√2.

Worked solution

Background: Lesson 28's comparison property: if m ≤ (integrand) ≤ M on [a, b], then m·(b − a) ≤ integral ≤ M·(b − a). Even unsolvable integrals can be TRAPPED between bounds — an extremely AP-flavored move that turns "impossible" into "known to one decimal place."
cos²x ranges between 0 and 1, so the integrand ranges between √(1 + 0) = 1 and √(1 + 1) = √2.
Lower bound: L ≥ 1·(π − 0) = π. Upper bound: L ≤ √2·π.
So π ≤ L ≤ π√2, i.e. roughly 3.14 ≤ L ≤ 4.44. (True value ≈ 3.82 — safely inside.)
1 ≤ √(1 + cos²x) ≤ √2 forces π ≤ L ≤ π√2
18.why shifts do nothing
(a) Using the formula, explain precisely why f(x) + C has the same arc length as f(x) on any interval. (b) The curve g(x) = f(x − 2) is f slid 2 units right. Explain why g on [2, 7] has the same length as f on [0, 5].

Worked solution

Background: arc length should behave like real rope: sliding a rope around a table — up, down, sideways — without bending it can't change its length. A good formula must respect that physical fact, and checking that it does is how mathematicians stress-test definitions.
(a) The integrand only contains f′, and (f + C)′ = f′ (the constant's derivative is 0). Identical integrand, identical limits → identical length. Geometrically: every tiny hypotenuse gets lifted by C but keeps its exact size and tilt.
(b) g′(x) = f′(x − 2) (the slope at x on g is the slope at the matching point x − 2 on f). As x runs over [2, 7], the value x − 2 runs over [0, 5] — so the integral ∫₂⁷ √(1 + f′(x − 2)²) dx visits exactly the same slope values as ∫₀⁵ √(1 + f′(u)²) du, tiny piece for tiny piece. Same hypotenuses, same total.
Shifts (vertical or horizontal) preserve every tiny hypotenuse — length unchanged
19.perfect-square family
Find the length of f(x) = x³/6 + 1/(2x) over [1, 2]. (Hint: compute (f′)² very carefully — the cross term is the hero.)

Worked solution

Background: a famous family of curves is engineered so that (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b² has cross term 2ab = 1/2; then adding 1 flips the middle sign and produces (a + b)² — a perfect square, and the root melts away. Watch it happen.
f′ = 3x²/6 − 1/(2x²) = x²/2 − 1/(2x²).
(f′)² = (x²/2)² − 2·(x²/2)(1/(2x²)) + (1/(2x²))² = x⁴/4 − 1/2 + 1/(4x⁴).
1 + (f′)² = x⁴/4 + 1/2 + 1/(4x⁴) = (x²/2 + 1/(2x²))² — same pieces, plus sign in the middle!
L = ∫₁² (x²/2 + 1/(2x²)) dx = [x³/6 − 1/(2x)]₁² = (8/6 − 1/4) − (1/6 − 1/2) = (4/3 − 1/4) + 1/3 = 5/3 − 1/4 = 17/12.
L = 17/12
20.challenge · the classic
Find the length of f(x) = x²/4 − (ln x)/2 over [1, 2]. Show every algebra step — this is the most famous "designed" arc length problem in calculus.

Worked solution

Background: the full perfect-square machine, now with a logarithm on board. Needed tools: derivative of ln x is 1/x (Lesson 13), the expansion (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b², and the antiderivative of 1/x being ln x. Every step is one you own — the beauty is how they interlock.
Step 1 — derivative: f′ = 2x/4 − (1/2)(1/x) = x/2 − 1/(2x).
Step 2 — square it: (f′)² = (x/2)² − 2·(x/2)·(1/(2x)) + (1/(2x))². The cross term: 2·(x/2)·(1/(2x)) = x/(2x) = 1/2. So (f′)² = x²/4 − 1/2 + 1/(4x²).
Step 3 — add 1: 1 + (f′)² = x²/4 + 1/2 + 1/(4x²). And since (x/2 + 1/(2x))² = x²/4 + 1/2 + 1/(4x²) (the same cross term, now added), the pile under the root is exactly (x/2 + 1/(2x))².
Step 4 — the root melts: √(1 + (f′)²) = x/2 + 1/(2x) (positive on [1, 2] — both terms are positive).
Step 5 — integrate: L = ∫₁² (x/2 + 1/(2x)) dx = [x²/4 + (ln x)/2]₁² = (4/4 + (ln 2)/2) − (1/4 + (ln 1)/2) = (1 + (ln 2)/2) − 1/4.
ln 1 = 0, so L = 3/4 + (ln 2)/2 ≈ 0.75 + 0.35 = 1.10. Sanity: endpoints (1, 1/4) and (2, 1 − (ln 2)/2 ≈ 0.65); chord ≈ √(1 + 0.16) ≈ 1.08 ≤ 1.10. ✓
L = 3/4 + (ln 2)/2
