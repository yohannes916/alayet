Lesson 12 — Motion II: Acceleration & Gravity

Integrating twice: from acceleration to velocity to position · Lesson 12 of 40

📚 All lessons← Prev: Lesson 11Next: Lesson 13 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: in Lesson 11 you integrated velocity to get position. Now we go one level deeper: acceleration tells how fast the velocity changes, so integrating acceleration gives velocity — and integrating again gives position. By the end you'll understand falling balls, thrown balls, and braking cars, and you'll see that the famous physics formulas are just integrals in disguise.

1 Acceleration, from scratch

  • You know velocity: how fast your position is changing, measured in meters per second (m/s).
  • Acceleration is one level up: it's how fast your VELOCITY is changing. Pressing a car's gas pedal doesn't teleport you forward — it makes your speedometer needle climb. That climbing is acceleration.
  • Units: if your velocity grows by 4 m/s during every second, your acceleration is "4 m/s per second", written 4 m/s². The little ² just means "per second, twice" — meters per second, per second.
  • So acceleration is the derivative of velocity — and that means, running our machine backwards, velocity is the integral of acceleration.
  • Here is the full chain, which this whole lesson lives on:
acceleration a(t) --integrate--> velocity v(t) --integrate--> position s(t) each arrow needs a STARTING VALUE: starting velocity v₀ for the first arrow, starting position s₀ for the second.
  • Why the starting values? Remember Lesson 5: an integral only tells you the change. If your velocity changed by +12 m/s, you still need to know where the speedometer started to know where it ends. Same for position.

2 Constant acceleration, worked slowly

Example: a cart accelerates at a steady a = 4 m/s², and it's already moving at 2 m/s when we start the clock.

Step 1 — velocity. The change in velocity up to time t is the integral of acceleration: ∫0t 4 du = 4t. Add the starting velocity:
v(t) = 2 + 4t  (velocity = start + integral of a). Check: at t = 0 it gives 2 ✓, and it grows by 4 every second ✓.

Step 2 — position. Now integrate the velocity (Lesson 11): distance traveled in the first t seconds = ∫0t (2 + 4u) du = 2t + 2t². If the cart starts at position 0, then s(t) = 2t + 2t².

After 3 seconds: v(3) = 2 + 12 = 14 m/s, and s(3) = 6 + 18 = 24 meters. Two integrations, two starting values, whole story known.
  • That's the entire method. Everything else in this lesson is this method wearing different costumes.

3 Gravity — the acceleration you live with every day

  • Here's a physics fact you can test with a pencil: near the ground, everything that falls speeds up at about 9.8 m/s² — a rock, a phone, a bowling ball. Gravity pulls, and the speed of a falling object climbs by about 9.8 m/s every second. That number is called g.
  • In this course we'll round it to g = 10 m/s² to keep the arithmetic friendly (real scientists use 9.8; the method is identical).
  • A dropped ball (dropped = starts with velocity 0, measuring downward as positive):
    • Velocity: v(t) = 0 + ∫0t 10 du = 10t m/s.
    • Distance fallen: s(t) = ∫0t 10u du = 5t² meters.
  • So a dropped ball falls 5t² meters in t seconds, moving at 10t m/s. Memorize the pattern, but know it comes from two integrals.
Worked example: you drop a ball off a bridge. After 3 seconds: velocity = 10 × 3 = 30 m/s (about highway speed!), and it has fallen 5 × 3² = 5 × 9 = 45 meters. Notice how fast falling gets serious — that's why heights are dangerous.

4 Throwing a ball UP — the full story arc

  • Now let's call up the positive direction. Gravity pulls down, so it subtracts from your velocity: acceleration = −10 m/s².
  • Throw a ball straight up at 20 m/s. Velocity = start + integral of acceleration: v(t) = 20 − 10t.
  • Read that formula like a story:
    • Rising: while v > 0 the ball is still moving up. That's for t < 2.
    • The peak: at the very top the ball hangs for an instant with velocity exactly 0. Solve 20 − 10t = 0 → t = 2 seconds.
    • Falling: after t = 2, v is negative — moving downward.
  • How high did it go? Height gained = integral of velocity while rising: ∫02 (20 − 10t) dt = [20t − 5t²]02 = 40 − 20 = 20 → 20 meters.
  • When does it land? Height at time t is s(t) = 20t − 5t². Set it to 0: 20t − 5t² = 5t(4 − t) = 0 → t = 4 s. Exactly twice the peak time — the trip up and the trip down are mirror images.
v (m/s) 20 ● | \ v = 20 − 10t 10 | \ | \ peak: v = 0 at t = 2 0 +-------●--------------→ t (s) | \ −10 | \ negative v = falling −20 | ● 0 1 2 3 4
  • This is signed area from Lesson 9 in action: area above the axis (rising, +20 m) and area below the axis (falling, −20 m) cancel — the ball ends where it started.

5 The famous physics formulas are just integrals

  • Physics textbooks hand students two formulas to memorize. You can now build them yourself. Suppose acceleration is a constant a, starting velocity is v₀, starting position is s₀.
  • First integration (a → v): change in velocity = ∫0t a du = at, so v = v₀ + at.
  • Second integration (v → s): change in position = ∫0t (v₀ + au) du = v₀t + ½at², so s = s₀ + v₀t + ½at².
  • Sanity-check the dropped ball: v₀ = 0, s₀ = 0, a = 10 → v = 10t ✓ and s = ½ × 10 × t² = 5t² ✓. The "5" in 5t² is just ½ of g.
  • Moral: the physics formulas aren't magic — they're what integration produces. If you ever forget them, you can re-derive them in four lines.

6 Sanity habits (use these on every problem)

  • Carry units at every step. m/s² × s = m/s (acceleration times time gives velocity ✓). m/s × s = m (velocity times time gives meters ✓). If your units come out wrong, your setup is wrong.
  • Check by differentiating. Integration and differentiation undo each other (Lesson 4). If you claim s(t) = 2t + 2t², differentiate: slope = 2 + 4t — that should be your v(t). It is ✓. Differentiate again: 4 — that should be a ✓. Two free error checks!
  • Check t = 0. Your v(t) should equal v₀ at t = 0, and s(t) should equal s₀. Takes two seconds, catches most mistakes.
✏️ Exercises — 20 problems (write your answer on paper, then reveal)
1.a → v
A cart starts from rest (velocity 0) and accelerates at a steady 3 m/s². Write the velocity formula v(t), and find the velocity after 4 seconds.

Worked solution

Background: acceleration is the rate at which velocity changes, so velocity = starting velocity + the integral of acceleration. For a constant acceleration a, that integral is just a × t (a rectangle of height a and width t on the acceleration graph).
v(t) = v₀ + ∫0t 3 du = 0 + 3t = 3t.
At t = 4: v(4) = 3 × 4 = 12. Units: (m/s²) × s = m/s ✓.
v(t) = 3t; after 4 s the velocity is 12 m/s
2.a → v · with a start
A bike is already rolling at 2 m/s when its rider starts pedaling, adding a constant acceleration of 4 m/s². Write v(t) and find the velocity after 5 seconds.

Worked solution

Background: an integral only gives the CHANGE in velocity. To get the actual velocity you must add the starting velocity: v(t) = v₀ + ∫a. Forgetting the starting value is the #1 mistake in this lesson.
Change in velocity by time t: ∫0t 4 du = 4t.
v(t) = 2 + 4t. Check t = 0: v = 2 ✓.
v(5) = 2 + 20 = 22 m/s.
v(t) = 2 + 4t; after 5 s the velocity is 22 m/s
3.gravity · dropped
You drop a ball from a tall bridge (use g = 10 m/s²). How fast is it moving after 3 seconds, and how far has it fallen?

Worked solution

Background (physics): gravity makes every falling object speed up by about 10 m/s each second (really 9.8, rounded for friendly numbers). "Dropped" means starting velocity 0. Integrating twice gives the standard pair: v = 10t and distance fallen = 5t².
Velocity: v(3) = 10 × 3 = 30 m/s.
Distance: s(3) = 5 × 3² = 5 × 9 = 45 m.
30 m/s, having fallen 45 meters
4.gravity · dropped
A rock is dropped from a cliff. After 2 seconds, what is its speed and how far has it fallen? (g = 10 m/s²)

Worked solution

Background: for any dropped object (starting velocity 0), integrating the constant acceleration 10 gives v = 10t, and integrating that velocity gives distance fallen = 5t². These two formulas answer every "dropped object" question.
Speed: v(2) = 10 × 2 = 20 m/s.
Distance: s(2) = 5 × 2² = 5 × 4 = 20 m.
Fun coincidence: at t = 2 the two numbers match (20 and 20) — but only at t = 2!
20 m/s, having fallen 20 meters
5.v → s
A scooter's velocity is v(t) = 6t m/s (it started from rest with acceleration 6 m/s²). How far does it travel in the first 3 seconds?

Worked solution

Background: this is the second link of the chain: distance = integral of velocity (Lesson 11). The antiderivative of 6t is 3t², because the derivative of 3t² is 6t.
Distance = ∫03 6t dt = [3t²]03 = 3 × 9 − 0 = 27.
Units: (m/s) × s = m ✓.
27 meters
6.graph · Δv = area
An acceleration graph shows one flat step: a = 2 m/s² from t = 0 to t = 5 s. The object's starting velocity is 3 m/s. Use the area under the acceleration graph to find the velocity at t = 5.

Worked solution

Background: just as area under a velocity graph = change in position, area under an acceleration graph = change in velocity. Same "area under the rate graph" idea from Lesson 2, one level up the chain.
Area = width × height = 5 s × 2 m/s² = 10 m/s. That's the CHANGE in velocity, Δv.
Final velocity = starting velocity + change = 3 + 10 = 13 m/s.
13 m/s
7.how long to fall
A ball is dropped from an 80-meter tower (g = 10 m/s²). How long does it take to reach the ground, and how fast is it going when it lands?

Worked solution

Background: distance fallen = 5t². Here the distance is GIVEN (80 m) and time is the unknown — so set 5t² equal to 80 and solve backwards. Then plug that time into v = 10t for the landing speed.
5t² = 80 → t² = 16 → t = 4 seconds.
Landing speed: v(4) = 10 × 4 = 40 m/s.
Check: 5 × 4² = 5 × 16 = 80 ✓.
4 seconds, landing at 40 m/s
8.thrown up · full arc
A ball is thrown straight up at 30 m/s (g = 10 m/s², up = positive). (a) When does it reach its peak? (b) How high is the peak? (c) When does it land back at its starting height?

Worked solution

Background: with up as positive, gravity is a = −10, so v(t) = 30 − 10t. The peak is the instant the ball stops rising: v = 0. Peak height = integral of v from 0 to the peak time. It lands when its height s(t) = 30t − 5t² returns to 0.
(a) 30 − 10t = 0 → t = 3 seconds.
(b) Height = ∫03 (30 − 10t) dt = [30t − 5t²]03 = 90 − 45 = 45 meters.
(c) 30t − 5t² = 5t(6 − t) = 0 → t = 6 seconds — exactly double the peak time, since up and down are mirror images.
(a) t = 3 s   (b) 45 m high   (c) lands at t = 6 s
9.braking car
A car moving at 20 m/s slams the brakes, giving a constant acceleration of −5 m/s² (negative = slowing down). (a) Write v(t). (b) When does the car stop? (c) How far does it travel while stopping?

Worked solution

Background: braking is just negative acceleration — it subtracts from the velocity every second. The car "stops" when v = 0. Stopping distance = the integral of v from 0 to the stopping time (area of a triangle on the velocity graph).
(a) v(t) = 20 + (−5)t = 20 − 5t.
(b) 20 − 5t = 0 → t = 4 seconds.
(c) Distance = ∫04 (20 − 5t) dt = [20t − 2.5t²]04 = 80 − 40 = 40 meters. (Triangle check: ½ × 4 × 20 = 40 ✓.)
(a) v = 20 − 5t   (b) stops at t = 4 s   (c) 40 meters
10.braking car
A truck moving at 40 m/s brakes with acceleration −8 m/s². How long until it stops, and what is its stopping distance?

Worked solution

Background: same recipe as the last problem: v(t) = v₀ + at with a negative a; stop when v = 0; distance = integral of v up to that moment.
v(t) = 40 − 8t. Stops when 40 − 8t = 0 → t = 5 seconds.
Distance = ∫05 (40 − 8t) dt = [40t − 4t²]05 = 200 − 100 = 100 meters.
Triangle check: ½ × 5 × 40 = 100 ✓. Notice: double the speed of a 20 m/s car doesn't just double the stopping distance — it's much worse. That's why speeding is dangerous.
Stops in 5 seconds, after 100 meters
11.full chain a → v → s
An object has constant acceleration 2 m/s², starting velocity 3 m/s, and starting position 5 m. Build v(t), then s(t), then find s(4).

Worked solution

Background: this is the whole chain in one problem: integrate a (plus starting velocity) to get v, then integrate v (plus starting position) to get s. Two integrations, two starting values.
Velocity: v(t) = 3 + ∫0t 2 du = 3 + 2t.
Position: s(t) = 5 + ∫0t (3 + 2u) du = 5 + 3t + t².
s(4) = 5 + 12 + 16 = 33. Check t = 0: s = 5 ✓, v = 3 ✓.
v(t) = 3 + 2t; s(t) = 5 + 3t + t²; s(4) = 33 meters
12.thrown up · big throw
A toy rocket is launched straight up at 50 m/s (treat it as a thrown ball: a = −10 m/s² the whole time). Find the peak time, the peak height, and the landing time.

Worked solution

Background: the thrown-ball recipe: v(t) = v₀ − 10t; peak when v = 0; peak height = ∫ v from 0 to peak time; lands at double the peak time (starting and ending at the same height).
Peak: 50 − 10t = 0 → t = 5 s.
Peak height = ∫05 (50 − 10t) dt = [50t − 5t²]05 = 250 − 125 = 125 m.
Lands: s(t) = 50t − 5t² = 5t(10 − t) = 0 → t = 10 s (double the peak time ✓).
Peak at t = 5 s, height 125 m, lands at t = 10 s
13.reverse gravity
A phone slips from a balcony 45 meters above the ground (g = 10 m/s², and yes, it's doomed). How fast is it moving when it hits the ground?

Worked solution

Background: two-step reverse problem: first use distance fallen = 5t² to find WHEN it lands, then plug that time into v = 10t to find HOW FAST. You can't get the speed without the time first.
5t² = 45 → t² = 9 → t = 3 seconds.
v(3) = 10 × 3 = 30 m/s.
(30 m/s is about 67 miles per hour. Phone case recommended.)
30 m/s
14.graph · two steps
An acceleration graph is a staircase: a = 3 m/s² from t = 0 to t = 4 s, then a = 1 m/s² from t = 4 to t = 6 s. The object starts from rest. Find its velocity at t = 6 by adding rectangle areas.

Worked solution

Background: area under the acceleration graph = change in velocity. A staircase graph means two rectangles; add their areas just like the staircase speed graphs in Lesson 1. Careful: the second rectangle's width is 6 − 4 = 2, not 6.
Rectangle 1: 3 m/s² × 4 s = 12 m/s of velocity gained.
Rectangle 2: 1 m/s² × 2 s = 2 m/s gained.
v(6) = 0 + 12 + 2 = 14 m/s.
14 m/s
15.derive the formula
Derive the formula v = v₀ + at yourself: start from a constant acceleration a and starting velocity v₀, and explain each step of the integration.

Worked solution

Background: "derive" means: don't quote the formula — BUILD it. The only two ingredients are (1) velocity change = integral of acceleration, and (2) actual velocity = starting velocity + change.
Step 1: acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, so the total change in velocity from time 0 to time t is ∫0t a du.
Step 2: a is constant, so the antiderivative of a is au, and ∫0t a du = [au]0t = at − 0 = at. (Picture: a rectangle of height a, width t.)
Step 3: velocity = start + change: v = v₀ + at. Check by differentiating: derivative of v₀ + at is a ✓, and at t = 0 it gives v₀ ✓.
v = v₀ + at — built from one integral, nothing memorized
16.derive the formula
Now derive the position formula s = s₀ + v₀t + ½at² by integrating the velocity v(t) = v₀ + at you built in problem 15.

Worked solution

Background: second link of the chain: position change = integral of velocity. Integrate each piece of v₀ + au separately (the antiderivative of a constant v₀ is v₀u; the antiderivative of au is ½au², since the derivative of ½au² is au).
Change in position = ∫0t (v₀ + au) du = [v₀u + ½au²]0t = v₀t + ½at².
Add the starting position: s = s₀ + v₀t + ½at².
Check by differentiating: derivative = v₀ + at = v ✓. Check the dropped ball: s₀ = 0, v₀ = 0, a = 10 → s = ½(10)t² = 5t² ✓ — the famous falling formula is just this one in disguise.
s = s₀ + v₀t + ½at² — the physics formula is an integral
17.check by differentiating
A friend claims that an object with position s(t) = 5 + 3t + t² has velocity v(t) = 3 + 2t and acceleration 2 m/s². Verify both claims by differentiating, and explain why this works as a check.

Worked solution

Background: integration and differentiation undo each other (the Fundamental Theorem, Lesson 7). So you can verify any integration by differentiating the result: the derivative of position must give back velocity, and the derivative of velocity must give back acceleration.
Derivative of s = 5 + 3t + t²: the 5 gives 0, the 3t gives 3, the t² gives 2t → v = 3 + 2t ✓.
Derivative of v = 3 + 2t: the 3 gives 0, the 2t gives 2 → a = 2 ✓.
Both claims check out. This is the same object as problem 11 — and the check works because differentiating retraces each integration step backwards.
Both correct: s′ = 3 + 2t = v, and v′ = 2 = a
18.challenge · two-phase rocket
A model rocket slides along a horizontal track. Phase 1: its engine gives a = 6 m/s² for 5 seconds, starting from rest. Phase 2: the engine cuts out and it coasts at a constant velocity for 10 more seconds (ignore friction). Find its velocity at the end of phase 1, and the TOTAL distance covered over all 15 seconds.

Worked solution

Background: when the motion has phases with different rules, split the problem at the phase change (the splitting idea from Lesson 6). Work phase 1 fully — its FINAL velocity becomes phase 2's STARTING velocity. "Coasting" means a = 0, so velocity stays constant.
Phase 1 velocity: v(t) = 6t, so at t = 5, v = 30 m/s.
Phase 1 distance: ∫05 6t dt = [3t²]05 = 75 meters.
Phase 2 distance: constant 30 m/s × 10 s = 300 meters.
Total = 75 + 300 = 375 meters.
v = 30 m/s after phase 1; total distance 375 meters
19.challenge · where is it now?
A ball is thrown straight up at 30 m/s. You showed in problem 8 that it peaks at t = 3 (height 45 m) and lands at t = 6. How high is the ball at t = 4 seconds — and is it moving up or down at that moment?

Worked solution

Background: the height formula s(t) = 30t − 5t² works for the WHOLE flight, before and after the peak — you don't need separate formulas for up and down. To tell the direction of motion, check the sign of v(t) = 30 − 10t: positive means rising, negative means falling (Lesson 9's signed velocity).
Height: s(4) = 30 × 4 − 5 × 4² = 120 − 80 = 40 meters.
Velocity: v(4) = 30 − 40 = −10 m/s → negative, so it's falling.
Sense check: at t = 4 it's one second past the peak (t = 3, 45 m), so it should be a bit below 45 m and heading down. 40 m and falling ✓.
40 meters up, moving DOWNWARD at 10 m/s
20.challenge · speed up then brake
A go-kart starts from rest, accelerates at 4 m/s² for 5 seconds, then immediately brakes at −5 m/s² until it stops. Find (a) the top speed, (b) the total time of the trip, and (c) the total distance.

Worked solution

Background: a two-phase problem where BOTH phases have acceleration. Handle each phase with its own clock and its own starting velocity: phase 1 ends at some speed, and that speed is where phase 2 begins. Add the times and add the distances at the end.
(a) Phase 1: v = 4t, so top speed = 4 × 5 = 20 m/s.
Phase 1 distance: ∫05 4t dt = [2t²]05 = 50 meters.
(b) Phase 2 (new clock, v₀ = 20, a = −5): v = 20 − 5t → stops at t = 4. Total time = 5 + 4 = 9 seconds.
(c) Phase 2 distance: ∫04 (20 − 5t) dt = [20t − 2.5t²]04 = 80 − 40 = 40 meters. Total = 50 + 40 = 90 meters.
(a) 20 m/s   (b) 9 seconds   (c) 90 meters
