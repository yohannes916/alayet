1 The whole course on one page

3 How to attack ANY problem — the 5-question checklist

  • Every problem in this course (and most integration problems anywhere) surrenders to the same five questions:
  • 1. What's the rate? Find the thing measured "per second / per hour / per minute." That's the function going inside the integral. If you're given the total and asked for something else, you may be working backwards instead.
  • 2. What's the window? When does the accumulating start and stop? Those are your limits a and b. Width is always end − start.
  • 3. Is there a starting amount? An integral only gives the change. If the tank already held 30 liters or the ball started 5 meters up, add the starting amount to the integral (L19).
  • 4. Could it go negative? If the rate dips below zero (draining, falling, spending), decide whether you want the net change (one integral) or the total activity (split at the zero and add the sizes) — that's L9.
  • 5. Which antiderivative tool fits? Scan the toolbox table: plain power rule? A special function? Something linear inside? A u-sub pattern (inside function AND its derivative both visible)? Pick the tool, then check by differentiating.

4 One big worked "boss problem" — watch all the tools at once

The problem: A delivery drone's upward velocity is v(t) = 6t − t² meters per second, for 0 ≤ t ≤ 6 seconds. It starts slow, speeds up, peaks, and glides back to velocity 0 at t = 6. Questions: (a) when is it moving fastest, and how fast? (b) how far does it travel in total? (c) what's its average velocity? (d) if it starts at height s(0) = 2 m, what's its height at t = 6?
  • (a) Fastest moment — a derivative question! "Fastest" asks where the rate itself peaks, so we differentiate the velocity: v′(t) = 6 − 2t, which is 0 at t = 3. Then v(3) = 18 − 9 = 9. Fastest at t = 3 s, moving 9 m/s. (Notice: not every question about v is an integral — the checklist's question 1 saves you here.)
  • (b) Total distance — checklist in action. Rate: v(t). Window: 0 to 6. Negative? 6t − t² = t(6 − t) is zero at t = 0 and t = 6 and positive in between — never negative, so net = total. Tool: power rule + FTC:
    ∫₀⁶ (6t − t²) dt = [3t² − t³/3]₀⁶ = (108 − 72) − 0 = 36. The drone travels 36 meters.
  • (c) Average velocity (L14): average = total ÷ width = (1/6) × 36 = 6 m/s. Sanity check: v ranges from 0 up to 9 and back — an average of 6 sits sensibly inside that range.
  • (d) Height with a starting amount (L19): height = start + change = s(6) = 2 + 36 = 38 meters. (Or the antiderivative way: s(t) = 3t² − t³/3 + C, with s(0) = 2 forcing C = 2, so s(6) = 108 − 72 + 2 = 38. Same answer, of course.)
  • One function, four different questions, four different tools — and the checklist told us which was which every time.

5 What's next in calculus (a peek over the fence)

  • Here's the wonderful secret about the rest of calculus: it's the same slicing idea, aimed at new targets. Want the volume of a vase? Slice it into thin circular disks, find each disk's tiny volume, and integrate — area under a graph becomes "volume under a surface of stacked slices." Want the length of a curvy road? Chop it into tiny nearly-straight segments and add them up. And differential equations — the language physics and biology are written in — are really just "here's a rate rule; integrate cleverly to find the total story." Every one of these starts with the move you learned in Lesson 1: chop, multiply, add.

6 You did it

  • Look at what you can do now: you can read a rate and see a total hiding in it. You can turn a story about water, money, gravity, or waves into a definite integral, pick the right antiderivative from a toolbox of nine rules, and compute an exact answer that would have taken infinitely many rectangles the slow way. That is not "a taste of calculus" — that is the genuine core of integral calculus, the same ideas a college course spends a semester on. Be proud of it. Now go show it off on the exercises below — they mix everything, just like real life does.
✏️ Capstone Exercises — 20 mixed problems (write your answer on paper, then reveal)
1.L1 · rate × time
Back to where it all began: a garden hose runs at a steady 15 liters per minute for 4 minutes. How much water comes out? (No symbols needed — just the Lesson 1 idea.)

Worked solution

Tool: Lesson 1 (rate × time). When a rate never changes, the total is a single multiplication: total = rate × time. This is the seed the whole course grew from — every integral is secretly lots of these little products added up.
Total = 15 liters/minute × 4 minutes = 60.
Units check: (liters/minute) × (minutes) = liters. ✓
60 liters
2.L2 · area = total
A go-kart's speed climbs in a straight line from 0 m/s to 12 m/s over 6 seconds. Use geometry (no antiderivatives!) to find how far it travels.

Worked solution

Tool: Lesson 2 (area under the rate graph = total). The distance traveled equals the area under the speed graph. A speed rising in a straight line from 0 makes a triangle: base = time, height = final speed, area = ½ × base × height.
Base = 6 seconds, height = 12 m/s.
Area = ½ × 6 × 12 = 36. Units: seconds × meters/second = meters.
36 meters
3.L7 · FTC
Evaluate ∫₀³ (x² + 1) dx.

Worked solution

Tool: Lessons 5 & 7 (power rule + Fundamental Theorem). The FTC says: find an antiderivative F, then the definite integral is F(top) − F(bottom). Power rule: raise the exponent by 1, divide by the new exponent. A constant 1 integrates to x.
Antiderivative: F(x) = x³/3 + x.
F(3) = 27/3 + 3 = 9 + 3 = 12.   F(0) = 0.
Integral = 12 − 0 = 12.
12
4.L16 · cosine
Evaluate ∫₀^(π/2) cos x dx. (Radians, as always.)

Worked solution

Tool: Lesson 16 (trig antiderivatives). ∫ cos x dx = sin x + C, because the derivative of sin is cos. Table values you know: sin 0 = 0, sin(π/2) = 1.
Integral = [sin x] from 0 to π/2 = sin(π/2) − sin(0).
= 1 − 0 = 1.
1
5.L15 · eˣ
Evaluate ∫₀¹ eˣ dx, exactly and then rounded to two decimals (use e ≈ 2.718).

Worked solution

Tool: Lesson 15 (the special function eˣ). eˣ is the function that is its own derivative — so it's also its own antiderivative: ∫ eˣ dx = eˣ + C. Also remember e⁰ = 1.
Integral = [eˣ] from 0 to 1 = e¹ − e⁰ = e − 1.
Numerically: 2.718 − 1 = 1.718 ≈ 1.72.
e − 1 ≈ 1.72
6.L17 · linear inside
Evaluate ∫₀¹ (2x + 1)³ dx.

Worked solution

Tool: Lesson 17 (linear insides — divide by a). When the inside is a straight-line expression ax + b, integrate the outside as usual, keep the inside, and divide by the inside's slope a. Here a = 2.
Antiderivative: (2x + 1)⁴/4, then ÷ 2 → (2x + 1)⁴/8.
At x = 1: (3)⁴/8 = 81/8.   At x = 0: (1)⁴/8 = 1/8.
Integral = 81/8 − 1/8 = 80/8 = 10.
10
7.L9 · net vs total
A toy robot moves along a line with velocity v(t) = 6 − 2t m/s for 0 ≤ t ≤ 5. Find (a) its net displacement and (b) the total distance it actually travels.

Worked solution

Tool: Lesson 9 (signed area). When a velocity goes negative, the integral counts backward motion as NEGATIVE area, giving the net change in position. For the total distance, split the window where v = 0 and add the SIZES of the two pieces.
(a) Net: ∫₀⁵ (6 − 2t) dt = [6t − t²]₀⁵ = 30 − 25 = 5 m.
(b) v = 0 when 6 − 2t = 0 → t = 3. Forward part: ∫₀³ (6 − 2t) dt = 18 − 9 = 9 m. Backward part: ∫₃⁵ = 5 − 9 = −4 m (that is, 4 m backward).
Total distance = 9 + 4 = 13 m. (Net 5 m: it ends only 5 m ahead, but its wheels rolled 13 m.)
(a) net = 5 m   (b) total = 13 m
8.L10 · between curves
Find the area of the region trapped between y = 6x − x² and y = x².

Worked solution

Tool: Lesson 10 (area between curves). Area between curves = ∫ (top − bottom). First find where they cross (set them equal) — those crossings are your limits. Then check which curve is on top in between.
Crossings: 6x − x² = x² → 6x = 2x² → 2x(x − 3) = 0 → x = 0 and x = 3.
Top check at x = 1: 6(1) − 1 = 5 versus 1² = 1 → the parabola 6x − x² is on top.
Area = ∫₀³ (6x − x² − x²) dx = ∫₀³ (6x − 2x²) dx = [3x² − (2/3)x³]₀³ = 27 − 18 = 9.
9 square units
9.L12 · gravity (g = 10)
A ball is thrown straight up from the ground at 20 m/s. Using g = 10 m/s², find (a) when it reaches its highest point, (b) how high that is, and (c) when it lands back on the ground.

Worked solution

Tool: Lesson 12 (acceleration & gravity). Gravity pulls velocity down by 10 m/s every second, so v(t) = (start speed) − 10t. Height is the integral of velocity. The peak happens exactly when v = 0 (moving neither up nor down).
(a) v(t) = 20 − 10t = 0 → t = 2 seconds.
(b) Height = ∫₀² (20 − 10t) dt = [20t − 5t²]₀² = 40 − 20 = 20 m.
(c) Height function: h(t) = 20t − 5t² = 5t(4 − t) = 0 → t = 4 seconds (the up trip and down trip are mirror images: 2 s up, 2 s down).
(a) t = 2 s   (b) 20 m high   (c) lands at t = 4 s
10.L13 + L19 · tank
A tank starts with 30 liters. Water flows IN at 2t liters per minute (a growing rate) while a drain lets water OUT at a steady 4 liters per minute, for 0 ≤ t ≤ 6. How much water is in the tank at t = 6?

Worked solution

Tools: Lesson 13 (accumulation with in-and-out rates) and Lesson 19 (starting amounts). The tank feels only the NET rate: in − out. The integral of the net rate gives the CHANGE; then add the starting amount: final = start + ∫ net rate.
Net rate = 2t − 4 liters per minute.
Change = ∫₀⁶ (2t − 4) dt = [t² − 4t]₀⁶ = 36 − 24 = 12 liters gained.
Final amount = 30 + 12 = 42 liters. (Fun detail: the net rate is negative until t = 2, so the tank actually dips to 30 − 4 = 26 L first, then refills and climbs.)
42 liters
11.L14 · average value
A machine's power use is P(t) = 12t − 3t² kilowatts over the interval 0 ≤ t ≤ 4 hours. Find its average power use over those 4 hours.

Worked solution

Tool: Lesson 14 (average value). The average value of f on [a, b] is (1/(b − a)) ∫ₐᵇ f — that is, the total accumulated divided by the width of the window. It's the one steady level that would accumulate the same total.
Total: ∫₀⁴ (12t − 3t²) dt = [6t² − t³]₀⁴ = 96 − 64 = 32 kilowatt-hours.
Average = (1/4) × 32 = 8 kilowatts.
Sanity check: P starts at 0, peaks at P(2) = 24 − 12 = 12, ends at 0 — an average of 8 sits reasonably inside [0, 12]. ✓
8 kilowatts
12.L15 · 1/x
Evaluate ∫₁^e (5/x) dx. (e is the number ≈ 2.718; here it's the top limit.)

Worked solution

Tool: Lesson 15 (the special function 1/x). The power rule fails for 1/x (it would divide by zero!), so 1/x has its own rule: ∫ 1/x dx = ln x + C. Key values: ln 1 = 0 and ln e = 1 (ln asks "e to what power?").
Integral = [5 ln x] from 1 to e = 5 ln e − 5 ln 1.
= 5(1) − 5(0) = 5.
5
13.L19 · initial value
A function satisfies f′(x) = 3x² − 4 and f(1) = 5. Find the formula for f(x), and then compute f(2).

Worked solution

Tools: Lesson 4 (antiderivatives + C) and Lesson 19 (initial values). Integrating f′ recovers f only up to an unknown constant C — infinitely many parallel curves have the same slope pattern. One known point (the initial value) pins down which curve is yours: plug it in and solve for C.
Antiderivative: f(x) = x³ − 4x + C.
Use f(1) = 5: 1 − 4 + C = 5 → C = 8. So f(x) = x³ − 4x + 8.
f(2) = 8 − 8 + 8 = 8.
f(x) = x³ − 4x + 8, and f(2) = 8
14.L13 · story → integral
Translate, then solve: "A bakery sells bread at a rate of 30 + 10t loaves per hour, where t is hours after opening. The bakery is open from t = 0 to t = 8." Write the definite integral for the total loaves sold, then evaluate it.

Worked solution

Tools: Lesson 6 (integral notation) and Lesson 13 (real-world accumulation). Translating a story: the "per hour" thing is the rate → it goes inside the integral; the open-to-close times are the limits. Total = ∫ (rate) over the window.
Setup: total loaves = ∫₀⁸ (30 + 10t) dt.
Antiderivative: 30t + 5t². Evaluate: (30·8 + 5·64) − 0 = 240 + 320 = 560.
∫₀⁸ (30 + 10t) dt = 560 loaves
15.L18 · u-substitution
Evaluate ∫₀² x(x² + 1)³ dx.

Worked solution

Tool: Lesson 18 (u-substitution). Spot an "inside function" whose derivative also appears as a factor. Here the inside is u = x² + 1, and du = 2x dx — and we DO have an x dx (that's du/2). Change the limits to u-values too: no converting back needed.
Let u = x² + 1, so du = 2x dx, meaning x dx = du/2.
Limits: x = 0 → u = 1; x = 2 → u = 5.
Integral = ∫₁⁵ u³ (du/2) = (1/2)[u⁴/4]₁⁵ = (1/8)(625 − 1) = 624/8 = 78.
78
16.reverse · find the time
A tank already holds 15 liters. Water pours in at a rate of 6t liters per minute. The tank is full at exactly 90 liters. At what time T is the tank full?

Worked solution

Tools: Lessons 13 & 19, run backwards. Usually you know the time window and compute the total. Here the total is GIVEN and the end time is the unknown — so write the amount as a function of T (start + integral up to T), set it equal to the target, and solve the equation.
Amount at time T: 15 + ∫₀ᵀ 6t dt = 15 + [3t²]₀ᵀ = 15 + 3T².
Set it to 90: 15 + 3T² = 90 → 3T² = 75 → T² = 25 → T = 5 (time can't be negative).
Check: 15 + 3(25) = 15 + 75 = 90. ✓
T = 5 minutes
17.two-phase rate
A cyclist accelerates smoothly with v(t) = 4t m/s for the first 2 seconds, then holds a steady 8 m/s from t = 2 until t = 10. How far does she travel in the whole 10 seconds?

Worked solution

Tools: Lesson 1's chop-and-add applied to integrals (with Lessons 7 & 11). When a rate follows different formulas on different stretches, split the integral at the changeover and add the pieces — exactly the Lesson 1 move, with an integral for each piece. (Nice check: 4t at t = 2 gives 8, matching the steady phase, so the speed doesn't jump.)
Phase 1: ∫₀² 4t dt = [2t²]₀² = 8 meters.
Phase 2: steady rate → 8 m/s × (10 − 2) s = 64 meters. (Same as ∫₂¹⁰ 8 dt = 80 − 16 = 64.)
Total = 8 + 64 = 72 meters.
72 meters
18.spot the error
Maya computes: ∫₀² (3x + 1)² dx = [(3x + 1)³/3]₀² = 343/3 − 1/3 = 114. Her answer is wrong. Find her exact mistake, then compute the correct value — and verify it a second way by multiplying out (3x + 1)² first.

Worked solution

Tool: Lesson 17 (linear insides — divide by a). With an inside function 3x + 1, the chain rule of derivatives sneaks a ×3 in — so the antiderivative must divide by that 3 to cancel it. Forgetting the ÷a is the single most common integration error. Always check by differentiating your answer!
The error: Maya divided by the new exponent 3 but forgot to ALSO divide by the inside slope a = 3. Check her answer by differentiating: d/dx[(3x+1)³/3] = 3(3x+1)²·3/3 = 3(3x + 1)² — three times too big.
Correct way: antiderivative = (3x + 1)³/(3·3) = (3x + 1)³/9. Evaluate: 7³/9 − 1³/9 = (343 − 1)/9 = 342/9 = 38.
Second check: (3x + 1)² = 9x² + 6x + 1, so ∫₀² = [3x³ + 3x² + x]₀² = 24 + 12 + 2 = 38. ✓ Same answer, so 38 is right (and 114 = 3 × 38 is exactly 3 times too big, matching the missing ÷3).
Error: forgot to divide by the inside slope 3. Correct value: 38
19.compare scenarios
Two pumps fill identical empty tanks. Pump A delivers a steady 12 liters per minute. Pump B starts slow but speeds up: 3t liters per minute. (a) After 10 minutes, which tank holds more, and by how much? (b) At what time (after t = 0) do the two tanks hold exactly the same amount?

Worked solution

Tool: Lesson 13 (accumulation), twice. Comparing scenarios = computing two accumulations with the same window and comparing. For the tie time, write each total as a function of T and set them equal — steady rates win early, growing rates win late, so there's a crossover.
(a) Tank A: 12 × 10 = 120 L. Tank B: ∫₀¹⁰ 3t dt = [1.5t²]₀¹⁰ = 150 L. B holds more, by 150 − 120 = 30 L.
(b) Totals at time T: A has 12T, B has 1.5T². Set equal: 12T = 1.5T² → 12 = 1.5T (dividing by T since T ≠ 0) → T = 8.
Check: at T = 8, A has 96 L and B has 1.5 × 64 = 96 L. ✓ Before t = 8 the steady pump leads; after, the accelerating one takes over for good.
(a) Tank B, by 30 liters   (b) tied at T = 8 minutes (96 L each)
20.grand finale 🚀
The rocket launch (multi-part — take it in stages!) A model rocket starts at rest on the ground. Its engine burns for 4 seconds, producing a steady upward acceleration of 5 m/s². Then the engine cuts out and gravity takes over (g = 10 m/s²). Find: (a) the rocket's velocity when the engine cuts out; (b) its height at that moment; (c) the time and height of its highest point; (d) its average velocity during the 4-second powered climb; (e) its height 2 seconds after the peak.

Worked solution

Tools, by part: (a) Lesson 12 (integrate acceleration to get velocity) · (b) Lesson 11 (integrate velocity to get position) · (c) Lessons 12 & 19 (gravity phase with initial values, peak where v = 0) · (d) Lesson 14 (average value) · (e) Lesson 12 again (falling from rest). A two-phase problem like Lesson 1's chopped trips — solve one phase, carry its ending values into the next as starting values.
(a) Powered phase, velocity: starts at rest, so v(4) = ∫₀⁴ 5 dt = 5 × 4 = 20 m/s.
(b) Powered phase, height: v(t) = 5t during the burn, so height = ∫₀⁴ 5t dt = [2.5t²]₀⁴ = 40 m. (It's the triangle ½ × 4 × 20 = 40 — Lesson 2 agrees!)
(c) Coasting phase: new clock s = t − 4. Starting values: velocity 20 m/s, height 40 m. Gravity: v = 20 − 10s, which is 0 at s = 2, i.e. t = 6 seconds. Extra height gained: ∫₀² (20 − 10s) ds = [20s − 5s²]₀² = 40 − 20 = 20 m. Peak height = 40 + 20 = 60 m.
(d) Average velocity while powered: average = total ÷ width = (1/4) ∫₀⁴ 5t dt = 40 ÷ 4 = 10 m/s — sensible, exactly halfway between the starting 0 and the ending 20, because the speed grew in a straight line.
(e) Falling from the peak: at the peak the velocity is 0, so in the next 2 seconds it falls ∫₀² 10s ds = [5s²]₀² = 20 m. Height = 60 − 20 = 40 m — right back to its engine-cutout height, at t = 8 s.
(a) 20 m/s   (b) 40 m   (c) peak at t = 6 s, 60 m high   (d) 10 m/s   (e) 40 m
