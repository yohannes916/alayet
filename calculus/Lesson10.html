Lesson 10 — Area Between Two Curves

Area Between Two Curves · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 10 of 20

📚 All lessons← Prev: Lesson 9Next: Lesson 11 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: learn to find the area of the region between two graphs — not just under one. The whole trick fits in one line: at every x the strip's height is top minus bottom, so you integrate ∫ (top − bottom) dx. Plus: how to figure out which curve is on top, and how to find where curves cross when nobody tells you the limits.

1 A new question — the gap between two graphs

  • So far, every area problem was one graph and the floor (the x-axis): area under a curve = total accumulated. Today the question changes: what's the area of the region between TWO graphs?
  • Picture two runners. The fast one's speed graph sits above the slow one's speed graph. The region between the two graphs isn't decoration — it's the lead that opens up between them. Fast runner's area = fast runner's distance; slow runner's area = slow runner's distance; the strip between the graphs = the difference = how far ahead the fast runner gets.
  • This "gap between two accumulations" shows up everywhere: two faucets filling different amounts, money coming in vs money going out, one car pulling away from another.

2 The idea: height of the strip = top − bottom

  • Think in rectangles first (like Lesson 1). At some x, the top curve has height top(x) and the bottom curve has height bottom(x). A skinny rectangle standing between them has height top(x) − bottom(x) — not the full height from the floor, just the gap.
  • Add up all the skinny rectangles and you get the familiar move: area between = ∫ (top − bottom) dx.
  • Another way to see the same thing: big area minus small area. The area under the top curve includes the whole region down to the floor. Subtract the area under the bottom curve, and what's left is exactly the strip between them:
top curve | ____________ | / \ | / ← the strip → \ | _____________________ | / bottom curve \ | / \ +----------------------------→ x area of strip = (area under top) − (area under bottom) = ∫ top dx − ∫ bottom dx = ∫ (top − bottom) dx
  • Those two views are the same because integrals let you subtract inside: ∫ top dx − ∫ bottom dx = ∫ (top − bottom) dx.

3 The recipe (3 steps, every time)

  • Step 1 — Who's on top? Pick a test x between the limits and plug it into both functions. The bigger answer belongs to the top curve. Don't guess from the formulas — test a point!
  • Step 2 — What are the limits? If the problem gives you "from x = a to x = b", great. If it says "the region enclosed by the two curves", the limits are where the curves cross: set the two functions equal to each other and solve. Those crossing points are your a and b.
  • Step 3 — Integrate the gap: area = ∫ab (top − bottom) dx, using antiderivatives and the Fundamental Theorem exactly like Lessons 6–8.
Why "top − bottom" and never the reverse: the gap top − bottom is a positive height, so the integral comes out positive — a real area. If you accidentally do bottom − top, every strip's height goes negative and you get the same number with a minus sign. If your "area" comes out negative, you subtracted in the wrong order — flip it.

4 Worked example with flat lines (check it with a rectangle)

Problem: find the area between y = 6 and y = 2 from x = 0 to x = 5.

Step 1 — top? 6 > 2 everywhere, so y = 6 is the top and y = 2 is the bottom.
Step 2 — limits? Given: 0 to 5.
Step 3 — integrate the gap: ∫05 (6 − 2) dx = ∫05 4 dx = 4x |05 = 20 − 0 = 20.

Check with plain geometry: the region is a rectangle — width 5, height 6 − 2 = 4 — so area = 4 × 5 = 20 ✓. The calculus recipe agrees with what your eyes already know. That's how you build trust in a new tool.

5 Worked example with a curve: y = x and y = x²

Problem: find the area of the region enclosed by y = x and y = x2.

Step 2 first (find the limits) — nobody gave us limits, so find the crossings: set x = x2, so x2 − x = 0, so x(x − 1) = 0 → they cross at x = 0 and x = 1.
Step 1 — who's on top between 0 and 1? Test x = ½: the line gives 0.5, the parabola gives (½)² = 0.25. So y = x is on top (0.5 > 0.25).
Step 3 — integrate the gap:
y 1 | ✕ ← they cross again at (1, 1) | / . | / . line y = x (top) | / ▒ . curve y = x² (bottom) | / ▒ . ▒ = the enclosed sliver | / .. 0 ✕------------------→ x 0 1
∫01 (x − x2) dx. Antiderivative (power rule, Lesson 5): x2/2 − x3/3. (Check by differentiating: derivative of x²/2 is x ✓, derivative of x³/3 is x² ✓.)
Plug in the top limit: 1/2 − 1/3 = 3/6 − 2/6 = 1/6. Plug in the bottom limit: 0 − 0 = 0.
Area = 1/6 − 0 = 1/6. A thin little sliver — and the numbers agree: it should be small.

6 Why "the floor" doesn't matter

  • Worry you might have: "what if both curves are way up high — doesn't all that empty space under them mess things up?" No — and here's why: the strip only cares about the gap, not the distance to the floor.
  • Try it: the area between y = 103 and y = 100 from x = 0 to 2 is ∫02 (103 − 100) dx = ∫02 3 dx = 6 — exactly the same as the area between y = 3 and y = 0. The "100" cancels in the subtraction.
  • Same with curves: between y = x2 + 100 and y = x2, the x² cancels and the gap is a constant 100. Sliding both curves up or down together doesn't change the strip between them — the subtraction erases whatever they share.
  • This even rescues negative territory: if both curves dip below the x-axis, top − bottom is still the honest positive gap, so the recipe keeps working without any Lesson-9-style sign headaches — as long as you keep the true top on top.
✏️ Exercises — 20 problems (write your answer on paper, then reveal)
1.flat lines
Find the area between the horizontal lines y = 8 and y = 3 from x = 0 to x = 4. Then check your answer with the rectangle formula.

Worked solution

Background: two horizontal lines make the simplest possible "between" region: a rectangle. The strip's height at every x is top − bottom, so the recipe says integrate that constant gap. It should match width × height exactly.
Top: y = 8 (since 8 > 3 everywhere). Gap = 8 − 3 = 5.
∫04 5 dx = 5x |04 = 20 − 0 = 20.
Rectangle check: width 4, height 5 → 4 × 5 = 20 ✓.
20 square units
2.flat lines
Find the area between y = 10 and y = 4 from x = 1 to x = 7.

Worked solution

Background: same rectangle idea, but watch the width — the region runs FROM x = 1 TO x = 7, so the width is 7 − 1 = 6, not 7. Width is always (right end − left end), just like rectangle widths in Lesson 1.
Top is y = 10, bottom is y = 4, gap = 10 − 4 = 6.
∫17 6 dx = 6x |17 = 42 − 6 = 36.
Rectangle check: width 6 × height 6 = 36 ✓.
36 square units
3.parallel lines
Find the area between the slanted lines y = x + 5 and y = x + 1 from x = 0 to x = 3.

Worked solution

Background: these two lines are parallel (same slope, 1), like two lanes of a slanted road. Even though BOTH lines climb, the gap between them never changes — and the recipe automatically notices that when you subtract.
Test x = 0: top candidate gives 5, other gives 1 → y = x + 5 is on top.
Gap = (x + 5) − (x + 1) = 4. The x's cancel — constant gap!
∫03 4 dx = 4x |03 = 12. (It's a slanted strip, but slanting a strip sideways doesn't change its area — think of a stack of cards pushed over.)
12 square units
4.line vs line
Find the area between y = x + 2 and y = x from x = 0 to x = 4. Do the two lines ever cross?

Worked solution

Background: to check whether two graphs cross, set them equal and try to solve. If the equation turns into something impossible, they never meet — which is exactly what parallel lines do.
Crossing check: x + 2 = x → 2 = 0, which is impossible. They never cross; y = x + 2 stays on top forever.
Gap = (x + 2) − x = 2.
∫04 2 dx = 2x |04 = 8.
Area = 8; the lines never cross
5.which is on top?
Between x = 0 and x = 1, which is on top: y = x or y = x2? And between x = 1 and x = 3, which is on top? (Test points — no integrating needed.)

Worked solution

Background: squaring a number between 0 and 1 makes it SMALLER (half of a half is a quarter), but squaring a number bigger than 1 makes it BIGGER. So these two graphs swap places at x = 1. Testing one point inside each stretch is the reliable way to see who's on top there.
Test x = ½ (between 0 and 1): line → 0.5, parabola → 0.25. The line y = x is on top.
Test x = 2 (between 1 and 3): line → 2, parabola → 4. Now y = x² is on top.
They trade places exactly at the crossing points x = 0 and x = 1 (where x = x²). That's why the recipe says to find crossings first — the "top" can change!
On (0, 1): y = x is on top. On (1, 3): y = x² is on top.
6.two runners
Runner A runs at a steady 8 m/s and runner B at a steady 5 m/s, both starting together. After 10 seconds, how big is A's lead? Explain why this is an area-between-two-curves question.

Worked solution

Background (physics): a speed of 8 m/s means 8 meters get added to your distance every second (Lesson 1: distance = area under the speed graph). Two runners give two speed graphs. The area between the graphs = A's distance − B's distance = the LEAD, i.e., the gap that opens up between them on the track.
Both speed graphs are horizontal lines: A at height 8 (top), B at height 5 (bottom).
Lead = ∫010 (8 − 5) dt = ∫010 3 dt = 3t |010 = 30.
Sanity check: A ran 80 m, B ran 50 m, difference 30 m ✓ — big area minus small area.
30 meters
7.line under a roof
Find the area between y = 6 (top) and y = 2x from x = 0 to x = 3. Then check your answer using the triangle area formula.

Worked solution

Background: the line y = 2x starts at the floor and climbs until it hits the roof y = 6 exactly at x = 3 (since 2 × 3 = 6). The region between them is a triangle with the right angle at the top-left — so geometry can double-check the calculus. Triangle area = ½ × base × height.
Top check at x = 1: roof gives 6, line gives 2 → the roof y = 6 is on top on [0, 3].
∫03 (6 − 2x) dx = (6x − x2) |03. (Check: derivative of 6x − x² is 6 − 2x ✓.)
At 3: 18 − 9 = 9. At 0: 0. Area = 9.
Triangle check: base 3, height 6 → ½ × 3 × 6 = 9 ✓.
9 square units
8.curve vs roof
Find the area between y = 4 and y = x2 from x = 0 to x = 2. (Notice the parabola reaches the roof exactly at x = 2.)

Worked solution

Background: the parabola y = x² climbs from 0 and touches the horizontal line y = 4 at x = 2 (because 2² = 4). Between x = 0 and x = 2 the region is the curvy space under the roof and above the bowl — a shape no rectangle or triangle formula handles. That's exactly what the integral recipe is for.
Top check at x = 1: roof → 4, parabola → 1 → y = 4 is on top.
∫02 (4 − x2) dx = (4x − x3/3) |02. (Check: derivative of 4x − x³/3 is 4 − x² ✓.)
At 2: 8 − 8/3 = 24/3 − 8/3 = 16/3. At 0: 0.
16/3 square units (≈ 5.33)
9.find the crossings
Find the area of the region enclosed by y = 2x and y = x2. (No limits given — you find them!)

Worked solution

Background: "the region enclosed by" means the pocket trapped between the two graphs. Its left and right edges are where the graphs CROSS — so step one is solving top = bottom. Setting the functions equal finds every x where the two graphs share a point.
Crossings: 2x = x² → x² − 2x = 0 → x(x − 2) = 0 → x = 0 and x = 2.
Top check at x = 1: line → 2, parabola → 1 → y = 2x is on top between the crossings.
∫02 (2x − x2) dx = (x2 − x3/3) |02 = (4 − 8/3) − 0 = 12/3 − 8/3 = 4/3.
4/3 square units
10.two runners, one speeding up
Runner A's speed is v = 2t m/s (speeding up) and runner B's speed is v = t m/s (also speeding up, but slower). Both start together. How far ahead is A after 4 seconds?

Worked solution

Background (physics): when speeds change over time, "how far ahead" is still the area between the two speed graphs — but now the graphs are slanted lines instead of flat ones, so we integrate instead of multiplying. The gap in speeds at time t is 2t − t = t, and integrating that gap accumulates the lead second by second.
Top check at t = 1: A → 2, B → 1 → A's graph is on top for t > 0.
Lead = ∫04 (2t − t) dt = ∫04 t dt = t2/2 |04 = 16/2 = 8.
Check the long way: A went ∫2t = t² → 16 m; B went ∫t = t²/2 → 8 m; difference 8 m ✓.
8 meters ahead
11.two faucets
Faucet A pours water at a steady 6 liters per minute. Faucet B starts at zero and pours at t liters per minute at time t (it opens up gradually). From t = 0 to t = 6 minutes, how many MORE liters does A deliver than B?

Worked solution

Background: a flow rate (liters per minute) is like a speed for water — total water = area under the rate graph (Lesson 1). "How many more liters" is the difference of two totals, which is the area between the two rate graphs. Note B's rate reaches 6 exactly at t = 6, so on this whole interval A's graph stays on top.
Top check at t = 2: A → 6, B → 2 → A on top on [0, 6].
Extra water = ∫06 (6 − t) dt = (6t − t2/2) |06 = (36 − 18) − 0 = 18.
Check: A delivered 6 × 6 = 36 L; B delivered ∫t = t²/2 → 18 L; 36 − 18 = 18 ✓.
18 liters more
12.revenue vs cost
A lemonade stand earns money at a steady rate of R = 12 dollars per day. Its costs grow: it spends at a rate of C = 2t dollars per day at time t. What is the total profit from t = 0 to t = 5 days?

Worked solution

Background (money): revenue rate = dollars flowing IN per day; cost rate = dollars flowing OUT per day. Profit accumulates at the rate (revenue − cost), so total profit = area between the revenue graph and the cost graph. Note the cost rate 2t only reaches 10 at day 5, still below 12, so revenue stays on top the whole time.
Top check at t = 1: revenue → 12, cost → 2 → revenue on top on [0, 5].
Profit = ∫05 (12 − 2t) dt = (12t − t2) |05. (Check: derivative of 12t − t² is 12 − 2t ✓.)
At 5: 60 − 25 = 35. At 0: 0.
$35 profit
13.crossing lines
The lines y = 2x and y = 8 − 2x cross somewhere. Find the crossing point, then find the area between the lines from x = 0 up to that crossing.

Worked solution

Background: one line climbs (y = 2x) and one falls (y = 8 − 2x), so they must cross exactly once — like two hikers walking toward each other. Set them equal to find where. Before the crossing, the falling line starts higher, so it's the top.
Crossing: 2x = 8 − 2x → 4x = 8 → x = 2 (both give y = 4 there).
Top check at x = 0: falling line → 8, climbing line → 0 → y = 8 − 2x is on top on [0, 2].
∫02 ((8 − 2x) − 2x) dx = ∫02 (8 − 4x) dx = (8x − 2x2) |02 = (16 − 8) − 0 = 8.
They cross at x = 2; area = 8 square units
14.the floor doesn't matter
Find the area between y = x2 + 10 and y = x2 + 7 from x = 0 to x = 4 — and explain why you never actually had to integrate x².

Worked solution

Background: both curves are the same parabola, one slid up 10 and one slid up 7 — like two identical hills, one stacked 3 units above the other. The strip between two graphs depends only on the GAP, not on how high either one floats above the floor. Whatever the two formulas share cancels in the subtraction.
Top check at x = 0: 10 vs 7 → y = x² + 10 is on top (it's higher at every x, since the +10 beats the +7).
Gap = (x² + 10) − (x² + 7) = 3. The x² terms cancel completely — that's why no x² integration is needed.
∫04 3 dx = 3x |04 = 12.
12 square units — the x² cancels because both curves share it
15.crossings on both sides
Find the area of the region enclosed by y = 4 and y = x2. (Careful: the parabola crosses the line in TWO places, one of them negative.)

Worked solution

Background: the parabola y = x² is a symmetric bowl. A horizontal line slices the bowl on both sides of the y-axis, because squaring a negative gives a positive: (−2)² = 4 just as 2² = 4. So when you solve x² = 4, keep BOTH square roots. The enclosed region is the cap of the bowl between the two crossings.
Crossings: x² = 4 → x = −2 and x = 2.
Top check at x = 0: line → 4, parabola → 0 → y = 4 is on top between the crossings.
∫−22 (4 − x2) dx = (4x − x3/3) |−22.
At 2: 8 − 8/3 = 16/3. At −2: −8 − (−8/3) = −8 + 8/3 = −16/3.
Area = 16/3 − (−16/3) = 32/3. (Symmetry check: it's twice problem 8's answer of 16/3 ✓ — the left half mirrors the right half.)
32/3 square units (≈ 10.67)
16.curves that touch
Show that y = x2 + 1 and y = 2x touch at exactly one point, decide which is on top everywhere else, and find the area between them from x = 0 to x = 2.

Worked solution

Background: when you set two curves equal and the equation has a DOUBLE root (a perfect square like (x − 1)² = 0), the curves don't cross — they kiss at one point and separate again, like a ball resting on a ramp. The same curve stays on top on both sides, and the "gap" briefly shrinks to zero without ever going negative.
Set equal: x² + 1 = 2x → x² − 2x + 1 = 0 → (x − 1)² = 0 → only x = 1. One touching point at (1, 2).
Top check at x = 0: parabola → 1, line → 0. At x = 2: parabola → 5, line → 4. The parabola is on top on both sides of the touch.
∫02 (x2 + 1 − 2x) dx = (x3/3 − x2 + x) |02. (Check: derivative is x² − 2x + 1 ✓.)
At 2: 8/3 − 4 + 2 = 8/3 − 2 = 2/3. At 0: 0.
They touch only at (1, 2); area = 2/3 square units
17.runner catch-up · challenge
Runner A runs at a steady 6 m/s. Runner B starts from rest with speed v = 2t m/s. (a) At what time do their SPEEDS become equal? (b) How big is A's lead at that moment? (c) At what time does B actually CATCH UP to A?

Worked solution

Background (physics): two different questions hide here. Speeds equal = the graphs cross (set 6 = 2t). But B catching up in POSITION means the lead shrinks back to zero — the area between the graphs where A is on top (before the crossing) gets exactly cancelled by the area where B is on top (after). The lead is biggest at the moment the speeds match, because until then A is still pulling away.
(a) Speeds equal: 6 = 2t → t = 3 seconds.
(b) Lead at t = 3: A on top before then (test t = 1: 6 vs 2). ∫03 (6 − 2t) dt = (6t − t2) |03 = 18 − 9 = 9 meters.
(c) Catch-up when total lead = 0: ∫0T (6 − 2t) dt = 6T − T2 = 0 → T(6 − T) = 0 → T = 6 seconds.
Check at t = 6: A has run 6 × 6 = 36 m; B has run ∫2t = t² → 36 m ✓. Dead even.
(a) t = 3 s   (b) 9 m lead   (c) B catches A at t = 6 s
18.enclosed by two curves
Find the area of the region enclosed by y = x and y = x3 in the first quadrant (where x ≥ 0).

Worked solution

Background: cubing acts like squaring did in problem 5: numbers between 0 and 1 get smaller when cubed ((½)³ = ⅛), so the cubic dips below the line there, and they meet again at x = 1 where 1³ = 1. "First quadrant" just means we only look at the pocket where x ≥ 0.
Crossings (x ≥ 0): x = x³ → x³ − x = 0 → x(x² − 1) = 0 → x = 0 and x = 1.
Top check at x = ½: line → 0.5, cubic → 0.125 → y = x is on top on (0, 1).
∫01 (x − x3) dx = (x2/2 − x4/4) |01 = (1/2 − 1/4) − 0 = 1/4. (Check: derivative of x²/2 − x⁴/4 is x − x³ ✓.)
1/4 square unit
19.top changes mid-way · challenge
Find the TOTAL area between y = x and y = x2 from x = 0 all the way to x = 2. (Warning: the curves cross inside this interval, and the top switches!)

Worked solution

Background: this is the between-curves version of Lesson 9's net-vs-total idea. If the top switches inside your interval and you integrate one single (top − bottom) the whole way, the two parts partly cancel — like mixing positive and negative area. For TRUE total area: split at the crossing, keep the correct top on each piece, and add the (positive) pieces.
Crossing inside (0, 2): x = x² → x = 1. Piece 1 is [0, 1], piece 2 is [1, 2].
Piece 1, top = x (test ½): ∫01 (x − x2) dx = (x2/2 − x3/3) |01 = 1/2 − 1/3 = 1/6.
Piece 2, top = x² (test x = 1.5: 2.25 vs 1.5): ∫12 (x2 − x) dx = (x3/3 − x2/2) |12 = (8/3 − 2) − (1/3 − 1/2) = 2/3 − (−1/6) = 2/3 + 1/6 = 5/6.
Total = 1/6 + 5/6 = 1. (If you'd integrated (x − x²) straight across [0, 2] you'd get 1/6 − 5/6 = −2/3 — a cancelled, meaningless "net".)
Total area = 1 square unit exactly
20.grand challenge
Find the area of the region enclosed by y = x2 and y = x + 2. Do all three recipe steps yourself: find the crossings, test who's on top, then integrate.

Worked solution

Background: the full recipe with nothing given. To find where a parabola meets a line, set them equal and solve the quadratic by factoring: get everything on one side, then look for two numbers that multiply to the constant and add to the middle coefficient. Expect two crossings — a line generally slices through a bowl in two places.
Step 2 (crossings): x² = x + 2 → x² − x − 2 = 0 → (x − 2)(x + 1) = 0 → x = −1 and x = 2.
Step 1 (top, test x = 0 between them): line → 0 + 2 = 2, parabola → 0 → the line y = x + 2 is on top.
Step 3: ∫−12 (x + 2 − x2) dx = (x2/2 + 2x − x3/3) |−12. (Check: derivative is x + 2 − x² ✓.)
At 2: 4/2 + 4 − 8/3 = 2 + 4 − 8/3 = 6 − 8/3 = 10/3.
At −1: 1/2 − 2 + 1/3 = 3/6 − 12/6 + 2/6 = −7/6.
Area = 10/3 − (−7/6) = 20/6 + 7/6 = 27/6 = 9/2.
9/2 square units (= 4.5)
