Lesson 1 — Adding Up Little Pieces

The Big Idea of Integration · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 1 of 20

📚 All lessonsNext: Lesson 2 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: understand the ONE question integration answers — "if I know how fast something is changing, what's the TOTAL?" — and learn to answer it by adding up little pieces. No new symbols yet; just the idea, with numbers you can handle.

1 Quick recap — what a derivative is (you've seen this)

  • A derivative answers the question: "how fast is this changing, right now?"
  • Example: your position while riding a bike. The derivative of your position is your speed — it tells how quickly the position is changing at each instant. On a graph, the derivative is the slope (steepness) at a point.
  • If your position after t hours is s = 10t miles, the derivative is 10 — you're moving at a steady 10 miles per hour.
  • So a derivative takes a total (like position) and gives you a rate (like speed).

2 The reverse question — the one integration answers

  • Imagine your bike's odometer is broken (it can't count miles), but the speedometer works fine. At the end of a ride, can you figure out how far you went?
  • You know the rate at every moment. You want the total. That is exactly the reverse of what a derivative does.
  • Integration is the reverse of differentiation: derivatives turn totals into rates; integration turns rates back into totals.
  • This "reverse" question shows up everywhere: speed → distance, water flowing in → water in the tank, money earned per day → total money, rain per hour → total rainfall.

3 The easiest case — a constant rate

  • If you ride at a steady 10 miles per hour for 3 hours, you went 10 × 3 = 30 miles. You already integrate — you just called it multiplying!
  • The rule for a constant rate: total = rate × time.
Worked example: A faucet pours 2 liters of water per minute into a bucket for 5 minutes. Total water = 2 × 5 = 10 liters. Rate × time = total.

4 When the rate changes — chop it into pieces

  • Real rides aren't steady. Suppose you ride 10 mph for 1 hour, then 14 mph for 2 hours, then 6 mph for 1 hour.
  • No single multiplication works — so chop the trip into pieces where the rate IS steady, multiply each piece, and add:
PieceSpeedTimeDistance
110 mph1 h10 mi
214 mph2 h28 mi
36 mph1 h6 mi
  • Total: 10 + 28 + 6 = 44 miles. That's it. That's the heart of integration: total = add up (rate × little chunk of time) for every piece.

5 Seeing it as a picture (this becomes important!)

  • Draw the speed graph: time along the bottom, speed going up. Each steady piece is a flat step, and each piece's distance (speed × time) is exactly the area of a rectangle — width = time, height = speed.
speed (mph) 14 | ┌────────────┐ | │ │ 10 | ┌──────┤ 28 mi │ | │10 mi │ │ 6 | │ │ ├──────┐ | │ │ │ 6 mi │ 0 +--------------------------------→ time (h) 0 1 3 4
  • Total distance = total area under the speed graph. This picture is the secret weapon of the whole course: the total accumulated = the area under the rate graph.
  • Next lessons: what if the graph is a slanted line, or a curve? (Preview: we'll slice it into skinny rectangles.)

6 The word and where we're going

  • This process — chop into pieces, multiply, add up — is called integration. "To integrate" literally means to combine pieces into a whole.
  • Every lesson in this course builds on this one idea. If you can do rate × time and add, you already understand what integration is. The rest is learning clever shortcuts for when the pieces get infinitely skinny.
✏️ Exercises — 20 problems (write your answer on paper, then reveal)
1.constant rate
A car drives at a steady 60 miles per hour for 2 hours. How far does it travel?

Worked solution

Background: "Miles per hour" is a rate — it tells how many miles get added to your trip for each hour that passes. When a rate never changes, the total is one multiplication: total = rate × time.
The speed is steady, so: distance = speed × time = 60 miles/hour × 2 hours.
Notice how the units work: (miles/hour) × (hours) — the hours cancel out, leaving just miles. Units cancelling correctly is a great way to check you set up a problem right.
120 miles
2.constant rate
A hose fills a pool with 8 gallons of water per minute. How much water flows in during 15 minutes?

Worked solution

Background: water flow is measured as a rate (gallons per minute). The pool doesn't care about the rate itself — it accumulates the TOTAL. Turning a flow rate into a total amount is exactly what integration does.
Total water = rate × time = 8 gallons/minute × 15 minutes.
8 × 15 = 120. The "per minute" and "minutes" cancel, leaving gallons.
120 gallons
3.constant rate · reverse
A snail travels 42 centimeters in 6 minutes at a perfectly steady pace. What was its speed, in centimeters per minute? (This is the derivative question — the reverse of the last two!)

Worked solution

Background: derivatives and integrals are reverse operations. Integration: rate × time = total. Derivative (for a steady rate): total ÷ time = rate. Recognizing WHICH direction a question asks is half of calculus.
We know the total (42 cm) and the time (6 min), and we want the rate.
rate = total ÷ time = 42 ÷ 6 = 7.
7 centimeters per minute
4.two pieces
You walk at 3 miles per hour for 2 hours, then jog at 5 miles per hour for 1 hour. What is your total distance?

Worked solution

Background: when the rate changes, no single multiplication works. The fix: chop the trip into pieces where the rate IS steady, compute each piece, and add. This "chop–multiply–add" is the heart of integration.
Piece 1 (walking): 3 mph × 2 h = 6 miles.
Piece 2 (jogging): 5 mph × 1 h = 5 miles.
Total = 6 + 5 = 11 miles.
11 miles
5.three pieces
A delivery truck drives 30 mph for 1 hour, 50 mph for 2 hours, and 20 mph for half an hour. How far does it go in total?

Worked solution

Background: same chop–multiply–add idea, now with a fractional time. A rate works for ANY amount of time, including half an hour: in half an hour at 20 mph you cover half of 20 miles.
Piece 1: 30 × 1 = 30 miles.
Piece 2: 50 × 2 = 100 miles.
Piece 3: 20 × 0.5 = 10 miles.
Total = 30 + 100 + 10 = 140 miles.
140 miles
6.rate table
A faucet's flow rate changes every minute: minute 1: 4 L/min · minute 2: 6 L/min · minute 3: 5 L/min · minute 4: 1 L/min. How much water total flows in the 4 minutes?

Worked solution

Background: a table of rates is like a staircase graph — each row is one steady piece lasting 1 minute. Since each piece is 1 minute wide, each piece contributes rate × 1 = the rate itself.
Minute 1: 4 × 1 = 4 L. Minute 2: 6 × 1 = 6 L. Minute 3: 5 × 1 = 5 L. Minute 4: 1 × 1 = 1 L.
Total = 4 + 6 + 5 + 1 = 16 liters.
16 liters
7.money rate
You earn $12 per hour babysitting. You work 3 hours on Friday and 4 hours on Saturday. How much do you earn in total?

Worked solution

Background: money can accumulate at a rate too — dollars per hour instead of miles per hour. Integration doesn't care WHAT is accumulating: distance, water, money, anything with a "per".
Friday: 12 × 3 = $36. Saturday: 12 × 4 = $48.
Total = 36 + 48 = $84. (Shortcut: the rate never changed, so 12 × 7 total hours = $84 — chopping is optional when the rate is constant.)
$84
8.area = total
A speed graph shows a single flat step: speed 40 km/h from time 0 to time 3 hours. What is the area of the rectangle under this step, and what does that area mean physically?

Worked solution

Background: on a rate graph, a steady rate makes a rectangle: width = the time it lasts, height = the rate. Area = width × height = time × rate = the TOTAL accumulated. Area under a rate graph isn't just geometry — it has a real-world meaning.
Width = 3 hours, height = 40 km/h.
Area = 3 × 40 = 120. The units: hours × km/hour = km.
Area = 120 → the car traveled 120 kilometers
9.staircase graph
A speed graph is a staircase: 5 m/s from t = 0 to t = 2 s, then 8 m/s from t = 2 to t = 6 s. Find the total distance by adding rectangle areas.

Worked solution

Background: careful with the second rectangle — its width is NOT 6. The step lasts FROM t = 2 TO t = 6, so its width is 6 − 2 = 4 seconds. Width is always (end time − start time).
Rectangle 1: width 2 − 0 = 2 s, height 5 m/s → area = 10 m.
Rectangle 2: width 6 − 2 = 4 s, height 8 m/s → area = 32 m.
Total = 10 + 32 = 42 meters.
42 meters
10.reverse lookup
A bakery oven uses electricity at 2 kilowatts (2 kW means 2 kilowatt-hours of energy per hour). The bakery used 9 kilowatt-hours today. For how many hours was the oven on?

Worked solution

Background (physics): power (kilowatts) is the RATE of using energy; energy (kilowatt-hours) is the TOTAL. Energy = power × time — same pattern as distance = speed × time. Here we know the total and the rate, and solve backwards for the time.
total = rate × time → 9 = 2 × time.
time = 9 ÷ 2 = 4.5.
4.5 hours
11.unit check
A plane flies at 500 miles per hour for 90 minutes. How far does it fly? (Careful with the units!)

Worked solution

Background: the rate is "per hour" but the time is in minutes — multiplying 500 × 90 would be nonsense (it mixes units). Always convert so the time units match the rate's units first.
90 minutes = 90 ÷ 60 = 1.5 hours.
distance = 500 × 1.5 = 750 miles.
750 miles
12.two-part day
Rain falls at 3 millimeters per hour from 6 AM to 10 AM, then at 1 millimeter per hour from 10 AM to 1 PM. How many millimeters of rain fell in total?

Worked solution

Background (weather): rainfall rate (mm per hour) tells how fast the water level in a straight-sided bucket would rise. The total rainfall is what a rain gauge shows at the end — the accumulated depth. First find how long each piece lasts.
Piece 1: 6 AM → 10 AM is 4 hours at 3 mm/h → 12 mm.
Piece 2: 10 AM → 1 PM is 3 hours at 1 mm/h → 3 mm.
Total = 12 + 3 = 15 mm.
15 millimeters
13.compare trips
Rider A goes 12 mph for 3 hours. Rider B goes 8 mph for 2 hours, then 10 mph for 2 hours. Who travels farther, and by how much?

Worked solution

Background: comparing two accumulations = computing two totals separately, then subtracting. On graphs, you'd be comparing the areas under two different speed graphs.
Rider A: 12 × 3 = 36 miles.
Rider B: 8 × 2 = 16 miles, plus 10 × 2 = 20 miles → 36... wait, 16 + 20 = 36 miles. They're tied? Check the arithmetic: 16 + 20 = 36. Yes!
Both traveled exactly 36 miles — different speed patterns can accumulate the same total. (Different-shaped graphs can enclose the same area.)
It's a tie — both travel 36 miles
14.filling + draining
Water flows INTO a tank at 10 liters per minute while a drain lets water OUT at 4 liters per minute. Both run for 6 minutes. How much water does the tank gain?

Worked solution

Background: when things flow in AND out, the tank only feels the NET rate: in-rate minus out-rate. Negative-feeling rates (draining) subtract from the accumulation — a preview of Lesson 10, where "area below the axis" counts as negative.
Net rate = 10 − 4 = 6 liters per minute (gaining).
Gain = 6 × 6 = 36 liters.
(Same answer the long way: in = 60 L, out = 24 L, gain = 60 − 24 = 36 L.)
36 liters
15.derivative ↔ integral
In one sentence each, say what question a DERIVATIVE answers and what question an INTEGRAL answers, using a car trip as your example.

Worked solution

Background: this pair of questions is the whole subject of calculus. Keeping them straight ("rate ↔ total") is more valuable than any single computation.
Derivative: "Given my position at every moment, how FAST am I going right now?" (total → rate; the speedometer computed from the odometer).
Integral: "Given my speed at every moment, how FAR have I gone in total?" (rate → total; the odometer rebuilt from the speedometer).
Derivative: rate right now. Integral: total accumulated.
16.heartbeats
During a workout, your heart beats at 90 beats per minute for 10 minutes, then 150 beats per minute for 20 minutes, then 100 beats per minute for 5 minutes while cooling down. How many times does your heart beat in total?

Worked solution

Background (biology): heart rate is a rate like any other — beats per minute. A fitness tracker that reports "total beats" is literally integrating your heart-rate graph all day.
Piece 1: 90 × 10 = 900 beats.
Piece 2: 150 × 20 = 3000 beats.
Piece 3: 100 × 5 = 500 beats.
Total = 900 + 3000 + 500 = 4400 beats.
4400 beats
17.find the missing piece
A cyclist wants to cover exactly 50 miles. She rides 12 mph for 2 hours, then 10 mph for 1 hour. She finishes the ride at 8 mph. How many hours must she ride at 8 mph?

Worked solution

Background: here the total is the GIVEN and one piece is unknown — so add up the known pieces, see what's missing, and solve backwards with total = rate × time.
Known pieces: 12 × 2 = 24 miles, and 10 × 1 = 10 miles → 34 miles done.
Missing distance = 50 − 34 = 16 miles.
At 8 mph: time = 16 ÷ 8 = 2 hours.
2 hours
18.average rate preview
A bus travels 30 mph for 1 hour, then 60 mph for 1 hour. (a) What is the total distance? (b) What single steady speed for 2 hours would give the same total? (That's called the average speed.)

Worked solution

Background: the "average rate" is the one constant rate that would accumulate the SAME total in the same time. It's total ÷ time. (Lesson 14 turns this into the official "average value" formula.) Note: you can only average the two speeds directly (30+60)/2 = 45 because the two pieces lasted EQUAL times.
(a) 30 × 1 + 60 × 1 = 30 + 60 = 90 miles.
(b) average speed = total ÷ time = 90 ÷ 2 = 45 mph. Check: 45 × 2 = 90 ✓.
(a) 90 miles   (b) 45 mph
19.build the graph
Describe (or sketch on paper) the speed graph of this trip, then find the total distance: stand still for 1 hour, walk 4 mph for 2 hours, run 8 mph for 1 hour.

Worked solution

Background: "standing still" is a rate of 0 — its rectangle has height 0 and adds NO area. Every rate graph piece contributes exactly its rectangle's area, even when that area is zero.
The graph: a flat line at height 0 from t = 0 to 1, a step up to height 4 from t = 1 to 3, a step up to height 8 from t = 3 to 4.
Areas: 0 × 1 = 0, then 4 × 2 = 8, then 8 × 1 = 8.
Total = 0 + 8 + 8 = 16 miles.
16 miles
20.challenge · think ahead
A rocket sled speeds up smoothly from 0 to 20 m/s over 10 seconds (its speed graph is a straight slanted line, not a staircase). Its speed is never steady, so rectangles don't fit exactly. (a) Why does our chop-multiply-add method have trouble here? (b) The area under this slanted line is a triangle — use the triangle area formula (½ × base × height) to find the distance anyway.

Worked solution

Background (physics): smoothly increasing speed = constant acceleration, like a falling ball or a drag racer. This problem is the bridge to Lesson 2: when the rate changes continuously, we can't chop it into perfectly steady pieces — but the "area under the rate graph = total" idea STILL works, so geometry can save us.
(a) The speed is different at every single instant — there's no interval, however small, where it's exactly constant. Any staircase of rectangles only approximates the true slanted line (we'll exploit that in Lesson 3!).
(b) The region under the line from t = 0 to 10 is a triangle: base = 10 seconds, height = 20 m/s.
Area = ½ × 10 × 20 = 100. Units: seconds × meters/second = meters.
(a) the rate is never constant, so no rectangle is exact   (b) 100 meters
