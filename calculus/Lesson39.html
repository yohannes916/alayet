1 Parametric curves — a point driven by a clock

  • Every graph so far was y = f(x): for each x, one height. But think of a video-game character moving around the screen. At each moment t (time!), the character has an x-coordinate AND a y-coordinate — and each is its own function of time:
A parametric curve is a moving point ( x(t), y(t) ) — two separate "clock-driven" functions, one for each coordinate. The variable t is called the parameter, and it usually means time.
  • Let's plot one by hand: x = t², y = t for t from −2 to 2. Just feed in clock times:

  • Since y = t, we have x = y² — a sideways parabola. Notice: this is NOT the graph of a function y = f(x) (it fails the vertical line test — x = 1 has two points!). Parametric language handles it easily.
  • Why we need this: ① curves that aren't function graphs — circles, loops, spirals; ② motion with direction — the curve knows WHERE the point is at each time and WHICH WAY it's going.
  • The star example: x = cos t, y = sin t. At t = 0 the point is (1, 0) (east). At t = π/2 it's (0, 1) (north). At t = π it's (−1, 0) (west). It traces the unit circle, counterclockwise, one full lap every 2π of time.

2 Velocity in 2-D — and speed via Pythagoras

  • The moving point has TWO velocities at once: dx/dt = how fast it moves horizontally, and dy/dt = how fast it moves vertically. Just ordinary derivatives of the two clock functions.
  • But how fast is the point actually moving along its path? Picture the two velocities as the sides of a rectangle: in a tiny slice of time the point moves a little bit right AND a little bit up, so its true motion is the diagonal. Diagonals come from the Pythagorean theorem:
↗ true motion (the diagonal) / | / | dy/dt (vertical speed) / | ●———————— dx/dt (horizontal speed) speed² = (dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)² ← Pythagoras!
Speed of a parametric point: speed = √( (dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)² )
Worked example: x = 3t, y = 4t. Then dx/dt = 3 and dy/dt = 4, so speed = √(3² + 4²) = √(9 + 16) = √25 = 5, at every moment. A 3-4-5 right triangle! The point slides along a straight line at a perfectly steady speed of 5.

3 Distance traveled = ∫ speed dt — Lesson 1's idea, now in 2-D!

  • Way back in Lesson 1: distance = speed × time, and when speed changes, distance = ∫ speed dt. That idea never stopped being true — it works even when the path curves through 2-D:
Parametric arc length (= distance traveled): L = ∫ √( (dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)² ) dt — literally "add up speed × tiny time" over the trip.
  • First worked example — the circle. Take x = cos t, y = sin t on [0, 2π]. Then dx/dt = −sin t and dy/dt = cos t, so speed = √(sin²t + cos²t).
  • Why is sin²t + cos²t = 1? Look at the unit circle: the point (cos t, sin t) sits at distance 1 from the origin, and its coordinates are the legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is that radius. Pythagoras: (leg)² + (leg)² = (hypotenuse)², i.e. cos²t + sin²t = 1²= 1, always. (Check t = 0: 0 + 1 = 1 ✓.)
  • So speed = √1 = 1: the point cruises at constant speed 1. Distance = ∫₀^2π 1 dt = 2π — exactly the circumference of a circle of radius 1. The formula rediscovered geometry! ✓
Second worked example (a beautiful u-sub): x = t², y = (2/3)t³ for t in [0, √3]. Find the distance traveled.

Step 1 — velocities: dx/dt = 2t,   dy/dt = 2t².
Step 2 — speed²: (2t)² + (2t²)² = 4t² + 4t⁴ = 4t²(1 + t²). Factoring made it a perfect setup!
Step 3 — speed: √(4t²(1+t²)) = 2t√(1 + t²) (fine since t ≥ 0 here, so √(4t²) = 2t).
Step 4 — integrate: L = ∫₀^√3 2t√(1 + t²) dt. The fingerprint! Let u = 1 + t², so du = 2t dt — exactly what's sitting there.
Step 5 — new limits: t = 0 → u = 1;   t = √3 → u = 1 + 3 = 4.
Step 6: L = ∫₁⁴ √u du = [ (2/3)u^(3/2) ]₁⁴ = (2/3)(4^(3/2) − 1) = (2/3)(8 − 1) = 14/3.
Check: d/du of (2/3)u^(3/2) is u^(1/2) = √u ✓, and 4^(3/2) = (√4)³ = 2³ = 8 ✓.

4 Polar coordinates — a new address system

  • Instead of "go right x, go up y," describe a point by: how far from the origin (r), and at what angle from due east (θ). Same points, new language — like giving directions by "2 miles northeast" instead of street grid.
  • Simple polar equations:
    • r = 2: all points at distance 2, any angle — a circle of radius 2.
    • θ = π/4: all points at angle 45°, any distance — a ray heading northeast.

5 Polar area — the slices are pizza wedges now

  • For y = f(x) we found area with skinny vertical rectangles. In polar land, the natural slice is a skinny pizza wedge: fix a tiny angle dθ, and the curve carves out a thin sector of radius r.
arc —— ╱╲ dθ wedge a full circle of radius r has area πr² r ╱ │ this wedge is the fraction dθ/(2π) of it: ╱ ↖ tiny angle dθ ●———————— wedge area = (dθ/2π) · πr² = ½ r² dθ origin
  • Deriving the wedge formula: a whole circle of radius r has area πr². A wedge with angle dθ is the fraction dθ/(2π) of the whole circle. So its area is (dθ/2π) · πr² = ½ r² dθ. The π's cancel — clean!
  • Add up all the wedges as θ sweeps around:
Polar area formula: A = ½ ∫ r² dθ   (r may depend on θ; the limits are the starting and ending angles of the sweep).
Sanity check — the circle r = 2, swept all the way around: A = ½ ∫₀^2π (2)² dθ = ½ ∫₀^2π 4 dθ = ½ · 4 · 2π = 4π. And geometry says π · 2² = 4π ✓. The wedge machine agrees with elementary school!

6 Worked: the area of the cardioid (and the cos² trick)

  • Area inside r = 1 + cos θ: sweep θ from 0 to 2π: A = ½ ∫₀^2π (1 + cos θ)² dθ.
  • Expand: (1 + cos θ)² = 1 + 2cos θ + cos²θ. The first two pieces are easy; but what is the integral of cos²θ? We need one new identity.
  • The half-angle identity: cos²θ = (1 + cos 2θ)/2. It converts a square (hard to integrate) into a plain cosine of a doubled angle (easy!). Verify it before trusting it:
    • θ = 0: left side cos²0 = 1; right side (1 + cos 0)/2 = (1+1)/2 = 1 ✓
    • θ = π/2: left side cos²(π/2) = 0; right side (1 + cos π)/2 = (1−1)/2 = 0 ✓
  • Integrate the three pieces from 0 to 2π:
    • ∫₀^2π 1 dθ = 2π.
    • ∫₀^2π 2cos θ dθ = [2 sin θ]₀^2π = 0 − 0 = 0. (A full lap of cosine cancels itself.)
    • ∫₀^2π cos²θ dθ = ∫₀^2π (1 + cos 2θ)/2 dθ = [ θ/2 + (sin 2θ)/4 ]₀^2π = (π + 0) − (0 + 0) = π.
  • Assemble: A = ½ (2π + 0 + π) = ½ · 3π = 3π/2. The heart holds exactly one-and-a-half π of area. ❤️
  • Check the antiderivative used: d/dθ [θ/2 + (sin 2θ)/4] = 1/2 + (2cos 2θ)/4 = (1 + cos 2θ)/2 = cos²θ ✓.

5.the circle
For the parametric circle x = cos t, y = sin t: (a) where is the point at t = π/2? (b) Show that x² + y² = 1 at every time t — why does that prove the point stays on the unit circle?

Worked solution

Background: x² + y² is the squared distance from the origin (Pythagoras: the legs are x and y, the hypotenuse is the distance). If x² + y² = 1 forever, the point stays exactly 1 unit from the origin — that IS the unit circle.
(a) At t = π/2: x = cos(π/2) = 0, y = sin(π/2) = 1 → the point (0, 1), the top of the circle.
(b) x² + y² = cos²t + sin²t = 1 by the Pythagorean identity (the coordinates of a unit-circle point are the legs of a right triangle with hypotenuse 1).
Distance from origin = √1 = 1 at all times → the point never leaves the unit circle.
(a) (0, 1)   (b) cos²t + sin²t = 1, so the distance from the origin is always 1

7.speed at a time
A point moves with x = 2t², y = 3t. (a) Find its speed at t = 1. (b) Find its speed at t = 0 — and explain in words what the point is doing at that instant.

Worked solution

dx/dt = 4t, dy/dt = 3.

12.cos² drill
Use the half-angle identity cos²θ = (1 + cos 2θ)/2 to compute ∫₀^π cos²θ dθ. (First verify the identity at θ = π.)

Worked solution

Background: squares of sine and cosine can't be integrated directly — the half-angle identity trades cos²θ for (1 + cos 2θ)/2, which is just a constant plus an easy cosine. Remember the linear-inside rule: ∫cos 2θ dθ = (sin 2θ)/2.
Identity check at θ = π: left side cos²π = (−1)² = 1; right side (1 + cos 2π)/2 = (1 + 1)/2 = 1 ✓.

13.laps!
A point moves with x = cos 2t, y = sin 2t for t in [0, 2π]. (a) Find its speed. (b) Find the total distance traveled. (c) The unit circle's circumference is only 2π — explain your answer to (b)!

Worked solution

Background: distance traveled = ∫ speed dt counts EVERY bit of motion, even retraced ground — just like total distance vs. net displacement for 1-D motion. The "2t" inside makes the clock spin twice as fast (chain rule brings out a 2).
(a) dx/dt = −2 sin 2t, dy/dt = 2 cos 2t. Speed = √(4sin²2t + 4cos²2t) = √(4(sin²2t + cos²2t)) = √4 = 2.
(b) L = ∫₀^2π 2 dt = 2 · 2π = 4π.
(c) The point still travels the unit circle (x² + y² = cos²2t + sin²2t = 1), but at speed 2 it completes a lap every π of time — so in time 2π it goes around TWICE. Distance 4π = two circumferences.
(a) speed = 2   (b) 4π   (c) it laps the circle twice

15.u-sub arc length

16.polar area · limits!
Find the area inside the circle r = 2cos θ. Warning: the correct sweep is θ from −π/2 to π/2 — explain why, then compute. (Check your answer against the geometry from Exercise 9.)

Worked solution

Background: r = 2cos θ finishes drawing its whole circle as θ runs from −π/2 to π/2 (r shrinks to 0 at both ends). If you keep sweeping to 2π, the curve just retraces itself and the integral double-counts the area. Always ask: "over which angles does the curve get drawn ONCE?"
A = ½ ∫_(−π/2)^(π/2) (2cos θ)² dθ = ½ ∫_(−π/2)^(π/2) 4cos²θ dθ = 2 ∫_(−π/2)^(π/2) cos²θ dθ.
Half-angle: cos²θ = (1 + cos 2θ)/2, antiderivative θ/2 + (sin 2θ)/4.
At π/2: π/4 + (sin π)/4 = π/4. At −π/2: −π/4 + (sin(−π))/4 = −π/4. Difference = π/2.
A = 2 · (π/2) = π. Geometry check: it's a circle of radius 1 (Exercise 9), area π · 1² = π ✓.

17.cardioid variant
Find the area inside the cardioid r = 1 + sin θ. (You'll need sin²θ = (1 − cos 2θ)/2 — the sine version of the half-angle identity; verify it at θ = π/2 first.)

Worked solution

Background: r = 1 + sin θ is the lesson's cardioid rotated to point north (fat side up) — rotating a shape can't change its area, so we expect 3π/2 again. The sine half-angle identity: sin²θ = (1 − cos 2θ)/2. Check at θ = π/2: left side 1; right side (1 − cos π)/2 = (1+1)/2 = 1 ✓.

A student finds the area inside r = 4cos θ using A = ½∫₀^2π (4cos θ)² dθ and gets 8π. But r = 4cos θ is a circle of radius 2, whose area is only 4π. What went wrong, and what is the correct computation?

20.challenge · rose petal
The polar curve r = 2 sin(2θ) is a four-petaled rose; one petal is drawn as θ runs from 0 to π/2 (r starts at 0, blooms out to 2 at θ = π/4, and returns to 0). Find the exact area of one petal.

Worked solution

Background: everything in this lesson at once: pick the sweep of angles for ONE tracing (here 0 to π/2), square r, and handle the square of sine with the half-angle identity. Careful — the angle inside is already 2θ, so sin²(2θ) = (1 − cos 4θ)/2 (double the inner angle).
A = ½ ∫₀^(π/2) (2 sin 2θ)² dθ = ½ ∫₀^(π/2) 4 sin²2θ dθ = 2 ∫₀^(π/2) sin²2θ dθ.
Identity: sin²(2θ) = (1 − cos 4θ)/2. (Check θ = π/4: left side sin²(π/2) = 1; right side (1 − cos π)/2 = 1 ✓.)
2 ∫₀^(π/2) (1 − cos 4θ)/2 dθ = ∫₀^(π/2) (1 − cos 4θ) dθ = [ θ − (sin 4θ)/4 ]₀^(π/2).
At π/2: π/2 − (sin 2π)/4 = π/2 − 0. At 0: 0. So A = π/2.
Check the antiderivative: d/dθ [θ − (sin 4θ)/4] = 1 − cos 4θ ✓.
Area of one petal = π/2
