Lesson 35 — Volumes II: Off-Axis & Cross-Sections

Spinning Around Other Lines · Slices That Aren't Circles · Lesson 35 of 40

📚 All lessons← Lesson 34Next: Lesson 36 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: level up Lesson 34's volume tools in two big ways. First: revolve regions around lines OTHER than the x-axis (like y = −1 or the y-axis) — the only thing that changes is the radius. Second: build solids with NO spinning at all — solids whose slices are squares, triangles, or semicircles standing on a flat base. One master idea runs through everything: volume = ∫ (area of a slice) dx.

1 Revolving around other horizontal lines — the radius is a DISTANCE

  • In Lesson 34 we spun regions around the x-axis. A point on the curve y = f(x) swept out a circle of radius f(x) — because f(x) is its distance to the x-axis.
  • That last sentence is the key. The radius was never "f(x) because that's the formula." It was f(x) because that's how far the point is from the spin line. Change the spin line, and the distance changes.
  • Spin around y = −1 (a line one unit BELOW the x-axis). A point at height f(x) is now f(x) + 1 away from the spin line — its height above the x-axis, PLUS the extra 1-unit gap down to y = −1. So radius = f(x) + 1.
  • Spin around y = 5 when the curve sits BELOW that line. Now the point at height f(x) is 5 − f(x) below the spin line, so radius = 5 − f(x).
  • The one rule: radius = distance from the spin line to the curve = |axis − curve|. Don't memorize sign patterns — draw a quick sketch, look at the gap, and write down the distance. The sketch is the method.
y 2 | y = √x ___—— 1 | __——‾‾ | _/ ↕ radius = √x + 1 0 +--/------------------------→ x | ↕ the extra gap of 1 −1 |═════════ spin line y = −1 ═════════ 0 1 2 3 4
Worked example (expand carefully!): take the region between y = √x and the line y = −1, from x = 0 to x = 4, and revolve it around y = −1. The region touches the spin line, so every slice is a full disk with radius = distance from y = −1 up to the curve: r(x) = √x + 1.

Squaring a sum, from scratch: (a + b)² = (a + b)(a + b) = a² + ab + ba + b² = a² + 2ab + b². The middle term 2ab is the one people forget — (a+b)² is NOT a² + b². Here:
(√x + 1)² = (√x)² + 2·√x·1 + 1² = x + 2√x + 1.

V = π∫₀⁴ (√x + 1)² dx = π∫₀⁴ (x + 2√x + 1) dx = π[x²/2 + (4/3)x^(3/2) + x]₀⁴
(the middle antiderivative: 2√x = 2x^(1/2) → 2·(2/3)x^(3/2) = (4/3)x^(3/2))
= π(16/2 + (4/3)·8 + 4) = π(8 + 32/3 + 4) = π(36/3 + 32/3) = 68π/3.
  • Sanity check: about the x-axis this region-to-the-curve volume would use radius √x and give π∫₀⁴ x dx = 8π = 24π/3. Spinning the fatter radius (√x + 1) should give MORE — and 68π/3 > 24π/3. ✓

2 Revolving about the y-axis — the same disk idea, sideways

  • Nothing in the disk method cares which axis you spin around. Spin around the y-axis and the whole story just turns 90°: slice horizontally, stack the disks vertically, and integrate dy.
  • A horizontal slice at height y sweeps a disk whose radius is the horizontal distance from the y-axis out to the curve — that's the x-value at that height. So we need the curve written as x as a function of y: V = π∫ (x(y))² dy.
  • New skill #1 — flip the formula: solve y = f(x) for x. Example: y = √x. Square both sides: y² = x, i.e. x = y². Same curve, described sideways.
  • New skill #2 — convert the limits: the integral now runs over y-values. If x ran from 0 to 4 on y = √x, then y runs from √0 = 0 up to √4 = 2. Always push the endpoints through the curve.
Worked example: the region between the curve y = √x (written sideways: x = y²) and the y-axis, for y from 0 to 2, revolved about the y-axis.

Horizontal slice at height y: a disk of radius x(y) = y², area π(y²)² = πy⁴.
V = π∫₀² y⁴ dy = π[y⁵/5]₀² = π · 32/5 = 32π/5.

Every step is Lesson 34's recipe — we only swapped which letter does the slicing.

3 Washers off-axis — same R² − r², radii measured to the new line

  • What if the region does NOT touch the spin line? Then revolving leaves a tunnel down the middle — a washer, exactly like Lesson 34, and A = π(R² − r²) still holds. The only update: both radii are distances to the NEW line.
  • Watch how this connects to Section 1. Take the region between y = √x and the x-axis (not down to −1!) on [0, 4], and spin it around y = −1. There's a 1-unit gap between the region and the spin line, so:
    • Outer radius: from y = −1 up to the curve: R = √x + 1.
    • Inner radius: from y = −1 up to the bottom edge (the x-axis, y = 0): r = 0 − (−1) = 1.
Worked example: V = π∫₀⁴ [(√x + 1)² − 1²] dx = π∫₀⁴ (x + 2√x + 1 − 1) dx = π∫₀⁴ (x + 2√x) dx
= π[x²/2 + (4/3)x^(3/2)]₀⁴ = π(8 + 32/3) = 56π/3.

Beautiful cross-check: Section 1's solid (region taken all the way down to y = −1) had volume 68π/3. Its core — the cylinder swept by the gap strip, radius 1, length 4 — has volume π·1²·4 = 4π = 12π/3. Drill out the core: 68π/3 − 12π/3 = 56π/3. ✓ The washer answer is exactly "full disk minus the hole."

4 Known cross-sections — no revolution at all!

  • Here is the twist that makes this lesson bigger than "spinning things." Remember the real engine of Lesson 34: V = ∫ A(x) dx — integrate the slice area. Nothing in that formula mentions circles. Circles only appeared because REVOLVING happens to make circular slices.
  • New kind of solid: start with a flat 2-D base region lying in the xy-plane. At each x, build a slice standing straight up, perpendicular to the x-axis, whose shape is stated in the problem: a square, a semicircle, a triangle... The slice's bottom edge sits on the base region.
  • So the slice's footprint width at position x is the width of the base region there: w(x) = top curve − bottom curve. That width is the side (or diameter) of the shape. Then V = ∫ A(x) dx with A(x) = the shape's area formula fed with w(x).
square slice standing up ┌──────┐ │ │ height = w(x) (same as the width — it's a square!) │ │ ──────┴──────┴──────────→ x base region in the flat plane; slice's bottom edge has length w(x) = top − bottom
  • Area formulas, built from scratch (w = the footprint width):
Slice shapeHow the area comes from wA(x)
Square (side w)side × sidew²
Semicircle (diameter w)radius is w/2, so ½·π(w/2)² = ½·πw²/4(π/8)w²
Isosceles right triangle, hypotenuse on baselegs ℓ with ℓ√2 = w → ℓ = w/√2; area = ½ℓ² = ½·w²/2w²/4

  • Notice every formula is (some constant)·w² — so these problems all boil down to integrating w(x)² and multiplying by the shape's constant. And only the semicircle's constant contains π, because only the semicircle is round.

5 Flagship examples — same base, different shapes

Worked example (squares): the base is the region between y = √x and the x-axis on [0, 4]. Cross-sections perpendicular to the x-axis are squares.

Footprint width: w(x) = top − bottom = √x − 0 = √x.
Slice area: A(x) = w² = (√x)² = x.
V = ∫₀⁴ x dx = [x²/2]₀⁴ = 8.

No π anywhere! Squares aren't circles — π has no business showing up.
Worked example (semicircles, same base): same region, but now the slices are semicircles with diameter w(x) = √x sitting on the base.

A(x) = (π/8)w² = (π/8)x.
V = (π/8)∫₀⁴ x dx = (π/8)·8 = π.

Same base, same ∫₀⁴ x dx = 8 — only the shape constant changed (1 for squares, π/8 for semicircles). The base does the integrating; the shape just scales it.

6 The recipe box (and the #1 error)

  • Cross-section recipe: (1) find the footprint width w(x) = top − bottom of the base region; (2) feed w into the shape's area formula to get A(x); (3) integrate: V = ∫ A(x) dx.
  • Off-axis recipe: sketch the spin line, write each radius as a distance |axis − curve|, then disks π∫r² or washers π∫(R² − r²) as before.
  • Common error #1: writing π out of habit on a squares-or-triangles problem. π belongs to circles only. Before you write π, ask: "is my slice actually round?"
  • Common error #2: using the old radius f(x) after the spin line moved. The radius is a distance to the CURRENT spin line — resketch every time.

1.radius as distance
(a) The curve y = x² is revolved about the line y = −2. What is the radius of the disk at position x? (b) The same curve, on 0 ≤ x ≤ 2 where it stays below y = 4, is revolved about y = 4. Now what is the radius? (No integrating — just the radii.)

Worked solution

Background: the radius of a slice is always the DISTANCE from the spin line to the curve. Sketch the spin line, sketch the curve, and measure the gap between them — one is above the other, so subtract the lower height from the upper height.
(a) The spin line y = −2 sits below the curve. Distance = (height of curve) − (height of line) = x² − (−2) = x² + 2.
(b) The spin line y = 4 sits above the curve (since x² ≤ 4 here). Distance = 4 − x².
(a) r = x² + 2   (b) r = 4 − x²
2.algebra warm-up
Expand these squares completely (you'll need both later this lesson): (a) (√x + 2)²   (b) (x² + 1)².

Worked solution

Background: off-axis volumes constantly square sums, so the pattern (a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b² must be automatic. The middle term 2ab is the famous casualty — (a + b)² is NOT a² + b². (Try a = b = 1: (1+1)² = 4, but 1² + 1² = 2.)
(a) (√x + 2)² = (√x)² + 2·√x·2 + 2² = x + 4√x + 4.
(b) (x² + 1)² = (x²)² + 2·x²·1 + 1² = x⁴ + 2x² + 1.
(a) x + 4√x + 4   (b) x⁴ + 2x² + 1
3.off-axis disk
The region between the line y = x and the line y = −1, for 0 ≤ x ≤ 2, is revolved about y = −1. Find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: the region touches the spin line, so slices are full disks (no hole). Radius = distance from y = −1 up to y = x, which is x − (−1) = x + 1. A handy antiderivative shortcut: ∫(x + 1)² dx = (x + 1)³/3, by the linear-inside rule from Lesson 17 (the inside x + 1 has slope 1).
V = π∫₀² (x + 1)² dx = π[(x + 1)³/3]₀².
At x = 2: 3³/3 = 27/3. At x = 0: 1³/3 = 1/3.
V = π(27/3 − 1/3) = 26π/3.
V = 26π/3
4.square cross-sections
The base of a solid is the region between y = x and the x-axis on [0, 2]. Cross-sections perpendicular to the x-axis are squares. Find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: no spinning here! The base is a flat triangle in the plane, and square slices stand on it. Recipe: footprint width w(x) = top − bottom, slice area A = w², volume = ∫A dx. No π — squares aren't circles.
w(x) = x − 0 = x, so A(x) = x².
V = ∫₀² x² dx = [x³/3]₀² = 8/3.
(This solid is actually a pyramid lying on its side — and 8/3 matches the pyramid formula ⅓·base·height = ⅓·(2·2)·2. ✓)
V = 8/3 (no π!)
5.y-axis revolution
The region bounded by the curve x = y², the y-axis, and the line y = 1 is revolved about the y-axis. Find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: spinning around the y-axis means slicing HORIZONTALLY: at each height y, the slice is a disk whose radius is the horizontal distance from the y-axis out to the curve — the x-value there. Everything becomes dy: V = π∫(x(y))² dy, with y-limits.
At height y, the region reaches from x = 0 out to x = y², so the disk radius is y².
V = π∫₀¹ (y²)² dy = π∫₀¹ y⁴ dy = π[y⁵/5]₀¹ = π/5.
V = π/5
6.no-π police
A friend solves a square-cross-section problem, gets ∫₀³ w² dx = 12, and then writes "V = 12π" — "because volume problems always have π." Set them straight: when does π belong in a volume answer, and when doesn't it?

Worked solution

Background: π enters volume formulas through exactly one door: the area of a CIRCLE, πr². Disks and washers have π because revolving creates circular slices. If the slices aren't circles, π never enters.
The master formula is V = ∫A(x) dx. For squares, A = w² — there is no π in the area of a square, so none appears in the volume.
π belongs only when the slice is round: disks (πr²), washers (π(R² − r²)), semicircles ((π/8)w²). For squares and triangles, the correct volume here is just 12.
π only comes from circular slices; the volume is 12, not 12π
7.off-axis disk
The region between y = √x and the line y = −2, for 0 ≤ x ≤ 4, is revolved about y = −2. Find the volume. (Your expansion from #2a will help.)

Worked solution

Background: the spin line dropped to y = −2, so the gap below the x-axis is now 2 units. Radius = distance from y = −2 up to the curve = √x + 2. The region touches the spin line, so these are full disks. Expand the square before integrating — never integrate a squared sum term-by-term without expanding.
V = π∫₀⁴ (√x + 2)² dx = π∫₀⁴ (x + 4√x + 4) dx.
Antiderivative: x²/2 + 4·(2/3)x^(3/2) + 4x = x²/2 + (8/3)x^(3/2) + 4x.
At x = 4: 8 + (8/3)(8) + 16 = 24 + 64/3 = 72/3 + 64/3 = 136/3. At x = 0: 0.
V = 136π/3
8.spin line above
The region bounded by y = x, the line y = 4, and the y-axis is revolved about the line y = 4. Find the volume, then check your answer with a geometry formula.

Worked solution

Background: when the spin line is ABOVE the region, the radius is (line) − (curve). Here the region's lower edge y = x is what sweeps the circle: radius = 4 − x, running from x = 0 (radius 4) down to x = 4 (radius 0). Sketch it — the solid should look like a cone lying on its side, which gives us a free check via V = ⅓πr²h.
V = π∫₀⁴ (4 − x)² dx = π[−(4 − x)³/3]₀⁴ (linear-inside rule; the inside slope −1 flips the sign).
At x = 4: −0/3 = 0. At x = 0: −64/3. So V = π(0 − (−64/3)) = 64π/3.
Geometry check: a cone with radius 4 and height 4 has V = ⅓π(4²)(4) = 64π/3. ✓
V = 64π/3
9.y-axis revolution
The region bounded by x = √y, the y-axis, and the line y = 4 is revolved about the y-axis. Find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: the curve is already written sideways (x as a function of y) — a gift! Horizontal slice at height y: disk of radius x(y) = √y. Squaring a square root undoes it: (√y)² = y, so this integral turns friendly instantly.
V = π∫₀⁴ (√y)² dy = π∫₀⁴ y dy.
= π[y²/2]₀⁴ = π·16/2 = 8π.
V = 8π
10.semicircle cross-sections
The base of a solid is the region between y = x and the x-axis on [0, 2]. Cross-sections perpendicular to the x-axis are semicircles with diameter sitting on the base. Find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: for a semicircle whose DIAMETER is the footprint w, the radius is w/2, so the area is ½·π(w/2)² = (π/8)w². This is the one cross-section shape that legitimately carries a π, because it's half a circle.
w(x) = x, so A(x) = (π/8)x².
V = (π/8)∫₀² x² dx = (π/8)·(8/3) = π/3.
(Compare #4: same base with squares gave 8/3. Same ∫w² dx, different shape constant.)
V = π/3
11.equilateral cross-sections
The base of a solid is the region between the horizontal line y = 2 and the line y = x, for 0 ≤ x ≤ 2. Cross-sections perpendicular to the x-axis are equilateral triangles. Find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: the footprint is w(x) = top − bottom — here the top is the flat line y = 2 and the bottom is y = x, so w = 2 − x (it shrinks from 2 to 0 as x goes 0 → 2). An equilateral triangle of side w has height (√3/2)w, so area = ½·w·(√3/2)w = (√3/4)w².
V = (√3/4)∫₀² (2 − x)² dx.
∫₀² (2 − x)² dx = [−(2 − x)³/3]₀² = 0 − (−8/3) = 8/3.
V = (√3/4)(8/3) = 8√3/12 = 2√3/3.
V = 2√3/3
12.off-axis washer · setup
The region between y = x and y = x² (they cross at x = 0 and x = 1, with the line on top between) is revolved about y = −1. Write the volume integral — do NOT evaluate.

Worked solution

Background: the region floats above the spin line (nearest edge is y = x², which only touches y = −1 nowhere on (0,1))... so revolving leaves a hole: washers. BOTH radii are distances to y = −1: add 1 to each curve's height. Outer radius uses the curve FARTHER from the spin line — that's the top curve y = x.
Outer: R(x) = x − (−1) = x + 1. Inner: r(x) = x² − (−1) = x² + 1.
V = π∫₀¹ [(x + 1)² − (x² + 1)²] dx.
V = π∫₀¹ [(x + 1)² − (x² + 1)²] dx
13.y-axis · convert limits
The region bounded by y = x³, the y-axis, and the line y = 8 is revolved about the y-axis. Rewrite the curve as x in terms of y, convert the limits, and find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: two conversions are needed. (1) Solve for x: y = x³ → x = y^(1/3) (cube root undoes cubing). (2) The y-limits: the region runs from y = 0 (the corner at the origin) up to y = 8. Then a power-of-a-power: (y^(1/3))² = y^(2/3), and the power rule handles fractional exponents fine.
V = π∫₀⁸ (y^(1/3))² dy = π∫₀⁸ y^(2/3) dy = π[(3/5)y^(5/3)]₀⁸.
8^(5/3) = (8^(1/3))⁵ = 2⁵ = 32.
V = π(3/5)(32) = 96π/5.
V = 96π/5
14.off-axis disk · setup
The region between y = x² and the line y = −1, for 0 ≤ x ≤ 2, is revolved about y = −1. Write the volume integral with the square fully EXPANDED — but do not evaluate.

Worked solution

Background: AP problems often award full setup credit and skip the arithmetic — "write but do not evaluate" is a real skill. The setup has all the thinking: radius = distance to the spin line, then a careful (a + b)² expansion (see #2b).
Radius = x² − (−1) = x² + 1. The region touches the spin line at x = 0... more importantly its lower edge IS the spin line, so full disks.
(x² + 1)² = x⁴ + 2x² + 1.
V = π∫₀² (x⁴ + 2x² + 1) dx. Stop here — setup complete.
V = π∫₀² (x⁴ + 2x² + 1) dx
15.same base, different shapes
Two solids share the SAME base (the region between y = x and the x-axis on [0, 2]): one has square cross-sections, the other semicircles. Using your answers to #4 and #10, find the ratio (square volume) : (semicircle volume). Then explain why this ratio would be the same for ANY base.

Worked solution

Background: both volumes are (shape constant) × ∫w² dx. The base only affects ∫w² dx — which is IDENTICAL for both solids. So the ratio of volumes is just the ratio of shape constants, no matter what the base looks like.
Ratio = (8/3) ÷ (π/3) = 8/π.
In general: V_square = 1·∫w²dx and V_semi = (π/8)·∫w²dx, so V_square/V_semi = 1/(π/8) = 8/π ≈ 2.55 always — a square slice holds 8/π times the area of the semicircle on the same footprint.
Ratio = 8/π, for every possible base
16.limits from intersections · setup
The region is enclosed by y = √x and y = x/2. (a) Find where the curves intersect. (b) Write (do not evaluate) the volume integral when this region is revolved about y = −1.

Worked solution

Background: when no interval is given, the curves' crossing points ARE the limits. Solve curve = curve. To decide which curve is the outer radius, test a point between the intersections — the curve farther from the spin line is the outer one.
(a) √x = x/2 → square both sides: x = x²/4 → x² − 4x = 0 → x(x − 4) = 0 → x = 0 and x = 4.
(b) Test x = 1: √1 = 1 vs 1/2 — so y = √x is the top curve. Spin line y = −1 is below: R = √x + 1, r = x/2 + 1. Washers (the region floats above the line).
V = π∫₀⁴ [(√x + 1)² − (x/2 + 1)²] dx.
(a) x = 0, 4   (b) V = π∫₀⁴ [(√x + 1)² − (x/2 + 1)²] dx
17.spot the error
Asked for the volume when the region between y = √x and y = −1 on [0, 4] revolves about y = −1, a student writes V = π∫₀⁴ (√x)² dx = π∫₀⁴ x dx = 8π. Find the error and give the correct volume.

Worked solution

Background: this is THE classic off-axis mistake: using the curve's height f(x) as the radius even after the spin line moved. The radius is the distance from the spin line to the curve — the student measured from the x-axis instead of from y = −1, losing the 1-unit gap.
Correct radius: √x − (−1) = √x + 1, not √x.
V = π∫₀⁴ (√x + 1)² dx = π∫₀⁴ (x + 2√x + 1) dx = π[x²/2 + (4/3)x^(3/2) + x]₀⁴ = π(8 + 32/3 + 4) = 68π/3.
Sanity: 68π/3 ≈ 22.7π > 8π — the fatter radius must give more volume, and it does.
Radius must be √x + 1 (distance to the spin line); V = 68π/3
18.spot the error
The base of a solid is the region between y = 2 and y = x on [0, 2]; cross-sections perpendicular to the x-axis are SQUARES. A student writes V = π∫₀² (2 − x)² dx = 8π/3. Find the error and give the correct volume.

Worked solution

Background: the setup is 90% right — footprint w = 2 − x is correct, and ∫₀²(2 − x)² dx = 8/3 is the right integral. The single error is the π stuck on out of habit. A square of side w has area w², not πw².
Correct: V = ∫₀² (2 − x)² dx = [−(2 − x)³/3]₀² = 0 − (−8/3) = 8/3.
The student's answer is exactly π times too big — that's the fingerprint of a wrongly imported circle formula.
Drop the π: V = 8/3
19.right-triangle slices
The base of a solid is the region between y = √x and the x-axis on [0, 4]. Cross-sections perpendicular to the x-axis are isosceles right triangles with their HYPOTENUSE on the base. Find the volume.

Worked solution

Background: re-derive the area formula rather than trusting memory. If the hypotenuse is w and the legs are ℓ, Pythagoras gives ℓ² + ℓ² = w², so ℓ² = w²/2. The right angle is between the legs, so area = ½·ℓ·ℓ = ½·(w²/2) = w²/4.
w(x) = √x, so A(x) = (√x)²/4 = x/4.
V = (1/4)∫₀⁴ x dx = (1/4)(8) = 2.
No π — triangles aren't circles. (Compare: same base gave 8 with squares and π with semicircles.)
V = 2
20.challenge · AP style
Let R be the first-quadrant region bounded by y = 4 − x², the x-axis, and the y-axis. (a) Find the area of R. (b) Find the volume when R is revolved about the x-axis. (c) Write — but do not evaluate — the volume integral when R is revolved about y = −1. (d) R is the base of a solid with square cross-sections perpendicular to the x-axis. Find its volume.

Worked solution

Background: this is the exact shape of an AP free-response volume question: one region, four different questions. Notice how each part reuses the same boundaries (x from 0 to 2, since 4 − x² = 0 at x = 2) but a different formula: plain area ∫f, disks π∫f², off-axis washers, then ∫A(x) with no π at all.
(a) Area = ∫₀² (4 − x²) dx = [4x − x³/3]₀² = 8 − 8/3 = 16/3.
(b) Disks, radius 4 − x²: V = π∫₀² (4 − x²)² dx = π∫₀² (16 − 8x² + x⁴) dx = π[16x − (8/3)x³ + x⁵/5]₀² = π(32 − 64/3 + 32/5) = π(480 − 320 + 96)/15 = 256π/15.
(c) About y = −1 the region floats 0 units... its bottom edge is the x-axis, 1 unit above the spin line → washers: R = (4 − x²) + 1 = 5 − x², r = 0 + 1 = 1. V = π∫₀² [(5 − x²)² − 1] dx. (Stop — setup only.)
(d) Squares: w = 4 − x², A = (4 − x²)². V = ∫₀² (4 − x²)² dx — the SAME integral as (b) without π — = 256/15.
(a) 16/3   (b) 256π/15   (c) π∫₀² [(5 − x²)² − 1] dx   (d) 256/15
