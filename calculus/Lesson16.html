Lesson 16 — Integrating Sine & Cosine

The Wave Functions Join the Toolbox · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 16 of 20

📚 All lessons← Prev: Lesson 15Next: Lesson 17 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: meet the two wave functions, sine and cosine, learn their antiderivatives — ∫cos x dx = sin x + C and ∫sin x dx = −cos x + C — and use them to find areas under waves. One sneaky minus sign wants to trip you up; by the end you'll see it coming every time.

1 Sine and cosine — the two wave functions

  • Picture a point riding around a circle of radius 1, counterclockwise, at a steady pace. Track two numbers as it goes: sin x is the point's height (how far above or below the center), and cos x is how far it is to the right or left of the center. Here x is how far around the circle the point has traveled.
  • As the point loops around and around, both numbers rise and fall forever between −1 and +1, over and over. Graphed, each one is a smooth, endless wave.
  • That's why these two functions matter so much: they describe anything that repeats — tides going in and out, a swing rocking back and forth, sound waves, your breathing, the seasons. Whenever nature cycles, sine and cosine are the math underneath.
  • They're the same wave, just shifted: cosine starts at the top (value 1), sine starts in the middle (value 0) heading up.

2 Radians — the angle measure calculus loves

  • Instead of splitting a full turn into 360 degrees, calculus measures angles by the distance walked around a circle of radius 1. A full lap around that circle is a walk of length 2π ≈ 6.28, so: full turn = 2π, half turn = π, quarter turn = π/2. That's the whole idea — an angle is just "how far around have I walked?" This measure is called radians, and the clean calculus formulas below are only true in radians (in degrees they'd sprout ugly conversion factors), so radians it is from here on.

4 The derivative facts (the circle of facts)

  • From derivatives you may remember this beautiful pair — take it as our starting point:
  • (sin x)′ = cos x   and   (cos x)′ = −sin x
  • Notice the pattern: differentiating walks you around a little circle of four facts — sin → cos → −sin → −cos → back to sin. The minus sign appears only when a cosine gets differentiated.

5 Reversing them — watch the minus sign!

  • Integration is the reverse of differentiation (Lessons 4–5): to integrate a function, ask "what has THIS as its derivative?"
  • Cosine is easy: what has derivative cos x? Sine does! So ∫cos x dx = sin x + C.
  • Sine needs care: what has derivative sin x? Your first guess might be cos x — but (cos x)′ = −sin x. Wrong sign! Fix it by flipping the sign of the guess: try −cos x. Check: (−cos x)′ = −(−sin x) = sin x ✓. So ∫sin x dx = −cos x + C.
⚠️ THE classic trip-up — box this in your brain:
∫cos x dx = sin x + C  (no minus)
∫sin x dx = −cos x + C  (minus!)
The minus sign lands on the cosine, and it appears when you integrate sine. If you're ever unsure, don't guess — differentiate your answer and see if you get back what's under the integral sign. Ten seconds of checking beats a wrong sign every time.

6 Definite integrals with the value table

  • Now combine the new antiderivatives with the FTC (Lessons 6–8): antiderivative at the top limit minus antiderivative at the bottom limit.
Worked example — one hump of sine: ∫₀^π sin x dx = [−cos x]₀^π = (−cos π) − (−cos 0) = (−(−1)) − (−1) = 1 + 1 = 2.
The area under one full hump of the sine wave is exactly 2. Not 2-point-something — exactly 2. A curvy, flowing wave enclosing a perfectly whole-number area is one of calculus's most delightful little surprises.
Worked example — quarter turn of cosine: ∫₀π/2 cos x dx = [sin x]₀π/2 = sin(π/2) − sin 0 = 1 − 0 = 1.
Check the signs made sense: cos x is positive on that whole stretch, so a positive answer is exactly what we expected.

7 The signed-area connection

  • What about a whole lap? ∫₀2π sin x dx = [−cos x]₀2π = (−cos 2π) − (−cos 0) = (−1) − (−1) = 0.
  • Zero?! Look at the picture in section 3: from 0 to π sine makes a hump above the axis (area +2), and from π to 2π an identical hump below the axis (signed area −2). From Lesson 9, area below the axis counts as negative — so the two humps cancel exactly: +2 + (−2) = 0.
  • Physically: if sin x is the rate water flows into a bay (in during the first half, out during the second), then after one full tide cycle the bay holds exactly what it started with. Net change: zero. The integral knew.
✏️ Exercises — 20 problems (write your answer on paper, then reveal)
1.antiderivative
Find ∫cos x dx. Then check your answer by differentiating it.

Worked solution

Background: an antiderivative of a function is anything whose derivative gives that function back. To integrate cos x, ask "what has derivative cos x?" — and always add + C, because any constant's derivative is 0, so a whole family of answers works.
The derivative fact (sin x)′ = cos x answers the question directly: sin x works.
Check by differentiating: (sin x + C)′ = cos x + 0 = cos x ✓ — exactly what's under the integral sign.
∫cos x dx = sin x + C
2.antiderivative · the minus
Find ∫sin x dx. Then check your answer by differentiating it.

Worked solution

Background: we need something whose derivative is sin x. The tempting guess cos x fails, because (cos x)′ = −sin x — the sign is wrong. The fix for a wrong sign is simple: flip the sign of your guess.
Guess −cos x instead. Check: (−cos x)′ = −(−sin x) = sin x ✓.
So the antiderivative is −cos x, plus the usual constant.
∫sin x dx = −cos x + C
3.value table
Without looking at the table (then check yourself): what are sin π and cos π?

Worked solution

Background: x = π means a half turn around the unit circle — the moving point is now on the far LEFT of the circle. sin x is the point's height; cos x is how far right (positive) or left (negative) it is.
Half way around, the point is level with the center → height = 0, so sin π = 0.
It's a full radius to the LEFT → cos π = −1.
sin π = 0    cos π = −1
4.constant multiple
Find ∫2 sin x dx.

Worked solution

Background: from Lesson 5, a constant multiplier just rides along through integration: ∫2·(something) dx = 2·∫(something) dx. So this is the sine rule from problem 2, doubled.
∫2 sin x dx = 2 · ∫sin x dx = 2 · (−cos x) + C = −2 cos x + C.
Check by differentiating: (−2 cos x)′ = −2 · (−sin x) = 2 sin x ✓.
−2 cos x + C
5.constant multiple
Find ∫3 cos x dx.

Worked solution

Background: same idea — the constant 3 rides along. Cosine is the "friendly" one: its antiderivative is plain sin x, no minus sign anywhere.
∫3 cos x dx = 3 · sin x + C = 3 sin x + C.
Check: (3 sin x)′ = 3 cos x ✓.
3 sin x + C
6.check by differentiating
A friend claims ∫sin x dx = −cos x + C but isn't sure about the minus sign. Settle it: differentiate −cos x, show every sign step, and explain in one sentence why the minus sign has to be there.

Worked solution

Background: "check by differentiating" is the universal answer-checker for integrals: if F′ gives back the function under the integral sign, F is right — no trust required. Here the key derivative fact is (cos x)′ = −sin x.
Differentiate: (−cos x)′ = −(cos x)′ = −(−sin x) = sin x ✓. Two minus signs cancel.
Why the minus must be there: differentiating cos x produces a minus sign, so we need to start with a minus already in place to cancel it and land on plain sin x.
(−cos x)′ = sin x, so ∫sin x dx = −cos x + C is correct
7.sum of waves
Find ∫(sin x + cos x) dx, and check by differentiating.

Worked solution

Background: from Lesson 5, integrals split over plus signs: integrate each piece separately and add. The only danger here is putting the minus sign on the wrong piece — it belongs to the sine piece's answer.
∫sin x dx = −cos x and ∫cos x dx = sin x.
Add them: −cos x + sin x + C.
Check: (−cos x + sin x)′ = sin x + cos x ✓.
sin x − cos x + C
8.mixed with power rule
Find ∫(x² + sin x) dx.

Worked solution

Background: old tools and new tools work side by side. The power rule (Lesson 5) says ∫x² dx = x³/3, and this lesson says ∫sin x dx = −cos x. Integrate term by term.
∫x² dx = x³/3   and   ∫sin x dx = −cos x.
Together: x³/3 − cos x + C.
Check: (x³/3 − cos x)′ = x² − (−sin x) = x² + sin x ✓.
x³/3 − cos x + C
9.definite integral
Compute ∫₀π/2 cos x dx.

Worked solution

Background: the FTC (Lessons 6–8): find an antiderivative, plug in the top limit, plug in the bottom limit, subtract. Antiderivative of cos x is sin x. You'll need the value table: sin(π/2) = 1 and sin 0 = 0.
∫₀π/2 cos x dx = [sin x]₀π/2 = sin(π/2) − sin 0.
= 1 − 0 = 1.
Sanity check: cos x stays positive from 0 to π/2, so a positive area makes sense.
1
10.definite integral
Compute ∫₀π/2 sin x dx. (Careful — this one has the minus-sign antiderivative AND a subtraction of limits. Track every sign.)

Worked solution

Background: the antiderivative of sin x is −cos x, so the FTC gives [−cos x] between the limits: (−cos of top) − (−cos of bottom). Double negatives WILL appear — write every step down rather than juggling signs in your head. Table values: cos(π/2) = 0, cos 0 = 1.
∫₀π/2 sin x dx = [−cos x]₀π/2 = (−cos(π/2)) − (−cos 0).
= (−0) − (−1) = 0 + 1 = 1.
Check the picture: sine climbs from 0 up to 1 on this stretch, all above the axis — a positive area a bit less than a 1-by-(π/2 ≈ 1.57) rectangle. 1 fits nicely.
1
11.the famous hump
Compute ∫₀^π sin x dx, showing every sign step.

Worked solution

Background: from 0 to π, sine rises from 0 to 1 and comes back down to 0 — one complete hump above the axis. This integral is ITS area. Table values needed: cos π = −1 and cos 0 = 1.
∫₀^π sin x dx = [−cos x]₀^π = (−cos π) − (−cos 0).
= (−(−1)) − (−1) = 1 + 1 = 2.
The area under one hump of sine is exactly 2. A smooth wave, a perfect whole number — worth remembering forever.
2
12.signed area
Compute ∫π/2π cos x dx. Your answer will be negative — explain what that negative means using the graph of cos x.

Worked solution

Background: from Lesson 9, a definite integral counts area BELOW the x-axis as negative. Between π/2 and π, cos x runs from 0 down to −1 — the whole stretch is below the axis, so expect a negative answer. Table values: sin π = 0, sin(π/2) = 1.
∫π/2π cos x dx = [sin x]π/2π = sin π − sin(π/2).
= 0 − 1 = −1.
Meaning: the region between the cosine curve and the axis there has size 1, but it hangs below the axis, so the integral reports it as −1. If cos x were a rate (like water flow), this stretch would be "flowing out."
−1 (an area of size 1, below the axis)
13.spot the error
A student writes: ∫sin x dx = cos x + C. Use check-by-differentiating to show this is wrong, then write the correct answer.

Worked solution

Background: this is THE most common sine/cosine integration mistake in the world, and differentiating catches it instantly. The test: differentiate the claimed answer — you must get back exactly what's under the integral sign, sign included.
Differentiate the claim: (cos x)′ = −sin x. But the integral wanted +sin x. The sign is wrong → the claim is false.
Correct answer: −cos x + C. Verify: (−cos x)′ = −(−sin x) = sin x ✓.
Wrong, because (cos x)′ = −sin x. Correct: ∫sin x dx = −cos x + C
14.net zero
Compute ∫₀2π sin x dx, and explain in a sentence why the answer makes sense from the picture of the sine wave.

Worked solution

Background: 0 to 2π is one complete lap of the wave: a hump above the axis (0 to π), then a matching hump below (π to 2π). Signed area (Lesson 9) means the two can cancel. Table values: cos 2π = 1, cos 0 = 1.
∫₀2π sin x dx = [−cos x]₀2π = (−cos 2π) − (−cos 0) = (−1) − (−1) = −1 + 1 = 0.
Why it makes sense: the up-hump contributes +2 and the identical down-hump contributes −2; over a full cycle they cancel exactly.
0 — the hump above the axis cancels the hump below
15.true area
Problem 14 says the integral of sin x over [0, 2π] is 0 — but the wave clearly encloses real area! Find the TOTAL area between the curve y = sin x and the x-axis from 0 to 2π (counting the below-axis part as positive area, the way a painter buying paint would).

Worked solution

Background: from Lessons 9–10: when a curve dips below the axis, "true area" ≠ "signed integral." The recipe: split the interval where the curve crosses the axis, integrate each piece, and add the SIZES (absolute values). Sine crosses at x = π.
Piece 1 (above axis): ∫₀^π sin x dx = [−cos x]₀^π = 1 + 1 = 2.
Piece 2 (below axis): ∫_π2π sin x dx = [−cos x]_π2π = (−cos 2π) − (−cos π) = (−1) − (1) = −2. Its size is |−2| = 2.
Total area = 2 + 2 = 4.
4 (while the signed integral is 0)
16.half a hump
Compute ∫π/2π sin x dx. Then explain why your answer is exactly half of the full hump from problem 11.

Worked solution

Background: the sine hump on [0, π] is perfectly symmetric around its peak at x = π/2 — the downhill half mirrors the uphill half. So integrating just the second half should give exactly half the hump's area. Table values: cos π = −1, cos(π/2) = 0.
∫π/2π sin x dx = [−cos x]π/2π = (−cos π) − (−cos(π/2)).
= (−(−1)) − (−0) = 1 − 0 = 1.
Half of the full hump's area 2 is 1 ✓ — the symmetry and the computation agree.
1 (half of the hump's area of 2, by symmetry)
17.average value
Using the Lesson 14 formula, find the average value of sin x on the interval [0, π]. Give the exact answer and a decimal estimate.

Worked solution

Background: Lesson 14's formula: average value = (integral over the interval) ÷ (length of the interval) — the height of the flat rectangle that holds the same area as the curve. Here the interval [0, π] has length π, and problem 11 already gave us the integral.
Integral: ∫₀^π sin x dx = 2 (the hump).
Average = 2 ÷ π = 2/π.
Decimal: 2/π ≈ 2 ÷ 3.14159 ≈ 0.64. Sensible: sine spends time near its peak of 1 and near 0 at the ends, so an average a bit above ½ rings true.
2/π ≈ 0.64
18.net zero · cosine
Compute ∫₀2π cos x dx, and explain the answer using signed area.

Worked solution

Background: cosine over one full lap starts at 1, dips down to −1, and climbs back to 1. Like sine, it spends equal amounts of "area effort" above and below the axis over a full cycle — so expect cancellation. Table values: sin 2π = 0, sin 0 = 0.
∫₀2π cos x dx = [sin x]₀2π = sin 2π − sin 0 = 0 − 0 = 0.
Signed-area story: cos x is above the axis on [0, π/2], below on [π/2, 3π/2], above again on [3π/2, 2π]. The positive pieces (1 + 1) exactly cancel the negative middle piece (−2).
0 — over a full cycle, cosine's above-axis and below-axis areas cancel
19.story · tides
The tide flows into a small harbor at the rate r(t) = sin t million liters per hour, where t is hours after low tide, valid from t = 0 to t = 2π (one full tide cycle, about 6.28 hours). (a) How much water flows IN during the first half of the cycle, from t = 0 to t = π? (b) What happens during the second half, from t = π to t = 2π? (c) How much more water is in the harbor at the end of the full cycle than at the start?

Worked solution

Background: from Lessons 1 and 13: integrating a flow RATE over a time interval gives the TOTAL amount accumulated. When the rate is negative, water is flowing OUT. This is the sine wave as nature actually uses it — tides really do rise and fall on (roughly) a sine schedule.
(a) Water in = ∫₀^π sin t dt = [−cos t]₀^π = 1 + 1 = 2 → 2 million liters flow in.
(b) On [π, 2π], sin t is negative — the tide reverses and water flows OUT. Amount: ∫_π2π sin t dt = (−cos 2π) − (−cos π) = −1 − 1 = −2, i.e. 2 million liters flow back out.
(c) Net change = ∫₀2π sin t dt = 0. The harbor ends the cycle holding exactly what it started with — the in-flow and out-flow cancel perfectly.
(a) 2 million liters in   (b) 2 million liters flow out   (c) net change 0
20.challenge
Compute ∫₀π (sin x + cos x) dx with full working: find the antiderivative of each term, evaluate at both limits, and track every sign.

Worked solution

Background: everything in this lesson at once — term-by-term integration, the minus-sign rule for sine, the FTC, and the value table (cos π = −1, cos 0 = 1, sin π = 0, sin 0 = 0). Write each stage on its own line and the signs stay tame.
Antiderivative: ∫(sin x + cos x) dx = −cos x + sin x. Check: (−cos x + sin x)′ = sin x + cos x ✓.
Top limit x = π: −cos π + sin π = −(−1) + 0 = 1.
Bottom limit x = 0: −cos 0 + sin 0 = −1 + 0 = −1.
Subtract: 1 − (−1) = 2. (Sensible: the sine part alone gives 2 — the famous hump — and the cosine part over [0, π] gives sin π − sin 0 = 0. Total 2 + 0 = 2 ✓.)
