Lesson 27 — FTC Part 2: Derivatives of Accumulation Functions

The Rate at Which the Total Grows · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 27 of 40

📚 All lessons← Prev: Lesson 26Next: Lesson 28 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: meet the accumulation function — an integral whose upper limit is a variable, like g(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt — and learn the second half of the Fundamental Theorem: the derivative of the total is the current rate, g′(x) = f(x). This one line is the beating heart of AP Calculus, and by the end of today you'll be able to differentiate integrals you could never compute by hand.

1 The accumulation function: an integral that's still running

  • Every definite integral you've done so far had two fixed numbers on it: ∫₀⁵ f(t) dt is one number, done, finished. Today we let the upper limit stay open:
    g(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt  =  "the total collected from 0 up to x"
  • This is a function of x. Feed in x = 3 and you get the number ∫₀³ f(t) dt. Feed in x = 7 and you get ∫₀⁷ f(t) dt. Different stopping points, different totals. g(x) = the total collected so far, as a function of where you stop.
  • The odometer story: if f(t) is your speed at time t, then g(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt is your odometer reading at time x — the distance accumulated from the start of the trip up to time x. As x moves forward, g(x) keeps updating. It's an integral that's still running.
  • Why is there a t inside and an x outside? The letter t is a dummy variable — a placeholder that sweeps through all the moments from 0 to x while the adding-up happens, then vanishes. It's exactly like the index k in sigma notation: in Σₖ₌₁ⁿ k², the answer depends on n, not on k — k is just the counter doing the sweeping. Same here: the answer g(x) depends on x (where you stop), not on t.
  • That's why we must NOT write ∫₀ˣ f(x) dx — the letter x would be doing two jobs at once (the stopping point AND the sweeping variable), and the expression becomes ambiguous nonsense. One letter, one job: t sweeps inside, x waits outside.

2 The theorem: FTC Part 2

  • Since g(x) is a function, we can ask the derivative question: how fast is g growing? How fast is the odometer ticking up? You already know the answer from real life: the odometer ticks up at exactly your current speed. That everyday fact is the theorem:
    FTC Part 2:  d/dx ∫ax f(t) dt = f(x)
  • In words: the RATE at which the total grows equals the CURRENT height being added. If water pours into a tank at 5 L/min right now, then the total amount in the tank is growing at 5 L/min right now. Nothing else could be true!
  • Picture it: g(x) is the area collected under f from a up to x. Nudge x forward by a sliver dx. The new area gained is a skinny strip of height f(x) and width dx — area ≈ f(x)·dx. So the growth rate is f(x)·dx / dx = f(x). The area grows at the speed of the current height.
  • This closes the circle we opened in Lesson 4. FTC Part 1 said: to compute an integral, undo a derivative (∫ₐᵇ f = F(b) − F(a)). FTC Part 2 says the reverse: if you differentiate an integral, you get the original function back. Integrate then differentiate — you return home. Differentiation and integration are true inverses, like squaring and square-rooting.

3 Immediate examples — and a superpower

Example 1: d/dx ∫₂ˣ t² dt = x². Just replace every t with x. Done.
(Check the long way: ∫₂ˣ t² dt = x³/3 − 8/3, and its derivative is x². ✓ Same answer, no antiderivative needed.)
Example 2: d/dx ∫₀ˣ √(1+t³) dt = √(1+x³).
Here's the superpower: nobody on Earth can write a simple antiderivative of √(1+t³) — it doesn't exist in terms of familiar functions. Yet the derivative of its accumulation function is instant. FTC 2 works even for integrals we could never do by hand!
  • The lower limit doesn't matter. Compare g(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt and h(x) = ∫₅ˣ f(t) dt. They differ only by the fixed chunk ∫₀⁵ f(t) dt — a constant. Constants die under d/dx, so g′ = h′ = f(x). Starting the odometer in a different city changes the reading, but not how fast it ticks.

4 The chain-rule upgrade: a moving upper limit

  • What if the upper limit isn't plain x but a function of x, like x²? Then g is a composition — "integrate up to (x²)" — and compositions call for the chain rule: plug the upper limit into f, then multiply by the derivative of the upper limit:
    d/dx ∫₀^(x²) f(t) dt = f(x²) · 2x
  • Why the extra factor? When x nudges forward by dx, the upper limit x² lurches forward by 2x·dx — the stopping point moves faster than x does, so area gets swallowed 2x times as fast.
Worked example: find d/dx ∫₁^(x³) cos t dt.
Step 1 — plug the upper limit into f: cos(x³).
Step 2 — multiply by the derivative of the upper limit: d/dx(x³) = 3x².
Answer: cos(x³) · 3x².
(Check the long way: ∫₁^(x³) cos t dt = sin(x³) − sin 1; its derivative is cos(x³)·3x². ✓)

5 Flipped: the variable in the LOWER limit

  • Sometimes the x sits on the bottom: ∫ₓ⁵ f(t) dt. Use the flip rule from Lesson 10 — swapping the limits flips the sign: ∫ₓ⁵ f(t) dt = −∫₅ˣ f(t) dt.
  • Now it's a normal accumulation function with a minus sign in front:
    d/dx ∫ₓ⁵ f(t) dt = −f(x)
  • Makes sense: as x grows, the interval [x, 5] shrinks, so the total is being eaten away — the derivative should be negative (when f is positive).

6 Using g′ = f to analyze g — the bridge to Lesson 28

  • You know from derivative-land: a function is increasing where its derivative is positive and decreasing where its derivative is negative. But now we know g′ IS f. So:
Question about gJust look at f!
Where is g increasing?where f > 0 (positive stuff is being added)
Where is g decreasing?where f < 0 (negative stuff is being added)
What is g′(3)?it's just f(3)
What is g(a)? (a = lower limit)0 — because ∫ₐᵃ = 0: you haven't collected anything yet
  • This turns hard-looking questions about a mysterious integral function into easy sign-reading questions about f. In Lesson 28 you'll be handed the graph of f and asked everything about g — the single most classic AP Calculus problem. Today's theorem is the key that unlocks it.

1.plain FTC 2
Let g(x) = ∫₀ˣ t² dt. Find g′(x).

Worked solution

Background: g(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt is an accumulation function — the total area under f collected from 0 up to x. FTC Part 2 says its derivative is simply the current height: g′(x) = f(x). To use it, take the function inside and replace the dummy variable t with x.
Here f(t) = t². By FTC 2, g′(x) = f(x) = x².
Check the long way: g(x) = ∫₀ˣ t² dt = x³/3, and d/dx (x³/3) = x². Same answer — but FTC 2 needed no antiderivative at all.
g′(x) = x²
2.plain FTC 2
Find d/dx ∫₂ˣ (t³ + 5t) dt.

Worked solution

Background: FTC 2: d/dx ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt = f(x), no matter what the lower limit a is. The lower limit only shifts g by a constant, and constants vanish under d/dx.
The inside function is f(t) = t³ + 5t. Replace t with x:
d/dx ∫₂ˣ (t³ + 5t) dt = x³ + 5x. The "2" on the bottom plays no role in the derivative.

3.impossible integral, easy derivative
Find d/dx ∫₀ˣ √(1 + t³) dt. (Fun fact: no simple antiderivative of √(1 + t³) exists!)

Worked solution

Background: some functions, like √(1+t³), have no antiderivative writable with familiar functions — you can never "find F and plug in." But FTC 2 doesn't care: the accumulation function still exists (the area is real!), and its derivative is still just the inside function evaluated at x.
By FTC 2, the answer is the integrand with t replaced by x.

g′(x) = 1/(1 + x²).
g′(1) = 1/(1 + 1²) = 1/2.

5.g(a) = 0
Let g(x) = ∫₃ˣ cos t dt. Find g(3) and g′(x). (One of these needs no computing at all.)

Worked solution

Background: an integral from a number to itself, ∫ₐᵃ, is always 0 — the region has zero width, so zero area is collected. For an accumulation function g(x) = ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt, that means g(a) = 0 automatically: at the starting line, nothing has accumulated yet.
g(3) = ∫₃³ cos t dt = 0. No work needed — you haven't gone anywhere yet.
g′(x) = cos x by FTC 2.
g(3) = 0,   g′(x) = cos x
6.dummy variable · conceptual
A student defines an accumulation function as g(x) = ∫₀ˣ x² dx. Explain why this notation is broken, and write it correctly. Why must the inside variable be a different letter, like t?

Worked solution

Background: in g(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt, the letter t is a dummy variable: it sweeps through every moment from 0 to x while the adding-up happens, then disappears from the answer. It's exactly like the index k in Σₖ₌₁ⁿ k² — the sum depends on n, never on k. The upper limit x is the real input: it says where to stop.
In "∫₀ˣ x² dx", the letter x is doing two different jobs at once: it's the fixed stopping point (upper limit) AND the sweeping variable that's supposed to run from 0 up to that stopping point. A single letter can't both stand still and move — the expression is ambiguous.
Correct version: g(x) = ∫₀ˣ t² dt. Now t sweeps from 0 to x (and vanishes), while x waits outside as the input. One letter, one job.
Write g(x) = ∫₀ˣ t² dt; the inside letter must differ from the limit so each letter has exactly one job
7.lower limit doesn't matter
Let g(x) = ∫₀ˣ 2t dt and h(x) = ∫₃ˣ 2t dt. (a) Compute g(x) and h(x) explicitly using FTC Part 1. (b) Find g′(x) and h′(x). (c) What is g(x) − h(x), and why does it explain part (b)?

Worked solution

Background: this problem lets you SEE why the lower limit is irrelevant to the derivative. Two accumulation functions with the same f but different starting points differ by a constant — the fixed chunk of area between the two starting points — and constants have derivative 0.
(a) An antiderivative of 2t is t². So g(x) = x² − 0² = x², and h(x) = x² − 3² = x² − 9.
(b) g′(x) = 2x and h′(x) = 2x. Identical! Both match FTC 2's prediction: the inside function with t → x.
(c) g(x) − h(x) = x² − (x² − 9) = 9, which is exactly ∫₀³ 2t dt = 9 — the head start g has over h. A constant gap between two functions means equal derivatives: they climb at the same rate, just from different floors.
(a) g = x², h = x² − 9   (b) both derivatives are 2x   (c) g − h = 9, a constant, so g′ = h′
8.chain rule limit
Find d/dx ∫₀^(x²) sin t dt.

Worked solution

Background: when the upper limit is a function of x (here x²), the accumulation function is a composition: "integrate up to (something)". The chain rule applies: plug the upper limit into f, then multiply by the derivative of the upper limit. d/dx ∫ₐ^(u(x)) f(t) dt = f(u(x)) · u′(x).
Plug the upper limit into f: sin(x²).
Multiply by the derivative of the upper limit: d/dx (x²) = 2x.
Check the long way: ∫₀^(x²) sin t dt = 1 − cos(x²); its derivative is sin(x²)·2x. ✓
sin(x²) · 2x
9.chain rule limit
Find d/dx ∫₁^(x³) cos t dt.

Worked solution

Background: same chain-rule pattern: f(upper limit) × (derivative of upper limit). The stopping point x³ races ahead of x, so the area accumulates faster by the factor 3x².
f(t) = cos t and the upper limit is x³, so the first factor is cos(x³).
d/dx (x³) = 3x², so multiply: cos(x³) · 3x².
3x² cos(x³)
10.chain rule limit
Find d/dx ∫₀^(2x) √(1 + t²) dt.

Worked solution

Background: the chain-rule factor appears even for simple upper limits like 2x — its derivative is 2, not 1, so forgetting it silently doubles/halves your answer. Always ask: "what is the upper limit, and what is ITS derivative?"
Plug the upper limit 2x into f: √(1 + (2x)²) = √(1 + 4x²).
Multiply by d/dx (2x) = 2.

11.flipped limits
Find d/dx ∫ₓ⁵ t² dt.

Worked solution

Background: when the variable sits in the LOWER limit, first flip the limits (which flips the sign, Lesson 10): ∫ₓ⁵ = −∫₅ˣ. Then it's a standard accumulation function with a minus sign riding along: the derivative is −f(x).
∫ₓ⁵ t² dt = −∫₅ˣ t² dt.
d/dx [−∫₅ˣ t² dt] = −x².
Sense check: as x grows toward 5, the interval [x, 5] shrinks, so positive area is being LOST — a negative derivative is exactly right.
−x²
12.flipped limits
Find d/dx ∫ₓ⁷ sin(t²) dt.

Worked solution

Background: sin(t²) is another famous function with no elementary antiderivative (it appears in optics as the "Fresnel integral"). Doesn't matter: flip the limits, pick up a minus, apply FTC 2.
∫ₓ⁷ sin(t²) dt = −∫₇ˣ sin(t²) dt.

13.full analysis
Let g(x) = ∫₁ˣ (t² − 4) dt for x ≥ 1. Find: (a) g(1); (b) g′(x); (c) g′(3); (d) the interval where g is decreasing and the interval where g is increasing, with justification.

Worked solution

Background: this is the classic four-question bundle about an accumulation function. Two free facts do most of the work: g(lower limit) = 0 (zero-width integral), and g′ = f (FTC 2). Then "where is g increasing?" becomes "where is f positive?" — a sign question about t² − 4.
(a) g(1) = ∫₁¹ (t² − 4) dt = 0. Starting line: nothing collected.
(b) g′(x) = x² − 4 by FTC 2.
(c) g′(3) = 3² − 4 = 5.
(d) g′(x) = x² − 4 = (x − 2)(x + 2). For x in (1, 2): g′ < 0 (e.g. at x = 1.5, 2.25 − 4 < 0), so g is decreasing on (1, 2). For x > 2: g′ > 0, so g is increasing on (2, ∞). Justification sentence: "g is decreasing on (1, 2) because g′(x) = x² − 4 is negative there, and increasing on (2, ∞) because g′ is positive there."
(a) 0   (b) x² − 4   (c) 5   (d) decreasing on (1, 2), increasing on (2, ∞), since g′ = x² − 4 changes sign at x = 2
14.evaluate g and g′
Let g(x) = ∫₀ˣ 1/(1 + t²) dt. Find g(0), g′(0), and g′(1). Then say in one sentence what g′(0) = 1 means about the graph of g near x = 0.

Worked solution

Background: g(0) uses the zero-width fact; the derivatives use FTC 2. And remember the meaning of a derivative: g′(0) is the slope of g's graph at x = 0.
g(0) = ∫₀⁰ = 0.
g′(x) = 1/(1 + x²), so g′(0) = 1/(1 + 0) = 1 and g′(1) = 1/(1 + 1) = 1/2.
Meaning: near x = 0 the graph of g passes through (0, 0) climbing with slope 1 — locally it looks like the line y = x. (This g is actually arctan x, and this is why arctan x ≈ x for small x!)
g(0) = 0,   g′(0) = 1,   g′(1) = 1/2; near 0, g climbs like the line y = x
15.tangent line · AP flavor
Let g(x) = ∫₂ˣ 2t dt. Write the equation of the tangent line to the graph of g at x = 3. (You need two things: the point and the slope. Get g(3) by geometry — the region under f(t) = 2t from t = 2 to t = 3 is a trapezoid — and get the slope from FTC 2.)

Worked solution

Background: a tangent line at x = a is y = g(a) + g′(a)(x − a). For accumulation functions, the two ingredients come from two different tools: g(a) is an AREA (geometry or FTC 1), while g′(a) = f(a) is free from FTC 2. A trapezoid's area is (average of the two parallel sides) × (width).
Point: g(3) = ∫₂³ 2t dt = area under the line y = 2t from 2 to 3. The trapezoid has parallel sides f(2) = 4 and f(3) = 6 and width 1, so area = ((4 + 6)/2) × 1 = 5. So the point is (3, 5). (Check with FTC 1: t² from 2 to 3 gives 9 − 4 = 5. ✓)
Slope: g′(x) = 2x by FTC 2, so g′(3) = 6.
Tangent line: y = 5 + 6(x − 3), i.e. y = 6x − 13.
y = 5 + 6(x − 3)   (equivalently y = 6x − 13)
16.spot the error
A student wrote: d/dx ∫₀^(x²) √(1 + t³) dt = √(1 + x⁶). The plugging-in is actually correct — (x²)³ = x⁶ — but the answer is still wrong. Find the error and fix it.

Worked solution

Background: the single most common FTC 2 mistake on the AP exam: substituting the upper limit into f correctly but FORGETTING the chain-rule factor. Whenever the upper limit is anything other than plain x, its derivative must multiply the answer.
The upper limit is u = x², a function of x, so the chain rule demands the factor u′ = 2x.
Correct answer: √(1 + (x²)³) · 2x = 2x√(1 + x⁶).
Quick self-test forever after: "Is the upper limit plain x? If not, multiply by its derivative."
Missing chain-rule factor: correct answer is 2x·√(1 + x⁶)
17.both limits moving · challenge
Find d/dx ∫ₓ^(x²) t dt. (Hint: split the integral at a constant, say 0: ∫ₓ^(x²) = ∫₀^(x²) − ∫₀ˣ. Then check your answer by computing the integral exactly first.)

Worked solution

Background: when BOTH limits contain x, split the integral at any constant c: ∫ₓ^(x²) f = ∫꜀^(x²) f − ∫꜀ˣ f (the pieces from c chain together, Lesson 10). Each piece is now a one-moving-limit problem: the first gets the chain rule, the second is plain FTC 2.
Split: ∫ₓ^(x²) t dt = ∫₀^(x²) t dt − ∫₀ˣ t dt.
First piece: f(x²) · 2x = x² · 2x = 2x³. Second piece: x. So the derivative is 2x³ − x.
Check exactly: ∫ₓ^(x²) t dt = [t²/2] from x to x² = x⁴/2 − x²/2. Its derivative: 2x³ − x. ✓ Perfect match.
2x³ − x
18.max/min analysis · AP flavor
Let g(x) = ∫₀ˣ (t − 1)(t − 3) dt. (a) Find g′(x) and all critical points of g. (b) Classify each as a local max or local min of g, with a justification sentence. (c) Show that g(3) = 0 by computing the integral with FTC Part 1.

Worked solution

Background: critical points of g are where g′ = 0. Since g′ = f (FTC 2), that means: where f = 0. To classify, use the first-derivative test: a local MAX where g′ = f switches + to −, a local MIN where it switches − to +. Graders want the sign-change stated explicitly.
(a) g′(x) = (x − 1)(x − 3) by FTC 2. Critical points where this is 0: x = 1 and x = 3.
(b) Sign of g′: positive for x < 1 (test x = 0: (−1)(−3) = 3 > 0), negative on (1, 3) (test x = 2: (1)(−1) = −1 < 0), positive for x > 3 (test x = 4: (3)(1) = 3 > 0). So: "g has a local maximum at x = 1 because g′ changes from positive to negative there, and a local minimum at x = 3 because g′ changes from negative to positive there."
(c) (t − 1)(t − 3) = t² − 4t + 3, antiderivative t³/3 − 2t² + 3t. At t = 3: 27/3 − 18 + 9 = 9 − 18 + 9 = 0; at t = 0: 0. So g(3) = 0 − 0 = 0. (The positive area from 0 to 1 exactly cancels the negative area from 1 to 3.)
(a) g′ = (x−1)(x−3); critical points x = 1, 3   (b) local max at x = 1 (+ to −), local min at x = 3 (− to +)   (c) g(3) = 0
19.second derivative · challenge
Let g(x) = ∫₀ˣ √(1 + t³) dt for x ≥ 0. (a) Explain why g is increasing for ALL x ≥ 0. (b) Find g″(x). (c) Is g concave up or concave down for x > 0? Justify.

Worked solution

Background: g′ = f (FTC 2), so g″ = f′ — the SECOND derivative of the accumulation function is the ordinary derivative of the inside function. Concave up means g″ > 0. To differentiate √(1 + x³) = (1 + x³)^(1/2), use the chain rule: ½(1 + x³)^(−1/2) · 3x².
(a) g′(x) = √(1 + x³), which is a square root of a positive number, hence > 0 for every x ≥ 0. A function whose derivative is always positive is always increasing. Sentence: "g is increasing on [0, ∞) because g′(x) = √(1 + x³) > 0 there."
(b) g″(x) = d/dx (1 + x³)^(1/2) = ½(1 + x³)^(−1/2) · 3x² = 3x² / (2√(1 + x³)).
(c) For x > 0: the top 3x² > 0 and the bottom 2√(1 + x³) > 0, so g″(x) > 0 — g is concave up. (The heights being added keep getting taller, so the total grows faster and faster.)
(a) g′ = √(1+x³) > 0 always   (b) g″ = 3x²/(2√(1+x³))   (c) concave up, since g″ > 0 for x > 0
20.challenge · full AP style
The graph of f consists of straight line segments: f starts at (0, 2), stays flat at height 2 until t = 2, then descends in a straight line from (2, 2) to (4, −2) (crossing zero at t = 3), then stays flat at height −2 until t = 6.
f(t) 2 | ●━━━━━━━● (0,2)→(2,2) | ╲ 0 +────┬────┬──╲─┬────┬────┬──→ t | 1 2 3╲ 4 5 6 −2 | ●━━━━━━━━━● (4,−2)→(6,−2)
Let g(x) = ∫₀ˣ f(t) dt. (a) Find g(2), g(3), g(4), and g(6). (b) Find g′(1). (c) On what interval is g increasing? Justify. (d) Find the absolute maximum value of g on [0, 6], and justify your answer.

Worked solution

Background: this is THE classic AP problem shape (a full lesson on it is next!). Values of g come from geometry — areas of rectangles and triangles, counted negative below the axis. Shape questions about g come from FTC 2: g′ = f, so read f's sign. For an absolute max on a closed interval, compare g at the critical point and at both endpoints.
(a) g(2): rectangle, height 2 × width 2 = 4. g(3) = g(2) + triangle from 2 to 3 (base 1, height 2): 4 + ½·1·2 = 5. g(4) = g(3) + area from 3 to 4, which is BELOW the axis: triangle ½·1·2 = 1 counted negative → 5 − 1 = 4. g(6) = g(4) + rectangle below the axis (height −2, width 2) → 4 − 4 = 0.
(b) g′(1) = f(1) = 2 (FTC 2 — just read the height of f at t = 1).
(c) g is increasing on (0, 3) because g′ = f is positive there (f is at height 2, then descending but still above zero until t = 3).
(d) Critical point: g′ = f changes from positive to negative at x = 3, so g has its only local max there, with g(3) = 5. Compare with endpoints: g(0) = 0 and g(6) = 0. The largest candidate is 5. Sentence: "The absolute maximum of g on [0, 6] is 5, at x = 3, because g′ = f changes from positive to negative only at x = 3, and g(3) = 5 exceeds the endpoint values g(0) = 0 and g(6) = 0."
(a) g(2) = 4, g(3) = 5, g(4) = 4, g(6) = 0   (b) 2   (c) increasing on (0, 3) since f > 0 there   (d) absolute max 5 at x = 3
