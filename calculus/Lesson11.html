Lesson 11 — Motion I: From Velocity to Position

Motion I: From Velocity to Position · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 11 of 40

🎯 Goal of this lesson: use integration on the physics problem it was practically invented for — motion. Given how fast something is moving at every moment (its velocity), find out where it is. One formula does it: new position = starting position + ∫ v dt. And Lesson 9's signed areas come alive: negative velocity literally means going backward.

1 The vocabulary — position and velocity, from scratch

  • Position, written s, means where you are — a spot on a number line. Think of mile markers on a highway: "s = 40" means you're standing at mile marker 40. Position can be zero (home base) or even negative (behind home base).
  • Velocity, written v, means how fast your position is changing — it's speed WITH a direction sign. Positive v = moving forward (position increasing); negative v = moving backward (position decreasing). A car cruising at v = −30 mph is doing 30 mph in reverse.
  • The calculus connection you already know from derivatives: velocity is the derivative of position. v tells the rate at which s changes, moment by moment — the speedometer is reporting the derivative of the odometer's story.

2 The integration link — velocity in, position out

  • If v is the derivative of s, then going backwards — from v to s — is integration. This is our Lesson 1 story with official names: velocity is a rate, position is the total it builds.
  • The change in position from time a to time b is called the displacement, and it's an integral: displacement = ∫ab v dt. It answers "how far did your position shift?" — not where you ended up.
  • To know where you actually ARE, you also need to know where you started (the integral can't know that — remember the "+C" mystery from Lesson 4!). The master formula:
Position formula: s(b) = s(a) + ∫ab v dt  — new position = starting position + accumulated velocity. In words: where you end up = where you began, plus every little bit of motion added up.

3 Worked example — a simple trip

Problem: an object moves with velocity v(t) = 2t m/s (it speeds up steadily). At time t = 0 it sits at position s = 3 m. Where is it at t = 4 seconds?

Step 1 — displacement: ∫04 2t dt = t2 |04 = 16 − 0 = 16 meters of forward motion. (Check the antiderivative: derivative of t² is 2t ✓.)
Step 2 — add the start: s(4) = 3 + 16 = 19 meters.

Don't forget the starting 3! The integral only measures the CHANGE; the starting position anchors it to the real number line.

4 Negative velocity — Lesson 9 comes alive

  • In Lesson 9, area below the x-axis counted as negative. For motion, that's not a bookkeeping trick — it's physical: when v is negative, you are moving backward, and your position goes DOWN.
  • So there are two different "how far" questions, and they get different answers when you back up:
    • Displacement (net): ∫ v dt with signs kept — "how far from where you started?" Forward and backward motion cancel.
    • Total distance traveled: add every piece as positive — "what does the odometer say?" An odometer never runs backward.
  • Walk 5 blocks east, then 5 blocks west: displacement 0 (you're home), total distance 10 blocks (your legs know it). To compute total distance with integrals: find where v = 0, integrate each piece separately, then add the absolute values — exactly the Lesson 9 net-vs-total recipe.

5 Worked example with a sign change

Problem: v(t) = 6 − 2t m/s on [0, 5]. Find the displacement AND the total distance traveled.

Where does v change sign? Solve v = 0: 6 − 2t = 0 → t = 3. Before t = 3, v is positive (test t = 0: v = 6, forward). After, negative (test t = 4: v = −2, backward). The object coasts forward, stops for an instant at t = 3, then reverses.

Forward piece: ∫03 (6 − 2t) dt = (6t − t2) |03 = (18 − 9) − 0 = +9 m.
Backward piece: ∫35 (6 − 2t) dt = (6t − t2) |35 = (30 − 25) − (18 − 9) = 5 − 9 = −4 m.

Displacement (net): 9 + (−4) = +5 m — it ends 5 m ahead of where it began.
Total distance: 9 + 4 = 13 m — the odometer counted the backing up too.

6 Reading a velocity graph

  • On a velocity graph, everything from Lessons 2 and 9 has a motion meaning: area above the time-axis pushes you forward; area below pulls you back.
v (m/s) 6 \ | \ forward: +9 m | \ (area above axis) | \ 0 +----\--------------→ t (s) | 3\ backward: −4 m | \ (area below axis) −4 | \ 0 3 5
  • That's the v = 6 − 2t example as a picture: a triangle above the axis (worth +9) and a smaller triangle below (worth −4). Net = 5, total = 13 — you can read both answers straight off the areas.
  • Rectangle and triangle shortcuts still work: if the velocity graph is made of flat steps and straight slants, you can find displacement by pure geometry — no antiderivatives needed.
✏️ Exercises — 20 problems (write your answer on paper, then reveal)
1.constant velocity
A robot rolls at a constant velocity of 5 m/s, starting from position s = 0. Where is it after 10 seconds?

Worked solution

Background (physics): velocity is the rate of change of position — "5 m/s" means the position number grows by 5 every second. New position = starting position + ∫ v dt. With a constant velocity the integral is just rate × time, our very first formula from Lesson 1.
Displacement = ∫010 5 dt = 5t |010 = 50 m.
Position = start + displacement = 0 + 50 = 50 m.
s = 50 meters
2.mile markers
A car passes mile marker 20 and cruises at a steady 60 mph. What mile marker is it at 3 hours later?

Worked solution

Background: mile markers ARE a position number line — the marker number is s. The integral gives how much the marker number changes; the starting marker anchors where you actually are. Forgetting the starting 20 is the classic mistake.
Displacement = ∫03 60 dt = 60t |03 = 180 miles.
Position = 20 + 180 = 200.
Mile marker 200
3.start + integral
A turtle starts at position s = 4 m and walks with constant velocity 3 m/s. Write its position formula s(t), then find where it is at t = 6 s.

Worked solution

Background: instead of answering one time at a time, you can build a position FORMULA: s(t) = s(0) + ∫₀ᵗ v du. It's an antiderivative of v whose "+C" is chosen to make s(0) come out right — this is what the +C from Lesson 4 is for.
s(t) = 4 + ∫0t 3 du = 4 + 3t. (Check: derivative of 4 + 3t is 3 = v ✓, and s(0) = 4 ✓.)
At t = 6: s = 4 + 18 = 22.
s(t) = 4 + 3t; s(6) = 22 meters
4.displacement
An object's velocity is v(t) = 2t m/s. Find its displacement between t = 1 and t = 3 seconds.

Worked solution

Background: displacement between two times is the definite integral of velocity between those times — no starting position needed, because "how much did s change?" doesn't ask where s began. Notice the limits are 1 and 3, not 0 and 3.
∫13 2t dt = t2 |13 = 9 − 1 = 8. (Check: derivative of t² is 2t ✓.)
8 meters
5.going backward
A remote-control car starts at position s = 12 m and drives with velocity v = −2 m/s (in reverse!) for 5 seconds. Where does it end up?

Worked solution

Background: negative velocity means the position number is going DOWN — the car moves toward smaller markers. The integral of a negative velocity is negative, and adding a negative displacement to the start moves you backward. Same formula, no special cases.
Displacement = ∫05 (−2) dt = −2t |05 = −10 m.
Position = 12 + (−10) = 2.
s = 2 meters
6.graph in words
A velocity graph shows v = 4 m/s (flat) from t = 0 to t = 3, then v = 0 from t = 3 to t = 5. Using rectangle areas only — no antiderivatives — find the displacement over the whole 5 seconds, and describe what the object did.

Worked solution

Background: displacement = area under the velocity graph (above the axis counts +, below counts −). Flat steps make rectangles, so geometry does the whole job. And v = 0 means NOT MOVING — a zero-height rectangle adds nothing.
Rectangle 1: height 4, width 3 → area 12 (moves forward 12 m).
Rectangle 2: height 0, width 2 → area 0 (parked for 2 seconds).
Displacement = 12 + 0 = 12 m. Story: it cruised forward for 3 seconds, then stood still.
12 meters forward (then parked)
7.curvy velocity
An object has velocity v(t) = 3t2 m/s and starts at position s = 1 m. Where is it at t = 2 seconds?

Worked solution

Background: now the velocity is a curve, so no rectangle trick works — but the power rule (Lesson 5) handles it: an antiderivative of 3t² is t³. Then it's the same two steps as always: integrate the velocity, add the start.
Displacement = ∫02 3t2 dt = t3 |02 = 8 − 0 = 8 m. (Check: derivative of t³ is 3t² ✓.)
Position = 1 + 8 = 9.
s = 9 meters
8.two-term velocity
Find the displacement of an object with velocity v(t) = t + 2 m/s from t = 0 to t = 4.

Worked solution

Background: integrate term by term, like adding two motions: a speeding-up part (t) and a steady part (2). The antiderivative of t is t²/2 and of 2 is 2t — check each by differentiating.
∫04 (t + 2) dt = (t2/2 + 2t) |04. (Check: derivative of t²/2 + 2t is t + 2 ✓.)
At 4: 16/2 + 8 = 8 + 8 = 16. At 0: 0.
16 meters
9.when does it stop?
A cart has velocity v(t) = 6 − 2t m/s. (a) At what time does it momentarily stop? (b) How far has it traveled by that moment (starting from t = 0)?

Worked solution

Background: "stops" means velocity = 0 — the instant between moving forward and (possibly) moving backward, like a ball at the top of a toss. To find it, solve v(t) = 0. Up to that moment v stayed positive, so displacement and distance agree.
(a) 6 − 2t = 0 → t = 3 seconds.
(b) ∫03 (6 − 2t) dt = (6t − t2) |03 = 18 − 9 = 9 m. (Check: derivative of 6t − t² is 6 − 2t ✓.)
(a) t = 3 s   (b) 9 meters
10.back to the start
A toy drone (moving along a straight track) starts at s = 0 with velocity v(t) = 4 − 2t m/s. (a) When does it turn around? (b) How far out does it get? (c) When is it back at its starting point?

Worked solution

Background: three different moments, three different equations. Turning around = velocity hits 0. Farthest point = position at that turnaround moment. Back at start = POSITION returns to 0, which happens later — the drone needs time to undo all its forward progress.
(a) v = 0: 4 − 2t = 0 → t = 2 s.
(b) Position formula: s(t) = 0 + ∫0t (4 − 2u) du = 4t − t2. (Check: derivative is 4 − 2t ✓.) At t = 2: s = 8 − 4 = 4 m out.
(c) Back at start: 4t − t² = 0 → t(4 − t) = 0 → t = 4 s (t = 0 is the departure).
Nice symmetry: 2 seconds out, 2 seconds back.
(a) t = 2 s   (b) 4 m from the start   (c) t = 4 s
11.net vs total
A delivery robot moves with v = 2 m/s from t = 0 to t = 3, then v = −1 m/s from t = 3 to t = 7. Find (a) its displacement and (b) its total distance traveled.

Worked solution

Background: displacement keeps the signs (backward motion subtracts — it's "how far from where you started"). Total distance treats every meter as positive (the odometer view). With flat velocities each piece is just rate × time, signs included.
Forward piece: 2 × 3 = +6 m. Backward piece: (−1) × 4 = −4 m. (Width of second piece: 7 − 3 = 4 s.)
(a) Displacement = 6 + (−4) = +2 m — it ends 2 m ahead.
(b) Total distance = 6 + 4 = 10 m — every meter counts.
(a) +2 meters   (b) 10 meters
12.triangle graph
A sprinter's velocity graph is a straight line climbing from v = 0 at t = 0 up to v = 6 m/s at t = 4 s. Using the triangle area formula, how far does the sprinter run in those 4 seconds?

Worked solution

Background: displacement is the area under the velocity graph, and this graph makes a triangle (base along the time axis, sloped top). Triangle area = ½ × base × height. Units check: seconds × (meters/second) = meters ✓.
Base = 4 s, height = 6 m/s.
Area = ½ × 4 × 6 = 12.
(Calculus double-check: the line is v = 1.5t, and ∫04 1.5t dt = 0.75t2 |04 = 12 ✓.)
12 meters
13.checkpoints
A scooter starts at position s = 5 m with velocity v(t) = 2t m/s. Find its position at t = 1, t = 3, and t = 5 seconds.

Worked solution

Background: rather than three separate integrals, build the position formula once — s(t) = start + ∫₀ᵗ v — then plug in each checkpoint time. This is how GPS units think: one running total, sampled whenever you ask.
s(t) = 5 + ∫0t 2u du = 5 + t². (Check: derivative is 2t ✓, s(0) = 5 ✓.)
t = 1: s = 5 + 1 = 6.   t = 3: s = 5 + 9 = 14.   t = 5: s = 5 + 25 = 30.
s(1) = 6 m, s(3) = 14 m, s(5) = 30 m
14.round trip
An object moves with v(t) = 3 − t m/s from t = 0 to t = 6. Show that it ends up exactly where it started, and find the total distance it traveled anyway.

Worked solution

Background: a displacement of ZERO doesn't mean nothing happened — it means the forward trip and the backward trip cancelled exactly, like walking to a friend's house and back. Net = 0, but the odometer (total distance) records both legs. Split at v = 0 to see the two legs.
v = 0 at t = 3. Antiderivative: 3t − t2/2. (Check: derivative is 3 − t ✓.)
Forward leg: ∫03 (3 − t) dt = (9 − 9/2) − 0 = +9/2.
Backward leg: ∫36 (3 − t) dt = (18 − 18) − (9 − 9/2) = 0 − 9/2 = −9/2.
Net = 9/2 − 9/2 = 0 — back at the start ✓. Total distance = 9/2 + 9/2 = 9.
Displacement 0; total distance 9 meters
15.starts backward
An object has velocity v(t) = t2 − 4 m/s on [0, 3]. (a) When does it turn around? (b) Find its displacement and its total distance traveled.

Worked solution

Background: this one starts out BACKWARD: at t = 0, v = −4 (negative!). It slows its retreat, turns around when v = 0, then comes forward. Same recipe: find the sign change, integrate each piece, keep signs for net, strip signs for total. Antiderivative of t² − 4 is t³/3 − 4t (derivative check: t² − 4 ✓).
(a) v = 0: t² = 4 → t = 2 (only the positive root matters on [0, 3]).
Backward piece: ∫02 (t2 − 4) dt = (t3/3 − 4t) |02 = (8/3 − 8) − 0 = −16/3.
Forward piece: ∫23 (t2 − 4) dt = (27/3 − 12) − (8/3 − 8) = (9 − 12) − (−16/3) = −3 + 16/3 = 7/3.
(b) Displacement = −16/3 + 7/3 = −9/3 = −3 m (net: 3 m BEHIND its start). Quick check integrating straight across: (27/3 − 12) − 0 = −3 ✓.
Total distance = 16/3 + 7/3 = 23/3 m.
(a) t = 2 s   (b) displacement −3 m; total distance 23/3 m (≈ 7.67)
16.speeding up then slowing
A go-kart starts at position s = 10 m with velocity v(t) = 6t − t2 m/s. (a) Check that it never moves backward between t = 0 and t = 6. (b) Where is it at t = 6?

Worked solution

Background: before splitting an integral, it's worth checking whether v ever actually goes negative. Factor it: 6t − t² = t(6 − t), and a product is ≥ 0 when both factors are — here for all t between 0 and 6. No sign change means displacement = total distance, one integral, no splitting.
(a) v = t(6 − t): on [0, 6], t ≥ 0 and 6 − t ≥ 0, so v ≥ 0. It only pauses at t = 0 and t = 6.
(b) Displacement = ∫06 (6t − t2) dt = (3t2 − t3/3) |06 = (108 − 72) − 0 = 36 m. (Check: derivative of 3t² − t³/3 is 6t − t² ✓.)
Position = 10 + 36 = 46 m.
(a) v = t(6 − t) ≥ 0 on [0, 6]   (b) s = 46 meters
17.out and back with a start
A cat walks along a fence starting at position s = 2 m, with velocity v(t) = 6 − 3t m/s. (a) Build the position formula s(t). (b) What is the farthest position it reaches? (c) When is it back at s = 2?

Worked solution

Background: the full toolkit in one problem. Position formula = start + antiderivative of v (choose the version worth 0 at t = 0). Farthest point happens at the turnaround (v = 0). "Back where it started" means s(t) equals the STARTING VALUE 2, not zero.
(a) s(t) = 2 + ∫0t (6 − 3u) du = 2 + 6t − 3t²/2. (Check: derivative is 6 − 3t ✓, s(0) = 2 ✓.)
(b) Turnaround: v = 0 → 6 = 3t → t = 2. Then s(2) = 2 + 12 − 6 = 8 m.
(c) s(t) = 2 → 6t − 3t²/2 = 0 → factor out 3t/2: (3t/2)(4 − t) = 0 → t = 4 s (t = 0 is the departure). Check: s(4) = 2 + 24 − 24 = 2 ✓.
(a) s(t) = 2 + 6t − 3t²/2   (b) farthest s = 8 m at t = 2   (c) back at t = 4 s
18.graph story · challenge
A velocity graph, described in words: v = 8 m/s (flat) for the first 5 seconds; then a straight line dropping from 8 down to 0 over the next 4 seconds; then v = −2 m/s (flat) for the final 3 seconds. Using only rectangles and triangles, find (a) the displacement and (b) the total distance for the whole 12 seconds.

Worked solution

Background: a piecewise velocity graph splits the trip into chapters — read each chapter's area from geometry, keeping the sign: above the axis +, below −. Rectangle = height × width; triangle = ½ × base × height. Then net keeps the signs and total drops them.
Chapter 1 (rectangle): 8 × 5 = +40 m.
Chapter 2 (triangle above axis): ½ × 4 × 8 = +16 m.
Chapter 3 (rectangle below axis): (−2) × 3 = −6 m.
(a) Displacement = 40 + 16 − 6 = 50 m. (b) Total distance = 40 + 16 + 6 = 62 m.
(a) +50 meters   (b) 62 meters
19.two-stage trip · challenge
A rover starts at position s = 1 m. For the first 3 seconds its velocity is v(t) = 2t m/s; after t = 3 it locks in a constant 6 m/s. Where is the rover at t = 7 seconds?

Worked solution

Background: when the velocity rule CHANGES mid-trip, integrate each stage with its own rule and chain the positions: the end of stage 1 becomes the start of stage 2. (Notice v is smooth here: 2t reaches 6 exactly at t = 3, then stays there.)
Stage 1: displacement = ∫03 2t dt = t2 |03 = 9 m → position at t = 3 is 1 + 9 = 10 m.
Stage 2: displacement = ∫37 6 dt = 6 × 4 = 24 m (constant rate × 4 seconds).
Position at t = 7: 10 + 24 = 34 m.
s = 34 meters
20.grand challenge
An object moves with velocity v(t) = t2 − 4t + 3 m/s on [0, 4]. Find every time it turns around, then compute its net displacement and its total distance traveled.

Worked solution

Background: a velocity that changes sign TWICE: forward, backward, forward again — like pacing in a hallway. Factor to find the turnarounds: t² − 4t + 3 = (t − 1)(t − 3), zero at t = 1 and t = 3. Integrate each of the three pieces with the antiderivative F(t) = t³/3 − 2t² + 3t (derivative check: t² − 4t + 3 ✓), then: net keeps the signs, total adds absolute values.
Sign check: at t = 0, v = 3 (+); at t = 2, v = 4 − 8 + 3 = −1 (−); at t = 4, v = 16 − 16 + 3 = 3 (+). Turnarounds at t = 1 and t = 3.
F values: F(0) = 0; F(1) = 1/3 − 2 + 3 = 4/3; F(3) = 9 − 18 + 9 = 0; F(4) = 64/3 − 32 + 12 = 64/3 − 20 = 4/3.
Piece [0, 1]: F(1) − F(0) = +4/3. Piece [1, 3]: F(3) − F(1) = 0 − 4/3 = −4/3. Piece [3, 4]: F(4) − F(3) = 4/3 − 0 = +4/3.
Net displacement = 4/3 − 4/3 + 4/3 = 4/3 m. (Shortcut check: F(4) − F(0) = 4/3 ✓.)
Total distance = 4/3 + 4/3 + 4/3 = 12/3 = 4 m. Three equal legs — a tidy coincidence!
Turnarounds at t = 1 s and t = 3 s; net = 4/3 m; total = 4 m
