Lesson 18 — Substitution II: u-Substitution

Undoing the Chain Rule · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 18 of 40

📚 All lessons← Prev: Lesson 17Next: Lesson 19 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: learn the most powerful integration tool in this course — u-substitution. Last lesson you handled a LINEAR inside (like 3x + 2) with the divide-by-a trick. Today the inside can be anything (like x² + 1), as long as you can spot one telltale clue: the derivative of the inside is sitting right there in the integral.

1 The next-level problem — and a suspicious coincidence

  • Try this one: ∫ 2x(x²+1)4 dx. There's an "inside function" again — the x²+1 tucked inside the 4th power.
  • Your first instinct might be Lesson 17: integrate the outside and divide by something. Stop! That trick was only legal because the inside was linear (its derivative was a plain constant, so dividing by that constant fixed everything). Here the inside is x²+1, whose derivative is 2x — that's not a constant, and you can never "divide by 2x" to fix an answer. The divide trick is illegal here.
  • But look closer at the problem. The derivative of the inside is 2x... and there's a 2x sitting right there as a factor in the integral. That is not a coincidence.
  • Remember what the chain rule does when you differentiate something with an inside: derivative of the outside × derivative of the inside. So whenever a function like (x²+1)5 gets differentiated, a copy of 2x pops out in front. Seeing 2x(x²+1)4 is like finding a chain-rule fingerprint at the scene: this integrand was produced by a chain-rule differentiation, and our job is to run it backwards.
the chain-rule fingerprint ∫ 2x (x²+1)⁴ dx ↑ ↑ │ └── the INSIDE function └──────── the derivative of that inside (2x), waiting there as a factor ✓ fingerprint!

2 The u-substitution ritual — six steps, every time

  • The trick is to rename the messy inside with a single letter, u, and rewrite the WHOLE integral in the u-language. If the fingerprint is there, every x disappears and you're left with an easy integral.
The recipe (memorize this — it never changes):
StepWhat you doFor ∫ 2x(x²+1)⁴ dx
1Pick u = the insideu = x²+1
2Compute du = (derivative of u) dxdu = 2x dx
3Swap everything into u-language∫ u4 du — every x is gone!
4Integrate the easy u-integralu5/5 + C
5Substitute back (u → inside)(x²+1)5/5 + C
6CHECK by differentiatingchain rule: 5(x²+1)4 · 2x / 5 = 2x(x²+1)4 ✓
  • In step 3, notice how the pieces pair up: the (x²+1)4 becomes u4, and the leftover 2x dx is exactly du. The fingerprint factor gets absorbed into du — that's why it had to be there.
  • Step 6 is not optional politeness — it's your safety net. Differentiating takes ten seconds and catches every mistake.

3 How to CHOOSE u

  • Rule of thumb: u = the inside — whatever is inside a power, inside an e-exponent, or inside a sin/cos.
  • Then run the fingerprint check: does the derivative of your u appear as a multiplier in the integral (exactly, or off by just a constant factor)? If yes, u-substitution will work. If no x-stuff matches, it won't.
IntegralThe inside → udu needsIs it there?
∫ 2x(x²+1)4 dxu = x²+12x dxyes — exactly ✓
∫ 3x² ex³ dxu = x³3x² dxyes — exactly ✓
∫ cos x (sin x)² dxu = sin xcos x dxyes — exactly ✓
∫ x(x²+3)4 dxu = x²+32x dxalmost — off by the constant 2 (fixable, next section)
∫ (x²+1)4 dxu = x²+12x dxno — no x factor at all (u-sub fails, section 6)

4 Handling a constant mismatch — the "borrow" move

  • Try ∫ x(x²+3)4 dx. Pick u = x²+3, so du = 2x dx. We need 2x dx but the integral only offers x dx. Off by a factor of 2 — a constant, and constants are always fixable.
  • Just solve the du equation for the piece you actually have: du = 2x dx means x dx = du/2. That's the borrow.
Worked: ∫ x(x²+3)4 dx with u = x²+3, du = 2x dx, x dx = du/2.
Swap: ∫ u4 · (du/2) = ½ ∫ u4 du = ½ · u5/5 = u5/10.
Back: (x²+3)5/10 + C.
Check: derivative = 5(x²+3)4 · 2x / 10 = x(x²+3)4 ✓ — the 5, the 2, and the 10 cancel perfectly.
  • This only works for a missing constant. If the mismatch involves x (say du needs x² but you only have x), no borrowing can save you — that's a genuine failure (section 6).

5 More worked examples — powers, e, and sin

Example (e-flavored): ∫ 2x ex² dx. Inside the exponent: u = x², so du = 2x dx — there it is, exactly.
Swap: ∫ eu du = eu + C. Back: ex² + C.
Check: d/dx ex² = ex² · 2x ✓ (e is its own derivative; the chain rule multiplies by the inside's derivative).
Example (e with a cube): ∫ 3x² ex³ dx. Take u = x³, du = 3x² dx — exact match.
Swap: ∫ eu du = eu + C. Back: ex³ + C.
Check: d/dx ex³ = ex³ · 3x² = 3x² ex³ ✓.
Example (trig inside a power): ∫ cos x (sin x)² dx. The inside of the square is sin x, so u = sin x, du = cos x dx — exact match.
Swap: ∫ u² du = u³/3 + C. Back: (sin x)³/3 + C.
Check: d/dx (sin x)³/3 = 3(sin x)² · cos x / 3 = cos x (sin x)² ✓.
  • Notice the pattern in all three: rename the inside, watch its derivative get absorbed into du, integrate something easy, swap back, check. Same six steps every single time.

6 When u-substitution DOESN'T work — recognizing failure is a skill

  • Try ∫ (x²+1)4 dx — same inside as before, but with no 2x factor (and no x factor of any kind).
  • Pick u = x²+1, du = 2x dx. To swap, you'd need a 2x dx in the integral — or at least an x dx to borrow with. There's nothing. You can't invent an x out of thin air (dividing by 2x is not allowed — only constants can be borrowed).
  • So u-substitution simply fails here. The honest way through is old-fashioned: expand the power and integrate term by term — (x²+1)²= x⁴+2x²+1 is quick; the 4th power is longer but doable.
  • Knowing when a tool doesn't apply is just as valuable as using it. A good habit: run the fingerprint check FIRST, before touching anything. No fingerprint (up to a constant) → no u-substitution.

7 Definite integrals with u-substitution

  • What about ∫₀¹ 2x(x²+1)² dx, with limits attached? Keep it simple — one method, three moves:
  • Move 1: ignore the limits and find the indefinite antiderivative by u-substitution. Here: u = x²+1, du = 2x dx, so ∫ u² du = u³/3.
  • Move 2: substitute back to x-language: (x²+1)³/3.
  • Move 3: now use the FTC exactly as always — plug in the limits: (1²+1)³/3 − (0²+1)³/3 = 8/3 − 1/3 = 7/3.
  • (There is a fancier method where the limits themselves get converted to u-values, but it's easy to garble — we'll stick to "solve indefinite, swap back, then plug in." It always works.)

Worked solution

Background: every integration answer can be verified by differentiating it — if you get back the original integrand, the answer is right. For a power with an inside, use the chain rule: bring the exponent down, lower the power by one, then multiply by the derivative of the inside.

17.story problem
Pollution flows into a lake at a rate of r(t) = 4t(t²+4) grams per hour, where t is in hours. How many grams enter the lake between t = 0 and t = 2? (Use u-substitution — then notice you could check by expanding.)

Worked solution

Background: total accumulated = the definite integral of the rate (Lessons 1–3 and 13). Here the rate has a u-sub structure: inside = t²+4, derivative 2t, and we have 4t = 2 · (2t) — a constant multiple, so it works.
u = t²+4, du = 2t dt, so 4t dt = 2 du.
Indefinite: ∫ u · 2 du = 2 · u²/2 = u² → back: (t²+4)².
Check: derivative of (t²+4)² = 2(t²+4) · 2t = 4t(t²+4) ✓.
Plug in limits: at t = 2: (4+4)² = 64. At t = 0: (0+4)² = 16. Total = 64 − 16 = 48.
48 grams of pollution
