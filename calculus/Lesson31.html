Lesson 31 — Integration by Parts II: Repeats, ln x & Definite

When Once Isn't Enough · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 31 of 40

← Lesson 30📚 All lessonsNext: Lesson 32 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: level up integration by parts. You'll learn to use parts twice in a row (for things like x²eˣ), to crack the famous ∫ ln x dx with a sneaky trick, to handle definite integrals with parts, and to use a fast "tabular" shortcut. By the end you'll also have a clear decision guide: parts, or u-substitution?

1 Quick recap — the formula and LIATE (from Lesson 30)

  • Integration by parts is the product rule run backwards. The formula:
    ∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du
    You split the integrand into two pieces — u (something you'll differentiate) and dv (something you'll antidifferentiate) — and trade your integral for a hopefully easier one, ∫ v du.
  • LIATE tells you what to pick for u. Scan the list top to bottom; whatever appears first in your integrand becomes u: Logs (ln x) · Inverse trig (arctan x) · Algebraic (x, x², polynomials) · Trig (sin x, cos x) · Exponential (eˣ).
  • Why it works: things near the top of LIATE get simpler when differentiated (ln x becomes 1/x; x² becomes 2x), while things near the bottom are easy to antidifferentiate (eˣ stays eˣ). Parts wants u to shrink and dv to be tame.
  • Lesson 30's flagship: ∫ x eˣ dx. Pick u = x, dv = eˣ dx → du = dx, v = eˣ → answer x eˣ − eˣ + C. We'll need this exact result again in a minute!

2 Parts TWICE — the ∫ x²eˣ dx story

  • Try ∫ x² eˣ dx. LIATE: x² is Algebraic, eˣ is Exponential, so u = x², dv = eˣ dx → du = 2x dx, v = eˣ.
  • First pass: ∫ x² eˣ dx = x² eˣ − ∫ eˣ · 2x dx = x² eˣ − 2 ∫ x eˣ dx.
  • Hmm — ∫ x eˣ dx is not instant… but it IS easier: the power dropped from x² to x. And we already know it needs parts again. That's fine! Parts is allowed to be repeated.
  • Second pass (Lesson 30's result, or redo it: u = x, dv = eˣ dx): ∫ x eˣ dx = x eˣ − eˣ.
  • Plug it back in, distributing the −2 carefully over both terms:
    ∫ x² eˣ dx = x² eˣ − 2(x eˣ − eˣ) + C = x² eˣ − 2x eˣ + 2eˣ + C
  • Check by differentiating (always!): d/dx of x²eˣ − 2xeˣ + 2eˣ = (2x eˣ + x²eˣ) − (2eˣ + 2x eˣ) + 2eˣ = x²eˣ. The 2xeˣ terms cancel, the 2eˣ terms cancel — only x²eˣ survives ✓.
  • The pattern: each pass of parts knocks the polynomial's power down by one. x² needs 2 passes, x³ would need 3. The polynomial is being "differentiated to death" while the eˣ (or sin, cos) just goes along for the ride.

3 The sneaky classic — ∫ ln x dx

  • Parts is for products… but ∫ ln x dx has no visible product. It's just ln x sitting alone. Are we stuck?
  • The trick: there IS a product hiding in plain sight — ln x · 1. Write dv = dx (that's the "times 1" part) and let u be the whole ln x:
  • u = ln x, dv = dx → du = (1/x) dx, v = x.
  • Now the formula:
    ∫ ln x dx = x ln x − ∫ x · (1/x) dx = x ln x − ∫ 1 dx = x ln x − x + C
    The magic: inside the new integral, x and 1/x cancel — the hard log disappears and we're left integrating the number 1.
  • Check it: d/dx of x ln x − x = (1 · ln x + x · 1/x) − 1 = ln x + 1 − 1 = ln x ✓ (product rule on x ln x, and the +1 and −1 cancel perfectly).
  • This "dv = dx" move works for any lone awkward function whose derivative is nicer than itself — ln x and arctan x are the two famous customers.

4 Definite integration by parts

  • For a definite integral, everything gets limits — including the uv part:
    ∫ab u dv = [uv]ab − ∫ab v du
    [uv]ab means: plug b into uv, plug a into uv, subtract — exactly like FTC evaluation.
  • Flagship: the area under y = ln x from 1 to e. Using Section 3's antiderivative:
    ∫1e ln x dx = [x ln x − x]1e = (e·ln e − e) − (1·ln 1 − 1) = (e·1 − e) − (0 − 1) = 0 − (−1) = 1
    (Remember: ln e = 1 and ln 1 = 0.) The area under ln from 1 to e is exactly 1 — a shockingly clean answer.

5 The tabular shortcut (optional, but fun)

  • When the integrand is polynomial × eˣ (or sin/cos), repeated parts follows a rhythm you can put in a table. Column D: differentiate the polynomial down to 0. Column I: antidifferentiate the other factor the same number of times. Signs alternate +, −, +, −…
signD (differentiate)I (antidifferentiate)
+x²eˣ
−2xeˣ
+2eˣ
0 (stop!)eˣ
  • Now multiply diagonally (each D entry times the I entry one row lower), attaching the signs:
    ∫ x² eˣ dx = + x²·eˣ − 2x·eˣ + 2·eˣ + C — the same answer as Section 2, in three lines!
  • It's not a new method — it's parts-twice with the bookkeeping pre-organized. Use it whenever the polynomial will eventually differentiate to 0.

6 Decision guide — which tool do I grab?

What you seeToolExample
a factor's "fingerprint" du is presentu-sub (check this FIRST — it's cheaper)∫ x ex² dx (that x is du's fingerprint)

  • Always check u-sub first. ∫ x ex² dx looks like a parts problem (x times an exponential) but it's really u-sub: with u = x², du = 2x dx is sitting right there. Parts would make it worse. The question to ask: "is one factor (almost) the derivative of something inside the other factor?"
✏️ Exercises — 20 problems (write your answer on paper, then reveal)
1.warm-up · the formula
Write the integration by parts formula from memory, then use it with u = x and dv = eˣ dx to find ∫ x eˣ dx.

Worked solution

Background: the parts formula ∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du comes from running the product rule backwards: since (uv)′ = u′v + uv′, integrating both sides and rearranging trades one product-integral for another. You pick u to differentiate and dv to antidifferentiate.
Formula: ∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du.
With u = x, dv = eˣ dx: differentiate u → du = dx; antidifferentiate dv → v = eˣ.
∫ x eˣ dx = x·eˣ − ∫ eˣ dx = x eˣ − eˣ + C.
Check by differentiating: (eˣ + x eˣ) − eˣ = x eˣ ✓.
x eˣ − eˣ + C
2.one pass · trig
Find ∫ x cos x dx using integration by parts.

Worked solution

Background: LIATE says the Algebraic factor (x) beats the Trig factor (cos x) for the role of u. Differentiating x turns it into 1 — it vanishes from the new integral, leaving pure trig. Recall: the antiderivative of cos x is sin x, and the antiderivative of sin x is −cos x.
u = x, dv = cos x dx → du = dx, v = sin x.
∫ x cos x dx = x sin x − ∫ sin x dx = x sin x − (−cos x) + C = x sin x + cos x + C.
Check: d/dx[x sin x + cos x] = (sin x + x cos x) − sin x = x cos x ✓.
x sin x + cos x + C
3.u-sub or parts?
Find ∫ x ex² dx. (Careful — is this really a parts problem?)

Worked solution

Background: this is the classic trap from the decision guide. "x times an exponential" LOOKS like Lesson 30, but check for a fingerprint first: if u = x², then du = 2x dx — and there's an x dx sitting in the integral. When the fingerprint is present, u-sub wins; it's one clean step instead of a parts battle.
Let u = x² → du = 2x dx → x dx = du/2.
∫ x ex² dx = ∫ eu · (du/2) = (1/2) eu + C = (1/2) ex² + C.
Check: d/dx[(1/2)ex²] = (1/2)ex² · 2x = x ex² ✓ (chain rule).
(1/2) ex² + C  (u-sub, NOT parts!)
4.the sneaky classic
Find ∫ ln x dx using the dv = dx trick. Show every step.

Worked solution

Background: ln x has no visible partner, so we invent one: ln x = ln x · 1, and the "1 dx" becomes dv. This works because ln x's DERIVATIVE (1/x) is much friendlier than ln x itself — parts moves the difficulty from ln x onto 1/x, where it cancels.
u = ln x, dv = dx → du = (1/x) dx, v = x.
∫ ln x dx = x ln x − ∫ x · (1/x) dx = x ln x − ∫ 1 dx.
= x ln x − x + C.
Check: d/dx[x ln x − x] = ln x + x·(1/x) − 1 = ln x + 1 − 1 = ln x ✓.
x ln x − x + C
5.check task
A classmate claims ∫ ln x dx = x ln x − x + C. Verify the claim by differentiating x ln x − x, showing the product rule in full.

Worked solution

Background: the product rule says (f·g)′ = f′g + fg′. Checking an antiderivative by differentiating is the single best habit in integration: differentiation is mechanical, so it catches any slip instantly. The check should reproduce EXACTLY the original integrand.
d/dx[x ln x]: here f = x, g = ln x, so f′g + fg′ = 1·ln x + x·(1/x) = ln x + 1.
d/dx[−x] = −1.
Total: ln x + 1 − 1 = ln x — exactly the integrand.
Claim verified: derivative = ln x ✓
6.one pass · trig
Find ∫ x sin x dx, and check your answer by differentiating.

Worked solution

Background: same setup as ∫ x cos x dx but with a sign to watch: the antiderivative of sin x is −cos x (because d/dx[−cos x] = sin x). Minus signs are where most parts mistakes live — write every one down.
u = x, dv = sin x dx → du = dx, v = −cos x.
∫ x sin x dx = x·(−cos x) − ∫ (−cos x) dx = −x cos x + ∫ cos x dx.
= −x cos x + sin x + C.
Check: d/dx[−x cos x + sin x] = (−cos x + x sin x) + cos x = x sin x ✓.
−x cos x + sin x + C
7.parts twice
Find ∫ x² eˣ dx by using integration by parts twice. Show both passes.

Worked solution

Background: with x², one pass of parts isn't enough — differentiating x² gives 2x, still a polynomial. But that's progress: each pass knocks the power down by one. Two passes takes x² → 2x → 2, and a constant times eˣ integrates instantly.
Pass 1: u = x², dv = eˣ dx → du = 2x dx, v = eˣ. So ∫ x²eˣ dx = x²eˣ − 2∫ x eˣ dx.
Pass 2 (on ∫ x eˣ dx): u = x, dv = eˣ dx → x eˣ − ∫ eˣ dx = x eˣ − eˣ.
Combine, distributing the −2 over BOTH terms: x²eˣ − 2(x eˣ − eˣ) + C = x²eˣ − 2x eˣ + 2eˣ + C.
Check: derivative = (2xeˣ + x²eˣ) − (2eˣ + 2xeˣ) + 2eˣ = x²eˣ ✓.
x² eˣ − 2x eˣ + 2eˣ + C
8.parts twice · trig
Find ∫ x² cos x dx. (Two passes — keep the signs organized!)

Worked solution

Background: polynomial × trig works exactly like polynomial × eˣ: differentiate the polynomial down while the trig factor cycles (cos → sin → −cos → −sin → cos…). Two passes are needed for x², and the trig cycle supplies the sign changes.
Pass 1: u = x², dv = cos x dx → du = 2x dx, v = sin x. So ∫ x² cos x dx = x² sin x − 2∫ x sin x dx.
Pass 2: from exercise 6, ∫ x sin x dx = −x cos x + sin x.
Combine: x² sin x − 2(−x cos x + sin x) + C = x² sin x + 2x cos x − 2 sin x + C.
Check: derivative = (2x sin x + x² cos x) + (2 cos x − 2x sin x) − 2 cos x = x² cos x ✓ — everything cancels except x² cos x.
x² sin x + 2x cos x − 2 sin x + C
9.ln as u
Find ∫ x ln x dx. (Hint: LIATE — which factor is u?)

Worked solution

Background: LIATE puts Logs before Algebraic, so u = ln x even though x "looks simpler." The reason: we don't KNOW an antiderivative of ln x by heart (that took a trick!), but we can differentiate it easily. Parts should differentiate the awkward factor and antidifferentiate the easy one.
u = ln x, dv = x dx → du = (1/x) dx, v = x²/2.
∫ x ln x dx = (x²/2) ln x − ∫ (x²/2)(1/x) dx = (x²/2) ln x − ∫ (x/2) dx.
= (x²/2) ln x − x²/4 + C.
Check: derivative = x ln x + (x²/2)(1/x) − x/2 = x ln x + x/2 − x/2 = x ln x ✓.
(x²/2) ln x − x²/4 + C
10.classify · the decision habit
For each integral, decide: u-sub or parts? Then solve the two u-sub ones. (a) ∫ x cos(x²) dx   (b) ∫ x cos x dx   (c) ∫ (ln x)/x dx   (d) ∫ x² eˣ dx

Worked solution

Background: the decision test is: "is one factor the fingerprint (derivative) of something inside the other?" If yes → u-sub, always cheaper. If the factors are unrelated (like x and cos x) → parts. Watch how a tiny change — cos(x²) vs cos x — flips the answer completely.
(a) u-sub: inside is x², and its fingerprint 2x dx is nearly present. u = x² → du = 2x dx → ∫ = (1/2)∫cos u du = (1/2) sin(x²) + C.
(b) parts: x is not the derivative of anything inside cos x (the inside is just x… but then the "fingerprint" would be 1, not x). Unrelated factors → parts. (Answer from ex. 2: x sin x + cos x + C.)
(c) u-sub: u = ln x → du = (1/x) dx, exactly present! ∫ = ∫ u du = u²/2 + C = (ln x)²/2 + C.
(d) parts, twice: x² and eˣ are unrelated; the fingerprint of x² would be 2x… but it's multiplying eˣ of plain x, not e of x². (Answer from ex. 7.)
(a) u-sub: (1/2)sin(x²) + C   (b) parts   (c) u-sub: (ln x)²/2 + C   (d) parts twice
11.definite · the flagship
Compute ∫1e ln x dx exactly, and say in words what the answer means about the graph of y = ln x.

Worked solution

Background: for definite parts, either find the full antiderivative first and then apply FTC, or use [uv]ₐᵇ − ∫ₐᵇ v du. Two log facts do the arithmetic: ln e = 1 (e is the base of ln) and ln 1 = 0 (anything to the 0th power is 1).
Antiderivative (ex. 4): x ln x − x.
At x = e: e·ln e − e = e·1 − e = 0.   At x = 1: 1·ln 1 − 1 = 0 − 1 = −1.
∫1e ln x dx = 0 − (−1) = 1.
Exactly 1 — the area under y = ln x from x = 1 to x = e is precisely 1 square unit.
12.definite · trig
Compute ∫0π x cos x dx. (Why does a negative answer make sense here?)

Worked solution

Background: evaluation facts needed: sin 0 = 0, sin π = 0, cos 0 = 1, cos π = −1. A definite integral counts area below the x-axis as negative. On [0, π], x cos x is positive only on [0, π/2] where x is still small, but negative on [π/2, π] where x is large — so the negative part dominates.
Antiderivative (ex. 2): x sin x + cos x.
At π: π·sin π + cos π = π·0 + (−1) = −1.   At 0: 0·sin 0 + cos 0 = 0 + 1 = 1.
∫0π x cos x dx = (−1) − 1 = −2.
−2  (the below-axis area outweighs the above-axis area)
13.tabular
Redo ∫ x² eˣ dx using the tabular method: build the D and I columns, then read off the answer with alternating signs.

Worked solution

Background: the tabular method is repeated parts with the bookkeeping pre-done. D column: differentiate until you hit 0. I column: antidifferentiate the same number of times. Multiply diagonally (each D entry × the I entry one row DOWN), with signs +, −, +, …
D column: x² → 2x → 2 → 0.   I column: eˣ → eˣ → eˣ → eˣ.
Diagonals with signs: +(x²)(eˣ), −(2x)(eˣ), +(2)(eˣ).
Answer: x²eˣ − 2xeˣ + 2eˣ + C — matches exercise 7 exactly, with far less writing.
x² eˣ − 2x eˣ + 2eˣ + C
14.tabular · e2x
Find ∫ x² e2x dx using the tabular method. (Careful: each antiderivative of e2x picks up a factor of 1/2.)

Worked solution

Background: the antiderivative of e2x is e2x/2 (check: differentiating brings the 2 back down by the chain rule). Each trip down the I column divides by 2 again: e2x → e2x/2 → e2x/4 → e2x/8.
D column: x² → 2x → 2 → 0.   I column: e2x → e2x/2 → e2x/4 → e2x/8.
Diagonals: +x²·(e2x/2) − 2x·(e2x/4) + 2·(e2x/8).
= (x²/2)e2x − (x/2)e2x + (1/4)e2x + C.
Check: derivative = 2e2x(x²/2 − x/2 + 1/4) + e2x(x − 1/2) = e2x(x² − x + 1/2 + x − 1/2) = x² e2x ✓.
e2x(x²/2 − x/2 + 1/4) + C
15.definite · parts twice
Compute ∫01 x² eˣ dx exactly, then give a decimal estimate. (Use e ≈ 2.718.)

Worked solution

Background: "exact" means leaving e as the letter e — the decimal 2.718… never ends, so e − 2 is the perfect answer and 0.718 is just an estimate. AP graders want the exact form; the decimal is only a sanity check that the answer is plausible (positive, smallish — right for a curve under e¹ ≈ 2.7 on a width-1 interval).
Antiderivative (ex. 7): x²eˣ − 2xeˣ + 2eˣ.
At 1: e − 2e + 2e = e.   At 0: 0 − 0 + 2·e⁰ = 2.
∫01 x²eˣ dx = e − 2 ≈ 2.718 − 2 = 0.718.
e − 2  (≈ 0.718)
16.definite · ln 2 exact form
Compute ∫12 x ln x dx exactly. (Your answer will contain ln 2 — that's fine! ln 2 ≈ 0.69 is a perfectly good "exact-form" ingredient, just like π.)

Worked solution

Background: numbers like ln 2, e, and π can't be written as finite decimals, so mathematicians leave them as symbols and the answer is still "exact." An answer like 2 ln 2 − 3/4 is complete; converting to ≈ 0.64 is optional. Recall ln 1 = 0.
Antiderivative (ex. 9): (x²/2) ln x − x²/4.
At 2: (4/2) ln 2 − 4/4 = 2 ln 2 − 1.   At 1: (1/2) ln 1 − 1/4 = 0 − 1/4 = −1/4.
∫12 x ln x dx = (2 ln 2 − 1) − (−1/4) = 2 ln 2 − 3/4.
Estimate: 2(0.693) − 0.75 ≈ 1.386 − 0.75 = 0.636 — positive and about right for this region ✓.
2 ln 2 − 3/4  (≈ 0.636)
17.spot the error
A student computes ∫ x² eˣ dx like this: "Pass 1: x²eˣ − 2∫ x eˣ dx. Pass 2: ∫ x eˣ dx = xeˣ − eˣ. Final answer: x²eˣ − 2xeˣ − 2eˣ + C." Find the error, fix it, and show how differentiating the wrong answer would have exposed the mistake.

Worked solution

Background: the #1 error in repeated parts is distribution: the second-pass result gets multiplied by a NEGATIVE constant, and the minus must hit every term. −2(xeˣ − eˣ) = −2xeˣ + 2eˣ, not −2xeˣ − 2eˣ. The differentiation check catches this every single time.
The error: distributing −2 over (xeˣ − eˣ), the student wrote −2eˣ instead of +2eˣ. A minus times a minus is a plus.
Correct: x²eˣ − 2xeˣ + 2eˣ + C.
Exposure by checking: derivative of the WRONG answer = (2xeˣ + x²eˣ) − (2eˣ + 2xeˣ) − 2eˣ = x²eˣ − 4eˣ ≠ x²eˣ. The leftover −4eˣ waves a red flag.
Sign slip in the distribution; correct answer: x²eˣ − 2xeˣ + 2eˣ + C
18.tabular · cubed
Find ∫ x³ eˣ dx. (Tabular strongly recommended — that's three passes of parts!)

Worked solution

Background: x³ needs THREE passes (x³ → 3x² → 6x → 6 → 0), which is where tabular really pays off. Signs alternate +, −, +, − down the diagonals. Expect the coefficients 1, 3, 6, 6 — each is the previous one times the falling power.
D column: x³ → 3x² → 6x → 6 → 0.   I column: eˣ (four times).
Diagonals with signs: +x³eˣ − 3x²eˣ + 6xeˣ − 6eˣ.
Answer: eˣ(x³ − 3x² + 6x − 6) + C.
Check: derivative = eˣ(x³ − 3x² + 6x − 6) + eˣ(3x² − 6x + 6) = eˣ·x³ ✓ — everything except x³ cancels.
eˣ(x³ − 3x² + 6x − 6) + C
19.parts twice · signs
Find ∫ x² sin x dx, and verify your answer by differentiating it.

Worked solution

Background: polynomial × sin needs the same two passes as polynomial × cos, but starts one step later in the trig cycle: the antiderivative of sin x is −cos x, then of −cos x is −sin x. Tabular works here too — I column: sin x → −cos x → −sin x.
Tabular. D column: x² → 2x → 2 → 0.   I column (each row is the antiderivative of the row above): sin x → −cos x → −sin x → cos x.
Each diagonal pairs a D entry with the I entry one row LOWER, signs +, −, +:   +x²·(−cos x),   −2x·(−sin x),   +2·(cos x).
= −x² cos x + 2x sin x + 2 cos x + C.
Check: derivative = (−2x cos x + x² sin x) + (2 sin x + 2x cos x) − 2 sin x = x² sin x ✓ — all four extra terms cancel in pairs.
−x² cos x + 2x sin x + 2 cos x + C
20.challenge · definite tabular
Compute ∫01 x² e2x dx exactly. (Use your antiderivative from exercise 14, and simplify to a single clean fraction.)

Worked solution

Background: this combines everything in the lesson: tabular parts for the antiderivative, FTC for evaluation, and exact-form arithmetic with e² (which just means e·e ≈ 7.389). Watch for lucky cancellation at the endpoints — clean problems often hide one.
Antiderivative (ex. 14): e2x(x²/2 − x/2 + 1/4).
At 1: e²(1/2 − 1/2 + 1/4) = e²/4 — the first two terms cancel!
At 0: e⁰(0 − 0 + 1/4) = 1/4.
∫01 x²e2x dx = e²/4 − 1/4 = (e² − 1)/4 ≈ (7.389 − 1)/4 ≈ 1.597.
(e² − 1)/4  (≈ 1.60)
