3 The Real BC Skill — Choosing the Tool

  • On an exam nobody writes "use parts here." You get a bare integral and 3 minutes. This flowchart is the habit to build — run it top to bottom and stop at the first "yes."
AN UNFAMILIAR INTEGRAL LANDS ON YOUR DESK │ ├─ 1. Is it already a basic form from the toolbox table? → just write it down │ ∫sec²x dx → tan x + C │ ├─ 2. Can I rewrite it into one? (split fractions, expand, powers) │ ∫(x²+1)/x dx = ∫(x + 1/x) dx → x²/2 + ln|x| + C │ ├─ 3. Is there an inside function whose derivative is also present? → u-SUB │ ∫x e^(x²) dx (inside x², derivative 2x — x is there) → e^(x²)/2 + C │ special case: top = derivative of bottom → ln|bottom| │ ├─ 4. Is it a product of two unrelated pieces? → PARTS (LIATE) │ ∫x cos x dx (x won't help u-sub; it simplifies when differentiated) │ also: a lone ln x or arctan x → parts with dv = dx │ ├─ 5. Is it a fraction whose bottom factors (and step 3 failed)? → PARTIAL FRACTIONS │ ∫1/((x−1)(x+3)) dx │ └─ 6. Does an ∞ or a blow-up appear? → replace it with a letter, integrate, take the LIMIT ∫₁^∞ 1/x⁴ dx = lim(b→∞) [−1/(3x³)]₁ᵇ = 1/3 Golden rule: always try the CHEAP tools first. Nobody uses parts on something u-sub can do.
  • The trap trio — three look-alikes that need three different tools. Train your eye on these: ∫1/(1+x²) dx → arctan · ∫x/(1+x²) dx → u-sub, gives ln · ∫1/(1+x)² dx → power rule, gives −1/(1+x).

5 The Boss Problem — a Real BC Free-Response

Problem. Let R be the region enclosed by the parabola y = 4 − x² and the x-axis. (a) Find the area of R. (b) Find the volume of the solid formed when R is revolved about the x-axis. (c) Find the volume of the solid whose base is R and whose cross-sections perpendicular to the x-axis are squares. (d) The horizontal line y = k cuts R into two pieces of equal area. Write (but do not solve) an equation for k.
  • Where are the edges? Set 4 − x² = 0 → x = −2 and x = 2. That's our interval, every time.
  • (a) Area. A = ∫−22 (4 − x²) dx. The integrand is even (Lesson 29!), so double the half: 2∫02(4 − x²) dx = 2[4x − x³/3]02 = 2(8 − 8/3) = 2 · 16/3 = 32/3.
  • (b) Revolved — disks. Each slice is a circle of radius 4 − x², so V = π∫−22 (4 − x²)² dx. Square first: (4 − x²)² = 16 − 8x² + x⁴. Using evenness again: V = 2π[16x − 8x³/3 + x⁵/5]02 = 2π(32 − 64/3 + 32/5). Common denominator 15: (480 − 320 + 96)/15 = 256/15 → V = 512π/15.
  • (c) Square cross-sections. The slice's width is w = 4 − x², and a square of side w has area w² — the same integral as part (b) but with no π, because squares aren't circles: V = ∫−22(4 − x²)² dx = 512/15.
  • (d) Setup only. Above the line y = k the parabola runs between x = ±√(4 − k), and the strip height there is (4 − x²) − k. Half the area is 16/3, so the equation is ∫−√(4−k)√(4−k) (4 − x² − k) dx = 16/3. (AP often asks only for the setup — write it and move on.)
  • Notice how many lessons showed up in one problem: intersections (L10), even symmetry (L29), disks (L34), cross-sections (L35), and area setups (L10 again).

1.L1 callback
Water flows into a tank at a steady 15 liters per minute for 8 minutes. How much water enters? Then say which single formula from the whole course this is the simplest possible case of.

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 1 (adding up rate × time): when a rate never changes, the total is one multiplication. Everything else in these 40 lessons exists to handle the case where the rate does change.
Total = rate × time = 15 × 8 = 120 liters.
This is the simplest case of total = ∫ab r(t) dt: with r constant, the integral is just a rectangle of width 8 and height 15.
120 liters — the constant-rate case of ∫ r(t) dt
2.choose the tool
For each integral, name the technique you'd use — do NOT compute anything: (a) ∫x sin x dx (b) ∫2x(x²+5)⁴ dx (c) ∫1/(4+x²) dx (d) ∫1/((x−2)(x+1)) dx (e) ∫√x dx

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 40's flowchart: the highest-value BC skill is routing a problem before touching it. Run the checklist: basic form? rewritable? u-sub fingerprint? product? factorable fraction?
(a) A product of unrelated pieces (a polynomial and a trig function) → integration by parts, u = x.
(b) Inside is x²+5, whose derivative 2x is sitting right there → u-substitution.
(c) Shape 1/(a²+x²) with a = 2, and no derivative-of-inside present → inverse trig (arctan) form.
(d) A fraction with a factored bottom, and the top (1) is not the bottom's derivative → partial fractions.
(e) Rewrite as x1/2 → power rule, the cheapest tool of all.
(a) parts (b) u-sub (c) arctan form (d) partial fractions (e) power rule
3.power rule · rewrite
Evaluate ∫14 (1/√x) dx.

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 21 (all powers): roots and reciprocals are powers in disguise. Rewrite first, then the ordinary power rule (raise the exponent by one, divide by the new exponent) applies.
Rewrite: 1/√x = x−1/2.
Antiderivative: x1/2/(1/2) = 2√x. (Dividing by ½ means multiplying by 2.) Check: d/dx[2x1/2] = x−1/2 ✓
Evaluate: [2√x]14 = 2·2 − 2·1 = 4 − 2 = 2.
2
4.basic trig
Evaluate ∫0π/4 sec²x dx.

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 24 (the trig library): because (tan x)′ = sec²x, reading that backwards gives ∫sec²x dx = tan x + C. Recall tan means sin/cos, and tan(π/4) = 1 because sine and cosine are equal at 45°.
∫sec²x dx = tan x, so the definite integral is [tan x]0π/4.
= tan(π/4) − tan(0) = 1 − 0.
1
5.FTC fluency
Evaluate ∫13 (3x² − 4x + 1) dx.

Worked solution

Tool — Lessons 7–8 (the Fundamental Theorem): antidifferentiate term by term, plug in the top limit, plug in the bottom limit, subtract. The most-used move in all of calculus.
F(x) = x³ − 2x² + x. (Check: F′ = 3x² − 4x + 1 ✓)
F(3) = 27 − 18 + 3 = 12.   F(1) = 1 − 2 + 1 = 0.
Answer = 12 − 0 = 12.
12
6.arctan form
Find ∫ 1/(x² + 9) dx, and check your answer by differentiating it.

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 25 (inverse trig): arctan x answers "the tangent of what angle is x?", and remarkably its derivative is the plain fraction 1/(1+x²). The general shape is ∫1/(a²+x²) dx = (1/a)arctan(x/a) + C.
Here a² = 9, so a = 3: the answer should be (1/3)arctan(x/3) + C.
Check: d/dx[(1/3)arctan(x/3)] = (1/3) · 1/(1 + x²/9) · (1/3) — the last factor is the chain rule from the inside x/3.
= (1/9) · 1/((9 + x²)/9) = (1/9) · 9/(9 + x²) = 1/(9 + x²) ✓
(1/3)arctan(x/3) + C
7.f′/f pattern
Find ∫ 2x/(x² + 9) dx. Compare it with problem 6 — same bottom, wildly different answer. Why?

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 26 (the f′/f pattern): whenever the numerator is exactly the derivative of the denominator, the integral is ln of the denominator. It comes from u-substitution: with u = bottom, du = top·dx, so the integral becomes ∫du/u = ln|u|.
Bottom: x² + 9. Its derivative: 2x — which is exactly the top.
So ∫2x/(x²+9) dx = ln(x² + 9) + C. (No absolute value needed: x² + 9 is always positive.)
Check: d/dx[ln(x²+9)] = 2x/(x²+9) ✓
Why so different from #6? The presence of that 2x on top is the whole story. With it, the integral is a u-substitution and gives a logarithm; without it, no substitution is possible and the answer is an angle (arctan). Same denominator, different numerator, different universe.
ln(x² + 9) + C
8.u-substitution
Evaluate ∫0π/4 tan x · sec²x dx.

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 26 (u-sub with changed limits): spot an inside function whose derivative is also present; then convert the limits to u-values so you never have to substitute back.
Let u = tan x. Then du = sec²x dx — and sec²x dx is precisely the rest of the integrand.
Change the limits: when x = 0, u = tan 0 = 0; when x = π/4, u = tan(π/4) = 1.
The integral becomes ∫01 u du = [u²/2]01 = 1/2 − 0.
1/2
9.integration by parts
Find ∫ x e3x dx.

Worked solution

Tool — Lessons 30–31 (integration by parts): ∫u dv = uv − ∫v du trades a hard integral for an easier one. Pick u = the piece that gets simpler when differentiated (LIATE puts the polynomial above the exponential), and dv = the piece you can integrate.
u = x, dv = e3xdx → du = dx, and v = e3x/3 (the ÷3 is Lesson 17's linear-inside rule).
Assemble: uv − ∫v du = x·e3x/3 − ∫ e3x/3 dx = x e3x/3 − e3x/9 + C.
Check (product rule): d/dx[x e3x/3] = e3x/3 + x e3x, and d/dx[−e3x/9] = −e3x/3. Adding: e3x/3 + x e3x − e3x/3 = x e3x ✓
x e3x/3 − e3x/9 + C
10.partial fractions
Find ∫ 1/((x − 1)(x + 3)) dx.

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 32 (partial fractions): adding fractions combines them; this runs the process backwards, splitting one awkward fraction into simple ones that each integrate to a logarithm.
Set up: 1/((x−1)(x+3)) = A/(x−1) + B/(x+3). Multiply through by the bottom: 1 = A(x+3) + B(x−1).
Plug in the magic values. x = 1: 1 = A(4) → A = 1/4.   x = −3: 1 = B(−4) → B = −1/4.
Verify the split: (1/4)/(x−1) − (1/4)/(x+3) = [(x+3) − (x−1)]/(4(x−1)(x+3)) = 4/(4(x−1)(x+3)) = 1/((x−1)(x+3)) ✓
Integrate each piece: (1/4)ln|x−1| − (1/4)ln|x+3| + C, which can be combined as (1/4)ln|(x−1)/(x+3)| + C.
(1/4)ln|x − 1| − (1/4)ln|x + 3| + C
11.improper integral
Evaluate ∫1∞ 1/x4 dx, or show it diverges.

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 33 (improper integrals): ∞ isn't a number you can plug in. Replace it with a letter b, integrate normally, then ask where the answer heads as b grows without bound. The p-rule: ∫₁∞1/xp converges exactly when p > 1 — and here p = 4.
Rewrite 1/x⁴ = x−4; antiderivative x−3/(−3) = −1/(3x³).
∫1b = [−1/(3x³)]1b = −1/(3b³) + 1/3.
As b → ∞, the term 1/(3b³) shrinks to 0, leaving 1/3. The region is infinitely long but has finite area.
1/3 (convergent)
12.FTC Part 2
Let g(x) = ∫0x² cos t dt. Find g′(x).

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 27 (FTC Part 2): d/dx ∫ax f(t) dt = f(x) — the rate at which a total grows is the height currently being added. When the upper limit is a function of x, the chain rule tacks on that function's derivative.
The upper limit is x², not x. So: evaluate the integrand at x², then multiply by the derivative of x².
g′(x) = cos(x²) · 2x.
Common error: writing just cos(x²) and forgetting the ·2x.
g′(x) = 2x cos(x²)
13.net vs total
A particle moves along a line with velocity v(t) = 3t² − 12t + 9 m/s for 0 ≤ t ≤ 4. Find (a) the net displacement and (b) the total distance travelled.

Worked solution

Tool — Lessons 9 & 11 (signed area, net vs total): a plain integral of velocity gives NET displacement, letting backward motion cancel forward motion. For TOTAL distance you must find where the velocity changes sign, integrate each stretch separately, and add the sizes.
Find the sign changes: 3t² − 12t + 9 = 3(t² − 4t + 3) = 3(t−1)(t−3), so v = 0 at t = 1 and t = 3. Testing: v is positive on [0,1), negative on (1,3), positive on (3,4].
Antiderivative: F(t) = t³ − 6t² + 9t. Values: F(0) = 0, F(1) = 1 − 6 + 9 = 4, F(3) = 27 − 54 + 27 = 0, F(4) = 64 − 96 + 36 = 4.
(a) Net = F(4) − F(0) = 4 − 0 = 4 metres.
(b) Pieces: [0,1] → 4 − 0 = +4; [1,3] → 0 − 4 = −4; [3,4] → 4 − 0 = +4. Total distance = |+4| + |−4| + |+4| = 12 metres.
(a) net displacement 4 m   (b) total distance 12 m
14.area between curves
Find the area of the region enclosed between y = x and y = x².

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 10 (area between curves): the strip height at each x is (top − bottom). When the limits aren't given, find them by setting the curves equal — those crossings are the natural edges of the enclosed region.
Intersections: x = x² → x² − x = 0 → x(x−1) = 0 → x = 0 and x = 1.
Who's on top? Test x = ½: the line gives 0.5, the parabola gives 0.25. The line y = x is above.
A = ∫01 (x − x²) dx = [x²/2 − x³/3]01 = 1/2 − 1/3 = 3/6 − 2/6 = 1/6.
1/6
15.volume · disks
The region under y = 1/x from x = 1 to x = 2 is revolved about the x-axis. Find the volume of the resulting solid.

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 34 (disks): revolving a region about the x-axis makes every cross-section a circle whose radius is the function's height, so V = π∫[f(x)]² dx. Square the radius first, then integrate.
Radius = 1/x, so [f(x)]² = 1/x².
V = π∫12 x−2 dx = π[−1/x]12.
= π(−1/2 − (−1)) = π(1/2).
Note how squaring helped: 1/x is the one function the power rule can't integrate (it needs ln), but 1/x² is perfectly ordinary.
π/2
16.average value
Find the average value of f(x) = 4 − x² on the interval [0, 2], and explain what that number means as a picture.

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 14 (average value): the average of a changing function is (total accumulated) ÷ (length of interval) — exactly like average speed being distance ÷ time. Formula: (1/(b−a))∫ab f.
Total: ∫02(4 − x²) dx = [4x − x³/3]02 = 8 − 8/3 = 24/3 − 8/3 = 16/3.
Length of interval: 2 − 0 = 2. Average = (16/3) ÷ 2 = 8/3 ≈ 2.67.
The picture: a rectangle of width 2 and height 8/3 has the exact same area (16/3) as the region under the curve — the average is the level the "water" would settle to if the curvy top were flattened.
Sanity check: f runs from 4 down to 0, so an average of 2.67 sits sensibly in between (a bit above the midpoint 2, since the curve stays high for a while before dropping).
8/3 ≈ 2.67
17.arc length
Find the exact length of the curve y = (2/3)x3/2 from x = 0 to x = 3.

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 36 (arc length): zoom into a tiny piece of curve and it's nearly a straight hypotenuse with legs dx and dy; Pythagoras then adds up to L = ∫√(1 + (f′)²) dx. Most such integrals are impossible by hand, so problems are designed so the inside of the root collapses — this is the famous clean family.
f′(x) = (2/3)·(3/2)x1/2 = x1/2 = √x.
Then (f′)² = x, so 1 + (f′)² = 1 + x — no radical mess at all.
L = ∫03 √(1 + x) dx. The inside is linear, so integrate as a power and divide by the slope 1: [(2/3)(1+x)3/2]03.
= (2/3)(43/2) − (2/3)(13/2) = (2/3)(8) − (2/3)(1) = (2/3)(7) = 14/3.
14/3 ≈ 4.67
18.differential equation
Solve dy/dx = 2xy with the initial condition y(0) = 3, then verify your solution satisfies the equation.

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 37 (separation of variables): a differential equation describes a function by its rate. "Separate" means sorting all the y's (with dy) to one side and all the x's (with dx) to the other, then integrating both sides. One initial condition pins down the constant.
Separate: dy/y = 2x dx.
Integrate both sides: ln|y| = x² + C.
Undo the log: |y| = ex²+C = eC·ex², so y = Aex² where A = ±eC is just a new constant.
Use y(0) = 3: 3 = A·e0 = A, so A = 3 → y = 3ex².
Verify: y′ = 3ex²·2x (chain rule) = 2x·(3ex²) = 2x·y ✓ and y(0) = 3 ✓
y = 3ex²
19.logistic · reading
A fish population satisfies dP/dt = 0.5P(1 − P/800), with P(0) = 100. Without solving the equation, answer: (a) the carrying capacity, (b) what P approaches as t → ∞, (c) the population when the growth rate is fastest, and (d) whether the population is growing at P = 100.

Worked solution

Tool — Lesson 38 (logistic growth): real populations can't grow forever, so dP/dt = kP(1 − P/L) throttles itself: when P is small the bracket is nearly 1 (almost pure exponential growth); when P nears L the bracket nears 0 and growth stalls. L is the carrying capacity. The whole point is that these questions need no solving at all.
(a) Comparing to the standard form, L = 800 fish (and k = 0.5).
(b) Logistic solutions rise toward the ceiling: P → 800 as t → ∞.
(c) The growth rate 0.5P(1 − P/800) is a downward parabola in P with zeros at P = 0 and P = 800, so it peaks halfway between: P = 400 = L/2.
(d) At P = 100: dP/dt = 0.5(100)(1 − 100/800) = 50 · (7/8) = 43.75 fish per unit time — positive, so yes, it is growing (and fairly briskly, since 100 is far below the ceiling).
(a) 800 (b) → 800 (c) P = 400 (d) yes, dP/dt = 43.75 > 0
20.challenge · grand finale
A drone's flight. A drone rises from a launch pad with upward velocity v(t) = 6t − t² metres per second for 0 ≤ t ≤ 6, starting at height 2 m. (a) When is the drone rising fastest, and how fast is it going then? (b) How high does it climb in total over the 6 seconds? (c) What is its height at t = 6? (d) What is its average velocity over the 6 seconds? (e) A second drone travels a curved path given parametrically by x = t², y = (2/3)t³. How far does it travel from t = 0 to t = √3?

Worked solution

Tools — Lessons 11, 12, 14, 19 and 39 together: this is what a BC free-response feels like — several lessons stacked into one story. "Fastest" is a derivative question (a maximum of v happens where v′ = 0); "how much climb" is an integral of the rate; "height" needs the starting value added; "average" divides the total by the time; and part (e) switches to parametric motion, where distance is the integral of speed.
(a) The drone's speed is v(t) itself; it's largest where v′(t) = 0. v′ = 6 − 2t = 0 → t = 3 s. Then v(3) = 18 − 9 = 9 m/s. (It's a maximum because v′ goes from positive to negative there.)
(b) Note v = t(6 − t) ≥ 0 on all of [0,6], so the drone never descends and the climb equals the integral: ∫06(6t − t²) dt = [3t² − t³/3]06 = (108 − 72) − 0 = 36 metres.
(c) Height = starting height + change = 2 + 36 = 38 metres.
(d) Average velocity = total displacement ÷ elapsed time = 36/6 = 6 m/s. (Sanity check: that's comfortably below the peak 9 m/s ✓)
(e) Parametric speed: dx/dt = 2t and dy/dt = 2t², so speed = √(4t² + 4t⁴) = √(4t²(1 + t²)) = 2t√(1 + t²) for t ≥ 0.
Distance = ∫0√3 2t√(1+t²) dt. Substitute u = 1 + t², du = 2t dt; limits become u = 1 (at t = 0) and u = 4 (at t = √3).
= ∫14 √u du = [(2/3)u3/2]14 = (2/3)(8) − (2/3)(1) = 14/3 metres ≈ 4.67 m.
(a) t = 3 s at 9 m/s   (b) 36 m   (c) 38 m   (d) 6 m/s   (e) 14/3 m
