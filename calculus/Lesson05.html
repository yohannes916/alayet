Lesson 5 — The Power Rule for Antiderivatives

Raise the Power, Divide by It · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 5 of 20

📚 All lessons← Prev: Lesson 4Next: Lesson 6 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: turn last lesson's guess-and-check into a machine. One rule — raise the power by one, divide by the new power — lets you write the antiderivative of ANY power of x, and with two helper rules you can handle any polynomial. Plus the habit that makes you unstoppable: always check by differentiating.

1 The pattern, made official

  • In Lesson 4 you found antiderivatives by asking "whose derivative is this?" and hunting. There's a pattern hiding in those hunts.
  • Differentiating xn does two things: the exponent hops down front as a multiplier, and the power drops by one. To undo that, do the opposite moves in the opposite order: RAISE the power by one, then DIVIDE by the new power.
  • The official rule: the antiderivative of xn is xn+1/(n+1) + C.
  • Why does dividing work? Watch the check for x3. The rule says: raise the power (x4), divide by the new power → x4/4 + C. Now differentiate it: the derivative of x4 is 4x3, so the derivative of x4/4 is 4x3/4 = x3 ✓. The ÷4 is there precisely to cancel the 4 that hops down when you differentiate.

2 Special cases — 1 and plain constants

  • Antiderivative of 1: remember that 1 = x0 (anything to the power 0 is 1). Apply the rule: raise the power to 1, divide by 1 → x1/1 = x. So the antiderivative of 1 is x + C. (Sanity check: the derivative of x is 1 ✓.)
  • Antiderivative of any constant k: it's kx + C — a straight line with slope k. Check: the derivative of kx is k ✓. Example: antiderivative of 9 is 9x + C.

3 The constant-multiple rule — constants ride along

  • A number multiplied in front just rides along: take the antiderivative of the power part, then keep the multiplier.
  • Example: antiderivative of 6x2. The x2 part becomes x3/3, and the 6 rides along: 6 · x3/3 = 2x3. Answer: 2x3 + C.
  • Check: derivative of 2x3 = 2 · 3x2 = 6x2 ✓. Often the riding constant and the dividing number simplify into something clean, like the 6/3 = 2 here.

4 The sum rule — one term at a time

  • To find the antiderivative of a sum (or difference), just do each term separately and keep the + and − signs between them. One single + C at the end covers everything.
  • Why it works: derivatives also work term by term, so undoing them can too.
  • This means you can now handle any polynomial: constants, powers, multipliers, sums — every tool is on the table.

5 Worked examples

Example 1 — antiderivative of 4x3 + 2x: term 1: 4 · x4/4 = x4. Term 2: 2 · x2/2 = x2. Answer: x4 + x2 + C. Check: derivative = 4x3 + 2x ✓.
Example 2 — antiderivative of x2 + x + 1: term by term: x3/3, then x2/2, then x. Answer: x3/3 + x2/2 + x + C. Check: 3x2/3 + 2x/2 + 1 = x2 + x + 1 ✓. Fractions like these are normal — leave them as fractions; don't turn them into messy decimals.
Example 3 — antiderivative of 9x2 − 4x + 5 (keeping fractions tidy): term 1: 9 · x3/3 = 3x3. Term 2: −4 · x2/2 = −2x2. Term 3: 5x. Answer: 3x3 − 2x2 + 5x + C. Check: 9x2 − 4x + 5 ✓. Notice how the fractions simplified away — that happens whenever the coefficient is a multiple of the new power.

6 The habit — ALWAYS check by differentiating

  • Every antiderivative you ever write can be verified in five seconds: differentiate it and compare with what you started with. If they match, you're right. Period.
  • The two most common slips — forgetting to divide by the new power, and dropping the + C — are BOTH caught instantly by this check (the first gives the wrong derivative; the second you catch by asking "did I write the family?").
  • From this lesson to the end of the course: no answer is finished until it's checked. The solutions below model this every time.

Worked solution

Raise the power: x1 becomes x2. Divide by the new power 2: x2/2.
Answer: x2/2 + C.

Find the antiderivative of x2.

Find the antiderivative of x5.

Worked solution

Raise: x5 → x6. Divide by the new power: x6/6.

4.constant
Find the antiderivative of 9.

Worked solution

Background: a lone constant k is a rate that never changes, and the function that grows at a steady rate k is the line kx. (You can also see it through the rule: 9 = 9x0, raise the power to 1, divide by 1 → 9x.)
Antiderivative of 9 is 9x + C.
Check: derivative of 9x + C = 9 ✓.

5.constant multiple
Find the antiderivative of 8x3.

Worked solution

Background: a number multiplied in front "rides along": handle the power of x with the rule, then multiply by the riding number. Often it simplifies against the division.
The x3 part: raise to x4, divide by 4 → x4/4.
The 8 rides along: 8 · x4/4 = 2x4.
Check: derivative of 2x4 = 2 · 4x3 = 8x3 ✓.
2x4 + C
6.constant multiple
Find the antiderivative of 10x4.

Worked solution

Background: same riding-along idea. The x4 part gets the power rule (new power 5), and the coefficient 10 multiplies the result. 10/5 simplifies to 2.
x4 → x5/5. Then 10 · x5/5 = 2x5.
Check: derivative of 2x5 = 2 · 5x4 = 10x4 ✓.
2x5 + C
7.sum of powers
Find the antiderivative of x3 + x.

Worked solution

Background: the sum rule: antidifferentiate each term on its own and keep the plus sign between the results. One + C at the end covers the whole answer — you don't write a separate C for each term.
Term 1: x3 → x4/4.
Term 2: x → x2/2.
Combine: x4/4 + x2/2 + C. Check: 4x3/4 + 2x/2 = x3 + x ✓.
x4/4 + x2/2 + C
8.polynomial
Find the antiderivative of 6x2 + 4x.

Worked solution

Background: this combines the sum rule (term by term) with the constant-multiple rule (coefficients ride along). Watch the coefficients simplify against the new powers: 6/3 and 4/2 both come out whole.
Term 1: 6x2 → 6 · x3/3 = 2x3.
Term 2: 4x → 4 · x2/2 = 2x2.
Combine: 2x3 + 2x2 + C. Check: 6x2 + 4x ✓.
2x3 + 2x2 + C
9.polynomial with constant
Find the antiderivative of x2 + 5.

Worked solution

Background: mixed terms are fine — each term gets whichever tool fits. Powers of x get the power rule; a lone constant k becomes kx. And it's completely normal for one term to keep a fraction while another doesn't.
Term 1: x2 → x3/3.
Term 2: 5 → 5x.
Combine: x3/3 + 5x + C. Check: 3x2/3 + 5 = x2 + 5 ✓.
x3/3 + 5x + C
10.verify by differentiating
Check whether x4/4 + x2/2 is an antiderivative of x3 + x. Show the differentiation.

Worked solution

Background: checking is the reverse trip: differentiate the candidate and see if you land on the original. When differentiating a fraction like x4/4, the 4 that hops down cancels the 4 underneath — that's the whole design of the power rule.
Derivative of x4/4 = 4x3/4 = x3.
Derivative of x2/2 = 2x/2 = x.
Total: x3 + x — an exact match with the target.
Yes ✓ — its derivative is exactly x3 + x
11.polynomial · minus sign
Find the antiderivative of 5x4 − 3x2.

Worked solution

Background: subtraction works exactly like addition — do each term separately and keep the minus sign between the results. A negative coefficient just rides along like any other constant.
Term 1: 5x4 → 5 · x5/5 = x5.
Term 2: 3x2 → 3 · x3/3 = x3, keeping the minus: −x3.
Combine: x5 − x3 + C. Check: 5x4 − 3x2 ✓.
x5 − x3 + C
12.constant multiple
Find the antiderivative of 7x6.

Worked solution

Background: when the coefficient equals the NEW power (here both are 7), the division cancels it completely — a satisfying special case worth recognizing on sight.
x6 → x7/7, and the 7 rides along: 7 · x7/7 = x7.
Check: derivative of x7 = 7x6 ✓.
x7 + C
13.spot the error
A student claims the antiderivative of x3 is x4/3 + C. Use the differentiation check to decide if they're right, and fix the answer if needed.

Worked solution

Background: the classic slip: dividing by the OLD power instead of the NEW one. The rule says divide by the new power (n + 1), because that's the number that will hop down when the answer is differentiated. The check exposes the slip immediately.
Check the claim: derivative of x4/3 = 4x3/3. That is NOT x3 (it's 4/3 as big). Wrong.
Fix: divide by the new power 4, not the old power 3 → x4/4 + C.
Check the fix: derivative of x4/4 = 4x3/4 = x3 ✓.
The student is wrong (derivative comes out 4x3/3); correct answer: x4/4 + C
14.full polynomial
Find the antiderivative of 4x3 + 6x + 1.

Worked solution

Background: a three-term polynomial uses everything at once: power rule per term, coefficients riding along, constant term becoming (constant)·x, one + C at the end.
Term 1: 4x3 → 4 · x4/4 = x4.
Term 2: 6x → 6 · x2/2 = 3x2.
Term 3: 1 → x.
Combine: x4 + 3x2 + x + C. Check: 4x3 + 6x + 1 ✓.
x4 + 3x2 + x + C
15.challenge polynomial
Find the antiderivative of 12x5 − 6x2 + 7.

Worked solution

Background: nothing new here — just more terms, bigger powers, and a minus sign. Work left to right, one term at a time, and finish with the check. Speed comes from calm repetition, not from skipping steps.
Term 1: 12x5 → 12 · x6/6 = 2x6.
Term 2: −6x2 → −6 · x3/3 = −2x3.
Term 3: 7 → 7x.
Combine: 2x6 − 2x3 + 7x + C. Check: 12x5 − 6x2 + 7 ✓.
2x6 − 2x3 + 7x + C
16.find the specific function
Find f(x) given f′(x) = 3x2 and f(1) = 4.

Worked solution

Background: from Lesson 4: the derivative pins f down only to a family (+ C); one known point picks out the exact member. Recipe: antidifferentiate, plug in the point, solve for C.
Antiderivative: 3x2 → 3 · x3/3 = x3, so f(x) = x3 + C.
Point: f(1) = 1 + C = 4 → C = 3.
f(x) = x3 + 3. Check: derivative = 3x2 ✓; f(1) = 1 + 3 = 4 ✓.
f(x) = x3 + 3
17.find the specific function
Find f(x) given f′(x) = x and f(2) = 5.

Worked solution

Background: same recipe, but now the antiderivative keeps a fraction — that's fine. When plugging the point into x2/2, square first, then halve: 22/2 = 4/2 = 2.
Antiderivative: f(x) = x2/2 + C.
Point: f(2) = 4/2 + C = 2 + C = 5 → C = 3.
f(x) = x2/2 + 3. Check: derivative = x ✓; f(2) = 2 + 3 = 5 ✓.
f(x) = x2/2 + 3
18.challenge · multiply first
Find the antiderivative of (x + 2)(x + 3). Hint: multiply it out first!

Worked solution

Background: our rules handle powers, multiples, and sums — NOT products of two x-expressions. So first rewrite the product as a plain polynomial using FOIL (First, Outer, Inner, Last): (x + 2)(x + 3) = x·x + x·3 + 2·x + 2·3.
Multiply out: x2 + 3x + 2x + 6 = x2 + 5x + 6.
Now antidifferentiate term by term: x2 → x3/3; 5x → 5x2/2; 6 → 6x.
Combine: x3/3 + 5x2/2 + 6x + C.
Check: derivative = x2 + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3) ✓.
x3/3 + 5x2/2 + 6x + C
19.challenge · function + point
Find f(x) given f′(x) = 4x3 − 2x and f(1) = 6.

Worked solution

Background: full recipe with a twist at the end: sometimes the x-terms cancel each other when you plug in the point, and C carries the whole value. Don't panic when that happens — just read off C.
Antiderivative: 4x3 → x4; −2x → −x2. So f(x) = x4 − x2 + C.
Point: f(1) = 1 − 1 + C = 0 + C = 6 → C = 6.
f(x) = x4 − x2 + 6. Check: derivative = 4x3 − 2x ✓; f(1) = 1 − 1 + 6 = 6 ✓.
f(x) = x4 − x2 + 6
20.challenge finale
Find f(x) given f′(x) = (x + 1)(x − 1) and f(3) = 7. (Multiply out first, then use the full recipe.)

Worked solution

Background: the grand combo: expand a product, antidifferentiate term by term, pin down C from a point, and verify. Handy expansion: (x + 1)(x − 1) = x2 − x + x − 1 = x2 − 1 (the middle terms cancel — this is the "difference of squares" pattern).
Multiply out: f′(x) = x2 − 1.
Antiderivative: x2 → x3/3; −1 → −x. So f(x) = x3/3 − x + C.
Point: f(3) = 27/3 − 3 + C = 9 − 3 + C = 6 + C = 7 → C = 1.
f(x) = x3/3 − x + 1. Check: derivative = 3x2/3 − 1 = x2 − 1 = (x + 1)(x − 1) ✓; f(3) = 9 − 3 + 1 = 7 ✓.
f(x) = x3/3 − x + 1
