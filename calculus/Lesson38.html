Lesson 38 — Differential Equations II: Growth, Decay & Logistic

Doubling, Half-Life, Cooling Coffee & the S-Curve · Intuitive Calculus for a Curious Student · Lesson 38 of 40

📚 All lessons← Prev: Lesson 37Next: Lesson 39 →
🎯 Goal of this lesson: put Lesson 37's star equation dy/dt = ky to work in the real world — populations that double, medicine that halves, coffee that cools — and meet the BC classic, logistic growth, where populations hit a ceiling. Best part: most AP logistic questions can be answered without solving anything.

1 The exponential family in the wild

  • Lesson 37 proved: dy/dt = ky ("rate proportional to size") has solution y = Ce^(kt). Now the upgrade: set t = 0 → y(0) = C·e⁰ = C. So the constant IS the starting amount. Write it with its real name:
  • y = y₀·e^(kt)   where y₀ = the amount you start with.
  • No mystery constant to hunt for — read the starting value straight off the problem. Then just two behaviors:
    • k > 0: growth. Bacteria, invested money, virus spread — the more you have, the faster it grows.
    • k < 0: decay. Radioactive atoms, medicine in your blood, foam on hot chocolate — the more there is, the faster it disappears.

2 Doubling time & half-life, from scratch

  • Exponential things have a magic property: they multiply by the SAME factor over every equal stretch of time. Growing things double every fixed time (the doubling time); decaying things halve every fixed time (the half-life).
  • That gives a wonderfully friendly formula. If something doubles every d hours: y = y₀·2^(t/d). Read the exponent as "how many doublings have happened so far." After d hours, t/d = 1 → one doubling. After 2d, two doublings. Halving: y = y₀·(1/2)^(t/h).
Worked example: a bacteria population starts at 500 and doubles every 3 hours.
Model: P = 500·2^(t/3) (t in hours).
After 9 hours: t/3 = 3 doublings → P(9) = 500·2³ = 500·8 = 4000.
Sanity check: 500 → 1000 (3 h) → 2000 (6 h) → 4000 (9 h) ✓.
  • Base-2 and base-e are the same function in different clothes: since 2 = e^(ln 2), we get 2^(t/3) = e^((ln 2/3)·t), so P = 500·2^(t/3) = 500·e^((ln 2/3)t) — an exponential with k = ln 2/3 ≈ 0.231 (using ln 2 ≈ 0.693). In general: k = ln 2 ÷ doubling time, and for decay k = −ln 2 ÷ half-life.
  • Use whichever outfit fits the question: base-2 for "how many doublings," base-e for matching a DE.

3 Solving for TIME — ln opens the exponent

  • "How much after 9 hours?" is plug-in. The reverse — "WHEN does it reach 16,000?" — needs to free a variable trapped in an exponent. That's exactly what logarithms do.
Worked example: same colony, P = 500·2^(t/3). When does P hit 16,000?
500·2^(t/3) = 16000 → divide by 500: 2^(t/3) = 32.
Recognize the power: 32 = 2⁵ → t/3 = 5 → t = 15 hours.
Check: 15 h = 5 doublings; 500·32 = 16,000 ✓.
The ln route (for when the power isn't recognizable): take ln of both sides of 2^(t/3) = 32 → (t/3)·ln 2 = ln 32 → t = 3·ln 32/ln 2 = 3·5 = 15 ✓ (since ln 32 = ln 2⁵ = 5 ln 2).
  • Habit to build: isolate the exponential first, THEN take the log. (Taking ln of "500·2^(t/3)" before dividing just makes a mess.)

4 Newton's Law of Cooling — decay of the GAP

  • Hot coffee doesn't cool at a constant rate — it cools fast when it's much hotter than the room, slowly when it's nearly room temperature. Newton's Law says: "cools at a rate proportional to how much hotter it is than the room":
  • dT/dt = −k(T − T_room)   (k > 0; the minus sign says hotter-than-room → cooling).
  • Clever substitution: let u = T − T_room, the GAP above room temperature. Since T_room is constant, du/dt = dT/dt = −ku — pure exponential decay! So the gap decays: u = u₀e^(−kt), which unwraps to:
  • T = T_room + (T₀ − T_room)e^(−kt)   — room temperature plus a shrinking gap.
  • Everything from section 2 applies to the gap: the gap has a half-life; the temperature itself does NOT halve. (Classic trap — exercise 18 springs it.)
Worked example: coffee at 90°, room at 20°. After 10 minutes it's 55°. Predict the temperature after 20 minutes.
Starting gap: 90 − 20 = 70°. After 10 min the gap is 55 − 20 = 35° — exactly half! So e^(−10k) = 1/2: the gap's half-life is 10 minutes.
After 20 min (two half-lives): gap = 70/4 = 17.5° → T = 20 + 17.5 = 37.5°.
Check with the formula: T = 20 + 70·e^(−20k) = 20 + 70·(e^(−10k))² = 20 + 70·(1/2)² = 20 + 17.5 ✓.

5 Logistic growth — the S-curve with a ceiling (BC classic)

  • Real populations can't double forever — food, space, and patience run out. The fix: multiply the exponential DE by a braking factor that shuts growth down near a ceiling L, the carrying capacity:
  • dP/dt = kP(1 − P/L)
  • Read the equation like a story:
    • P tiny (way below L): the brake (1 − P/L) ≈ 1 → dP/dt ≈ kP → almost pure exponential growth.
    • P near L: the brake ≈ 0 → growth grinds to a halt. The population levels off at the ceiling.
    • P > L (overcrowded): the brake goes negative → population shrinks back down toward L.
  • Where is growth FASTEST? Expand: kP(1 − P/L) = kP − kP²/L — as a function of P this is a downward-opening parabola with roots at P = 0 and P = L. A parabola peaks midway between its roots: at P = L/2. Growth is fastest at HALF the carrying capacity — that's the steepest point of the S-curve.
the logistic S-curve P L ─┤ · · · · · · · · · · · · ───────── ← ceiling: P → L, never crosses │ ╱ L/2┤· · · · ·╳╱ ← steepest here: growth fastest at P = L/2 │ ╱ │ __╱ ← starts almost exponential └─────────────────────────→ t
  • The solution formula (learn to recognize it; deriving it would use partial fractions — a Lesson 32 wink, but the AP never asks for the derivation): P = L/(1 + Ae^(−kt)). As t → ∞, e^(−kt) → 0, so P → L no matter where it started. At t = 0: P(0) = L/(1 + A), so A is set by the starting population.

6 AP question patterns — answers WITHOUT solving

  • Logistic questions look scary but are usually reading questions. Given dP/dt = kP(1 − P/L):
    • "What is lim P as t → ∞?" → L, the carrying capacity (read it from the DE: growth stops there). Just check whether the start is below L (grows up to L) or above (falls to L).
    • "When is growth fastest?" / "Find P when dP/dt is greatest" → P = L/2, always.
    • "What is dP/dt when P = (number)?" → plug into the DE. Arithmetic, not calculus.
  • Example, in one breath: dP/dt = 0.4P(1 − P/1200) → carrying capacity 1200, limit 1200, growth fastest at P = 600, and at P = 300 the growth rate is 0.4·300·(3/4) = 90. Four answers, zero integrals.
✏️ Exercises — 20 problems (write your answer on paper, then reveal)
1.words → DE
A population of fish, P, grows at a rate proportional to its size. Write the differential equation, and state what its solution family looks like.

Worked solution

Background: "rate" = dP/dt; "proportional to its size" = a constant times P. Translating sentences into DEs is the first line of nearly every AP growth problem — and Lesson 37 already solved this equation once and for all.
"Rate of growth" → dP/dt. "Proportional to its size" → = kP for some constant k > 0.
So dP/dt = kP, whose solutions are P = P₀e^(kt) (exponential growth from the starting amount P₀).
dP/dt = kP (k > 0) — solutions P = P₀e^(kt), exponential growth
2.words → DE
A mug of tea at temperature T sits in a 20° room and cools at a rate proportional to how much hotter it is than the room. Write the differential equation (careful with the sign!).

Worked solution

Background: Newton's Law of Cooling. The "how much hotter than the room" quantity is the gap T − 20. When the tea IS hotter (gap positive), the temperature must be FALLING — so the rate needs a minus sign.
Gap above room temperature: T − 20.
"Cools at a rate proportional to the gap": dT/dt = −k(T − 20), with k > 0.
Sign check: if T = 90, then dT/dt = −70k < 0 → cooling ✓. If T somehow were 10 (colder than the room), dT/dt = +10k > 0 → warming up toward the room ✓. The equation is smarter than it looks.
dT/dt = −k(T − 20), k > 0
3.doubling
A bacteria colony starts at 300 cells and doubles every 4 hours. Write a formula for P(t) (t in hours) and find the population after 12 hours.

Worked solution

Background: doubling every d hours → P = P₀·2^(t/d). The exponent t/d counts how many doublings have happened. This friendly base-2 form is exactly equivalent to P₀e^(kt) with k = ln 2/d.
Model: P = 300·2^(t/4).
At t = 12: t/4 = 3 doublings → P(12) = 300·2³ = 300·8 = 2400.
Sanity check: 300 → 600 (4 h) → 1200 (8 h) → 2400 (12 h) ✓.
P = 300·2^(t/4); P(12) = 2400 cells
4.half-life
Caffeine leaves your body with a half-life of about 6 hours. If a large coffee delivers 200 mg of caffeine, how much remains after 18 hours?

Worked solution

Background (biology): your liver removes caffeine at a rate proportional to how much is present — the decay DE dy/dt = −ky in action, which is why caffeine has a half-life. Halving formula: y = y₀·(1/2)^(t/h).
Number of half-lives in 18 hours: 18/6 = 3.
y = 200·(1/2)³ = 200/8 = 25.
Ladder check: 200 → 100 (6 h) → 50 (12 h) → 25 (18 h) ✓.
25 mg of caffeine remains
5.logistic reading
A deer population follows dP/dt = 0.4P(1 − P/1200), starting at P(0) = 100. (a) What is the carrying capacity? (b) What is the limit of P as t → ∞?

Worked solution

Background: in dP/dt = kP(1 − P/L), the number under P in the brake factor IS the carrying capacity L — the ceiling where growth stops. Every logistic population heads to L in the long run (from below or above), no solving required.
(a) Match dP/dt = 0.4P(1 − P/1200) to kP(1 − P/L): k = 0.4 and L = 1200.
(b) Since P starts at 100 (below the ceiling), it grows along the S-curve and levels off at the ceiling: lim P = 1200. (Check the DE: at P = 1200 the brake is 1 − 1 = 0, so growth stops exactly there.)
(a) carrying capacity L = 1200 deer   (b) lim P = 1200
6.C = starting amount
Solve dy/dt = ky with initial condition y(0) = y₀, and explain in one sentence why writing the solution as y = y₀e^(kt) is nicer than y = Ce^(kt).

Worked solution

Background: Lesson 37 derived the general solution y = Ce^(kt) via separation. An initial condition at t = 0 is the friendliest possible: e⁰ = 1, so the constant stops being mysterious.
General solution: y = Ce^(kt).
Plug in t = 0: y₀ = C·e⁰ = C·1 = C. So C = y₀.
Therefore y = y₀e^(kt). Verify: derivative = k·y₀e^(kt) = ky ✓ and y(0) = y₀ ✓.
Why nicer: the constant has a physical MEANING — it's the starting amount, readable straight from the problem instead of solved for.
y = y₀e^(kt); the constant is simply the initial amount
7.solve for time
A colony follows P = 100·2^(t/5) (t in hours). At what time does the population reach 3200?

Worked solution

Background: to free t from an exponent: isolate the exponential FIRST (divide off the 100), then either recognize a power of 2 or take ln of both sides. Recognizing 32 = 2⁵ is faster when it works.
100·2^(t/5) = 3200 → 2^(t/5) = 32.
32 = 2⁵ → t/5 = 5 → t = 25.
(ln route: (t/5)ln 2 = ln 32 → t = 5·ln 32/ln 2 = 5·5 = 25, same answer.)
Check: 25 hours = 5 doublings; 100·32 = 3200 ✓.
t = 25 hours
8.doubling money
A savings account doubles in value every 10 years. You deposit $2000. (a) How much is in the account after 30 years? (b) When will the account hold $32,000?

Worked solution

Background (finance): compound interest grows money at a rate proportional to the balance — the same DE as bacteria, so the same doubling formula: A = 2000·2^(t/10). "Doublings" is the natural unit of thought.
(a) 30 years = 3 doublings: A = 2000·2³ = 2000·8 = $16,000.
(b) 2000·2^(t/10) = 32000 → 2^(t/10) = 16 = 2⁴ → t/10 = 4 → t = 40.
Check: $2000 → 4000 → 8000 → 16,000 (30 yr) → 32,000 (40 yr) ✓.
(a) $16,000   (b) after 40 years
9.base-2 ↔ base-e
(a) Rewrite P = 500·2^(t/3) in the form P = 500·e^(kt) — what is k (exactly, and as a decimal using ln 2 ≈ 0.693)? (b) A different culture follows N = 40·e^(0.173t) with t in hours. Using 0.173 ≈ ln 2/4, what is its doubling time?

Worked solution

Background: 2 = e^(ln 2), so 2^(t/d) = e^((ln 2/d)t). The dictionary between the two outfits: k = ln 2 ÷ (doubling time), and doubling time = ln 2 ÷ k. One function, two costumes.
(a) 2^(t/3) = (e^(ln 2))^(t/3) = e^((ln 2/3)t), so k = ln 2/3 ≈ 0.693/3 = 0.231. Thus P = 500e^(0.231t) (approximately).
(b) doubling time = ln 2/k ≈ ln 2/(ln 2/4) = 4. So N doubles every 4 hours.
Check (b): N = 40e^((ln2/4)t) = 40·2^(t/4) — after 4 hours, 40·2 = 80, doubled ✓.
(a) k = ln 2/3 ≈ 0.231   (b) doubling time = 4 hours
10.half-life · solve for time
A radioactive sample starts at 800 grams and has a half-life of 12 years. After how many years will only 100 grams remain?

Worked solution

Background (physics): each atom decays at random, but with so many atoms the total follows dy/dt = −ky exactly — hence a fixed half-life. Ask: how many halvings turn 800 into 100?
800 → 400 → 200 → 100: that's 3 halvings.
Each halving takes 12 years → t = 3·12 = 36 years.
Formula check: 800·(1/2)^(36/12) = 800·(1/2)³ = 800/8 = 100 ✓.
36 years
11.Newton's cooling
A bowl of soup at 100° sits in a 20° room. After 5 minutes it has cooled to 60°. (a) Find the half-life of the GAP between soup and room. (b) Predict the soup's temperature after 15 minutes.

Worked solution

Background: Newton's Law makes the GAP u = T − T_room decay exponentially: u = u₀e^(−kt). Work entirely in gap-language, then translate back by adding room temperature at the end.
(a) Starting gap: 100 − 20 = 80°. After 5 min: gap = 60 − 20 = 40° — exactly half. So the gap's half-life is 5 minutes.
(b) 15 minutes = 3 gap-halvings: 80 → 40 → 20 → 10°. Temperature: T = 20 + 10 = 30°.
Formula check: T = 20 + 80·(1/2)^(15/5) = 20 + 80/8 = 20 + 10 = 30 ✓.
(a) gap half-life = 5 minutes   (b) T(15) = 30°
12.logistic reading
For dP/dt = 0.4P(1 − P/1200): (a) at what population is growth fastest? (b) What is the growth rate dP/dt when P = 300? (c) And when P = 600?

Worked solution

Background: dP/dt = kP(1 − P/L) is a downward parabola in P with roots 0 and L, so it peaks at P = L/2 — growth is fastest at HALF the carrying capacity. For specific rates, just plug the P-value into the DE: arithmetic, not calculus.
(a) L = 1200 → fastest growth at P = L/2 = 600.
(b) dP/dt = 0.4·300·(1 − 300/1200) = 120·(1 − 1/4) = 120·3/4 = 90 deer per year.
(c) dP/dt = 0.4·600·(1 − 600/1200) = 240·(1/2) = 120 — bigger than at P = 300, as expected: 600 is the peak.
(a) P = 600   (b) 90 per year   (c) 120 per year (the maximum rate)
13.words → logistic DE
A pond can support at most 5000 fish. The fish population grows logistically with constant k = 0.2 (per month). Write the differential equation, and state the population at which the pond's fish population grows fastest.

Worked solution

Background: "can support at most" is the carrying capacity L — it goes under P inside the brake factor (1 − P/L). Then the fastest-growth population is automatic: L/2, no solving.
L = 5000, k = 0.2 → dP/dt = 0.2P(1 − P/5000).
Growth fastest at P = L/2 = 2500 fish.
Check the shape: at P = 2500, dP/dt = 0.2·2500·(1/2) = 250/month; at P = 1000, 0.2·1000·(4/5) = 160; at P = 4000, 0.2·4000·(1/5) = 160 — symmetric around the peak ✓.
dP/dt = 0.2P(1 − P/5000); fastest growth at P = 2500
14.asymptote thinking
Your hot chocolate is at 70° in a 20° room. Your friend says: "Just wait long enough and it will reach exactly 20°." Is your friend right? Use the formula T = 20 + 50e^(−kt) to answer precisely.

Worked solution

Background: e^(−kt) shrinks toward 0 as t grows but NEVER equals 0 — an exponential is always positive. So the gap shrinks forever without vanishing: room temperature is a horizontal asymptote, approached but never touched (in the model).
T = 20 + 50e^(−kt). For T to equal exactly 20, we'd need 50e^(−kt) = 0, i.e. e^(−kt) = 0.
But e^(anything) > 0 always. So T > 20 for every finite t.
However, lim T as t → ∞ = 20 + 50·0 = 20: it gets as CLOSE as you like. After enough half-lives the difference is far too small to taste or even measure.
Not exactly ever — T approaches 20° as an asymptote, getting arbitrarily close but never touching it
15.derive · u-substitution
Starting from dT/dt = −k(T − T_room), use the substitution u = T − T_room to derive the formula T = T_room + (T₀ − T_room)e^(−kt), where T₀ is the starting temperature. Verify your answer satisfies the DE.

Worked solution

Background: the substitution works because T_room is a CONSTANT — differentiating u = T − T_room kills it, leaving du/dt = dT/dt. This turns an unfamiliar DE into the pure-decay equation we already solved in Lesson 37.
Let u = T − T_room (the gap). Then du/dt = dT/dt − 0 = dT/dt.
The DE becomes du/dt = −ku — pure exponential decay! Solution: u = u₀e^(−kt), where u₀ = u(0) = T₀ − T_room.
Translate back: T − T_room = (T₀ − T_room)e^(−kt) → T = T_room + (T₀ − T_room)e^(−kt).
Verify: dT/dt = (T₀ − T_room)·(−k)e^(−kt) = −k·[(T₀ − T_room)e^(−kt)] = −k(T − T_room) ✓. And T(0) = T_room + (T₀ − T_room)·1 = T₀ ✓.
T = T_room + (T₀ − T_room)e^(−kt) — room temperature plus an exponentially decaying gap
16.verify logistic form
Without doing the full algebra, give a conceptual verification that P = L/(1 + Ae^(−kt)) (with A > 0) behaves like a solution of dP/dt = kP(1 − P/L): check (a) the limit as t → ∞, (b) the value at t = 0, (c) that P stays strictly between 0 and L, and (d) the behavior when P is far below L.

Worked solution

Background: "conceptual verification" = check that the formula has every behavior the DE demands. (A full check would substitute and grind the algebra — partial fractions could even DERIVE the formula, Lesson 32 style — but behavior-checking is what the AP expects you to do fluently.)
(a) As t → ∞: e^(−kt) → 0, so P → L/(1 + 0) = L. Matches the DE's ceiling: growth stops at carrying capacity ✓.
(b) At t = 0: P(0) = L/(1 + A). Since A > 0, this is less than L — some starting value below the ceiling, and A can be tuned to hit any such start ✓.
(c) The denominator 1 + Ae^(−kt) is always bigger than 1 (so P < L) and always finite (so P > 0): the curve lives strictly between 0 and L, exactly where the DE keeps dP/dt positive ✓.
(d) Early on (t small, P far below L): the brake (1 − P/L) ≈ 1, and the DE says growth ≈ kP — near-exponential. The formula agrees: for large Ae^(−kt), P ≈ (L/A)e^(kt), an exponential ✓.
All four behaviors match the DE: rises from L/(1+A), nearly exponential at first, stays below L, levels off at L
17.exponential or logistic?
Two colonies are measured daily. Colony A: 100, 200, 400, 800, 1600, ... Colony B: 100, 190, 340, 550, 640, 690, 699, 700, 700, ... Which model fits each colony — exponential or logistic? Estimate any constants you can (doubling time, carrying capacity, fastest-growth population).

Worked solution

Background: fingerprints of each model. Exponential: same multiplying factor every step, forever. Logistic: starts out exponential-ish, then growth slows and the values flatten toward a ceiling. Real data usually shows the ceiling — nothing doubles forever.
Colony A: each day is exactly ×2 the day before — a constant ratio. Exponential, doubling time = 1 day (P = 100·2^t).
Colony B: early growth is quick (100 → 190 is nearly ×2), but the jumps shrink and the values level off at 700. Logistic with carrying capacity L ≈ 700.
Colony B's fastest growth happened near L/2 = 350 — consistent with the data: the biggest one-day jump (340 → 550, a jump of 210) straddles P = 350 ✓.
A: exponential (doubles daily) · B: logistic, L ≈ 700, fastest growth near P = 350
18.spot the error
A student says: "The cocoa is 80° in a 20° room, and the half-life is 10 minutes — so after 10 minutes the cocoa will be at 40°, half of 80." Find the error and compute the correct 10-minute temperature.

Worked solution

Background: in Newton's cooling, the exponentially decaying quantity is the GAP above room temperature, not the temperature itself. Halving the raw temperature would absurdly predict the cocoa keeps halving toward 0° — colder than the room!
The error: applying the half-life to T = 80 instead of to the gap u = T − 20.
Correct: starting gap = 80 − 20 = 60°. After one half-life the gap is 30°.
T = 20 + 30 = 50° — not 40°.
(Sanity: the student's rule, applied twice more, would give 20° then 10° — below room temperature, impossible. The gap version gives 50 → 35 → 27.5°, gliding down toward 20° ✓.)
Half-life applies to the gap (T − 20), not T: after 10 minutes T = 20 + 60/2 = 50°
19.doubling time from k
An investment grows continuously: A = 300·e^(0.10t) (t in years). Derive its doubling time from scratch (set up the equation and solve with ln), then give a decimal using ln 2 ≈ 0.693.

Worked solution

Background: the doubling time d satisfies "after d years the amount is twice the start": A(d) = 2·A(0). The 300 cancels — doubling time never depends on how much you start with, only on k. The result d = ln 2/k is the famous "rule of 69.3" (bankers round it to the Rule of 72).
Set up: 300e^(0.10d) = 2·300 → e^(0.10d) = 2.
Take ln: 0.10d = ln 2 → d = ln 2/0.10.
Decimal: d ≈ 0.693/0.10 = 6.93 years.
Check: e^(0.10·6.93) = e^(0.693) ≈ 2 ✓.
d = ln 2/0.10 ≈ 6.93 years (about 7 years to double)
20.challenge · AP-style mixed
Part I — Fish: a lake's population follows dP/dt = 0.6P(1 − P/2400) with P(0) = 200. (a) Find lim P as t → ∞. (b) Find the population at which dP/dt is greatest, and the value of dP/dt there. (c) Find dP/dt when P = 400. Part II — Pie: a pie at 100° sits in a 20° room and cools to 60° in 15 minutes. (d) Find the half-life of the pie's gap above room temperature. (e) When will the pie reach 25°?

Worked solution

Background: a full AP-style workout mixing both stars of this lesson. Notice how much is answerable by READING: (a) is the carrying capacity, (b) is L/2, (c) is plug-in arithmetic. Only Part II needs the half-life machinery — and even that is counting halvings.
(a) Carrying capacity L = 2400; starting below it (200), the population climbs the S-curve → lim P = 2400.
(b) Fastest at P = L/2 = 1200. There dP/dt = 0.6·1200·(1 − 1200/2400) = 720·(1/2) = 360 fish per unit time.
(c) At P = 400: dP/dt = 0.6·400·(1 − 400/2400) = 240·(5/6) = 200.
(d) Starting gap: 100 − 20 = 80°. After 15 min: gap = 60 − 20 = 40° = half. Gap half-life = 15 minutes.
(e) Reaching 25° means gap = 5°. From 80: 80 → 40 → 20 → 10 → 5, that's 4 halvings → t = 4·15 = 60 minutes. Check: T = 20 + 80·(1/2)^(60/15) = 20 + 80/16 = 20 + 5 = 25 ✓.
(a) 2400   (b) P = 1200, dP/dt = 360   (c) 200   (d) 15 minutes   (e) after 60 minutes
